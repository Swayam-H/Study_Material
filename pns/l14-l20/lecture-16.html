Lecture 16

Accept–reject, and what "Xn​→X" means

Deck B, slides 2 to 11 · 24 min read · 10 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. The lecture has two halves.

  1. Accept–reject (slides 2 to 7). The inverse transform needs F−1 in a usable form. Accept–reject needs only the pmf or pdf, plus some distribution you can already sample from. It finishes the simulation chapter.
  2. Convergence of random variables (slides 7 to 11). The SLLN was used on slide 17 of L15 without saying what "nSn​​→E[X]" means for random quantities. These slides start answering that by recalling convergence of numbers and of functions. L17 and L18 then define five kinds of convergence.

Part A: the slides

Slide 2/30: Recap

On the slide. Inverse transform method. FX​(X) is uniform! Importance sampling.

These are the three tools from L14 and L15. See the previous section for each.

Slide 3/30: The accept–reject method

On the slide. Suppose you want to generate samples from X with pmf p(⋅) using samples from Y with pmf q(⋅). Suppose that q(y)p(y)​≤c for all y. The method: Step 1: generate a sample y∼q(⋅). Step 2: generate u∼U(0,1). Step 3: if u≤cq(y)p(y)​, accept y as a sample from X. Step 4: if not, reject y and go back to Step 1.

The vocabulary. p is the target (what you want). q is the proposal (what you can sample). c is the envelope constant: cq(y) sits on top of p(y) everywhere, like an envelope.

The intuition. A proposal y is kept with probability cq(y)p(y)​, a number in [0,1] because of the bound p≤cq.

  • Where q proposes a value more often than p wants (q(y) large relative to p(y)), the ratio is small and most proposals of y are thrown away.
  • Where p wants a value relatively more, the ratio is near 1 and proposals of y are almost always kept.

The thinning reshapes the proposal's distribution into the target's.

Two facts you can read off immediately.

  • c≥1, always. Sum p(y)≤cq(y) over all y: 1≤c⋅1. Equality c=1 forces p=q (nothing to do).
  • The support condition. We need q(y)>0 wherever p(y)>0; otherwise qp​ is not bounded and no c exists. You cannot reach values your proposal never produces. This is the same condition as in importance sampling.

Slide 4/30: Flowchart, and the question

On the slide. A flowchart (from Ross): Start, generate Y∼g, generate a random number U, is U≤cg(Y)f(Y)​? Yes: set X=Y. No: go back to the start. Why does the method work? What is P(y∣accept)? Is it p(y)?

The flowchart uses f and g for densities, the continuous version. The algorithm is the same with pdfs in place of pmfs, and so is the proof (with sums replaced by integrals; see below).

The question on the slide is the key point. Every accepted value y is the output, so its distribution is the distribution of Y given that it was accepted. The method works exactly when that conditional distribution is p.

Slides 5/30 and 6/30: Proof

On the slide. P(accept∣y)=P(u≤cq(y)p(y)​)=cq(y)p(y)​ since u∼U(0,1). So the joint probability of sampling y∼q(⋅) and accepting it is P(sample y and accept)=q(y)⋅cq(y)p(y)​=cp(y)​. The marginal probability of accepting (the normalizing constant) is P(accept)=∑y​cp(y)​=c1​. Hence

P(y∣accept)=P(accept)P(sample y and accept)​=1/cp(y)/c​=p(y).

Therefore the accepted samples are distributed according to p(⋅).

The proof is Bayes' rule in three steps. Think of one round as a two-stage experiment: first Y is drawn, then a coin with success probability cq(Y)p(Y)​ is tossed.

  1. Likelihood. Given Y=y, the round accepts when U≤cq(y)p(y)​. Since U is uniform and independent of Y, and the threshold is in [0,1], this has probability cq(y)p(y)​ (toolkit fact 6).
  2. Joint. Multiply by the prior q(y): the q(y) cancels, leaving cp(y)​. This cancellation is the whole trick.
  3. Normalise. Summing over y gives P(accept)=c1​∑y​p(y)=c1​, and dividing gives p(y).

What the slide's proof leaves implicit: the repeated rounds. The output is the y from the first accepted round, not from a single round. Rounds are independent and identical, so the first accepted round is distributed like one round conditioned on acceptance. To see it directly, add up over the round k in which the first acceptance happens:

P(X=y)=k≥1∑​first k−1 rejected(1−c1​)k−1​​⋅round k: y and acceptcp(y)​​​=cp(y)​⋅1−(1−1/c)1​=p(y).✓

The continuous version (pdfs f, g). The same computation with CDFs:

P(Y≤x∣accept)=P(accept)P(Y≤x, accept)​=∫−∞∞​g(y)cg(y)f(y)​dy∫−∞x​g(y)cg(y)f(y)​dy​=c1​c1​F(x)​=F(x).

This is last year's HW6 Q18 and probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Exercise 6.

How many rounds? (Not on the slide, but examined: last year's HW7 Q8.) Each round accepts with probability c1​, independently of the other rounds. So the number of rounds N until the first acceptance is Geometric(c1​):

P(accept)=c1​,N∼Geometric(c1​),E[N]=c​

Generating k samples takes a sum of k independent geometrics, so the expected number of rounds is kc. Each round uses two uniforms (one inside the proposal, one for the test), so expect about 2kc uniforms.

Choosing c. Any c≥supy​q(y)p(y)​ gives correct samples, but a bigger c means more rejections. The best choice is

c∗=ymax​q(y)p(y)​​

and a good proposal is one shaped like the target, so that c∗ is close to 1.

A picture that explains everything (continuous case). Draw the curve cg(x) above f(x). A round picks Y∼g and then a height V=U⋅cg(Y), uniform in [0,cg(Y)]. The point (Y,V) is uniform in the region under cg. "Accept if U≤cg(Y)f(Y)​" means "accept if V≤f(Y)", that is, if the point falls under f. Accepted points are uniform under f, so their x-coordinates have density f. The acceptance probability is area under cgarea under f​=c1​.

A practical bonus. The test only uses the ratio cq(y)p(y)​. If you know p only up to a constant, p=p~​/Z with Z unknown, use p~​ and a c~≥maxqp~​​ instead. The Z cancels. That is why accept–reject (and MCMC, slide 7) is used in Bayesian statistics, where normalising constants are often impossible to compute.

Slide 7/30: Stochastic simulation

On the slide. This was a brief introduction to stochastic simulation. Refer to the book Simulation by Sheldon Ross! Some popular techniques: the inverse transform method, accept–reject (rejection sampling), importance sampling, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods: the Hastings–Metropolis algorithm, Gibbs sampling, slice sampling.

The three methods you studied, side by side.

Inverse transform Accept–reject Importance sampling
Output exact samples of X exact samples of X an estimate of E[h(X)] (weighted samples)
Needs F−1 (or a CDF you can search) pdf/pmf p (up to a constant), a proposal q and c≥maxp/q pdf f, a proposal g>0 where hf=0
Cost one uniform per sample c rounds per sample on average n proposal samples per estimate
Typical use Exp, Geometric, Weibull, Cauchy, discrete tables Beta, Gamma with non-integer shape, normal, truncated distributions rare events, variance reduction

MCMC in one paragraph (not examined in detail). When even accept–reject is too wasteful, for example in high dimensions where c becomes astronomically large, MCMC builds a Markov chain whose long-run distribution is the target p, and uses the chain's states as (dependent) samples. Hastings–Metropolis proposes a move and accepts it with a probability that, like accept–reject, involves only ratios of p. Gibbs sampling updates one coordinate at a time from its conditional distribution. These methods rely on convergence results for dependent sequences, which is one more reason for the next topic.

Slide 7/30 (second): "Convergence of Random Variables"

This is a section title. (The deck has two slides numbered 7.) Everything from here to the end of L18 is about this.

Slide 8/30: Pointwise convergence

On the slide. When do we say {xn​} converges to x∈R? We say xn​→x if for every ϵ>0 we can find N(ϵ)∈N such that ∣xn​−x∣<ϵ for n>N(ϵ). What about convergence of functions? We say that the sequence of functions Fn​(⋅) converges to F(⋅) pointwise if the sequence {Fn​(x)} converges to F(x) for all x∈R.

Reading the ϵ–N definition. Pick any tolerance ϵ, however small. Then from some index on, every term is within ϵ of x. The index N(ϵ) may grow as ϵ shrinks; that is allowed. Two ways a sequence fails: it keeps jumping away (like (−1)n), or it drifts off (like n).

Example. xn​=n1​→0: given ϵ, take N(ϵ)=⌈1/ϵ⌉; then n>N gives n1​<ϵ.

Pointwise convergence of functions. Freeze a point x. Then F1​(x),F2​(x),… is an ordinary sequence of numbers. Pointwise convergence asks that each such sequence converges, separately for each x.

Example. Fn​(x)=xn on [0,1]. For 0≤x<1, xn→0. For x=1, 1n=1→1. So

Fn​→F pointwise,F(x)={01​0≤x<1x=1​

Every Fn​ is continuous but the limit is not. Pointwise convergence can break continuity.

Why this slide is here. A random variable is a function X:Ω→R. So a sequence of random variables X1​,X2​,… is a sequence of functions on Ω, and "Xn​→X pointwise on Ω" (that is, Xn​(ω)→X(ω) for every outcome ω) is the first and strongest notion of convergence, sure convergence, in L17. CDFs are functions on R, and "FXn​​(x)→FX​(x) pointwise" (at continuity points) is the weakest notion, convergence in distribution, in L18.

Slide 9/30: Uniform convergence

On the slide. (Pointwise definition repeated.) For every x, the sequence {Fn​(x)} converges to F(x). For every ϵ, there exists N(ϵ,x), which can depend on x. Only those Fn​(x) are ϵ-close to F(x) for which n>N(ϵ,x). If N(ϵ,x)=N(ϵ) (independent of x) for every x∈R, then the convergence of Fn​ to F is called uniform convergence.

The difference is the order of "for all" and "there exists".

  • Pointwise: for every x and every ϵ, there is an N(ϵ,x). Different points may need different, ever larger, N.
  • Uniform: for every ϵ there is one N(ϵ) that works for all x at once.

Equivalently, uniform convergence means the worst-case gap goes to 0:

Fn​→F uniformly⟺xsup​∣Fn​(x)−F(x)∣→0​

Picture an ϵ-band around the graph of F. Uniform convergence means that from some n on, the whole graph of Fn​ lies inside the band.

Examples.

  1. xn on [0,1]: pointwise, not uniform. At a fixed x<1, xn<ϵ needs n>lnxlnϵ​. With ϵ=0.1: x=0.5 needs n>3.3, but x=0.99 needs n>229, and as x→1 the required N blows up. No single N works. Equivalently, sup0≤x<1​∣xn−0∣=1 for every n. On [0,0.9] the convergence is uniform, since sup=0.9n→0. Uniformity depends on the domain.
  2. Fn​(x)=nx​ on R: pointwise to 0, not uniform. N(ϵ,x)=ϵ∣x∣​ grows with ∣x∣; supx​n∣x∣​=∞. On any bounded interval it is uniform.
  3. Fn​(x)=1+nx2x​ on R: uniform to 0. Calculus gives the maximum of ∣Fn​∣ at x=n​1​, with value 2n​1​→0. One N(ϵ)=4ϵ21​ works for all x.
  4. Fn​(x)=nxe−nx on [0,∞): pointwise to 0, not uniform. For fixed x>0, the exponential beats the n. But the bump at x=n1​ always has height e−1, and it just moves towards 0. So sup∣Fn​∣=e−1 does not shrink.
  5. A CDF example: Fn​(x)=1−e−nx for x>0 (and 0 for x≤0). This is the CDF of Exp(n). It tends to 1 for each x>0 and stays 0 for x≤0. The convergence is not uniform on (0,∞), since supx>0​e−nx=1, but it is uniform on [a,∞) for any a>0. This exact example returns in L18 (slide 23), where the point x=0 causes trouble.

A fact worth knowing. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. So example 1's discontinuous limit already proves that the convergence there cannot be uniform.

Slide 10/30: Convergence of a sequence of random variables

On the slide. We are now interested in the convergence of an infinite sequence of random variables {Xn​} to some limiting random variable X. What does Xn​→X even mean? When you perform the random experiment once, you get a sequence of realizations {xn​} and x. If you are "lucky", maybe xn​→x. But if you perform the experiment again, you may not be so lucky and get a different sequence {xn′​} which may not converge to x′. We will come up with notions of convergence that depend on how often you see the sequence of realizations converging.

A concrete version. Toss a fair coin forever, and let Xn​ be the fraction of heads in the first n tosses. One run of the experiment is one infinite sequence of tosses, an outcome ω, and it produces a sequence of numbers xn​=Xn​(ω).

  • ω=HTHTHT… gives xn​→21​. Lucky.
  • ω=HHHH… gives xn​=1 for all n, so xn​→1=21​. Unlucky.
  • ω=HTTHHHHTTTTTTTT… (blocks that double in length, alternating) gives xn​ swinging between about 31​ and 32​ forever, with no limit at all. Also unlucky.

So "does Xn​→21​?" has no yes/no answer for every ω. The useful questions are how many outcomes are unlucky, measured by probability, and how badly. The answers give different notions:

How we measure "converging" Notion Where
xn​→x for every outcome sure convergence L17
the unlucky outcomes have probability 0 almost sure convergence L17
for each large n, a big deviation ∣Xn​−X∣>ϵ is improbable convergence in probability L18
the average deviation E∣Xn​−X∣r goes to 0 convergence in r-th mean L18
only the CDFs converge convergence in distribution L18

For the coin, the SLLN (L17) says the unlucky outcomes have probability 0: the fraction of heads converges to 21​ almost surely.

Slide 11/30: What can X and Xn​ be?

On the slide. Convergence of Xn​→X. Here X could even be a deterministic number. The Xn​'s could be dependent on each other. Each random variable Xn​ could have a different law (pmf/pdf).

Each bullet with an example.

  • X deterministic. The sample mean μ^​n​ converges to the constant μ (SLLN). A constant c is a random variable too: X(ω)=c for every ω.
  • The Xn​ dependent. The sample means μ^​n​ and μ^​n+1​ share n of their terms, so they are strongly dependent. The SLLN needs i.i.d. inputs Xi​, but the sequence that converges, μ^​n​, is not i.i.d.
  • Different laws. Xn​∼N(0,n1​), or Xn​∼ Binomial(n,nλ​), change distribution with n.

In general, nothing ties the Xn​ together except that they all live on the same probability space Ω (needed for every mode except convergence in distribution).

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

Accept–reject

B1 (last year's Tutorial 6 Q9). Target p(x)=2x on [0,1], proposal q=U(0,1). (a) Smallest c with p≤cq. (b) The algorithm.

Solution

Solution. (a) q=1 on [0,1], so q(x)p(x)​=2x≤2, with equality at x=1. c=2.

(b)

  1. Draw Y∼U(0,1) and U∼U(0,1) independently.
  2. If U≤cq(Y)p(Y)​=22Y​=Y, output X=Y. Otherwise go back to step 1.

Each round accepts with probability c1​=21​, so on average 2 rounds per sample.

B9 (last year's HW7 Q8). Expected number of iterations to generate k samples by rejection?

Solution

Solution. Each round accepts with probability c1​, independently. So the rounds needed for the j-th sample, Nj​, are Geometric(c1​) with E[Nj​]=c, and

B10 (concept check, exam style). Is the following "proof" correct? "Accepted samples have pmf p because P(accept∣y)=cq(y)p(y)​, which is proportional to p(y)."

Answer. No. P(accept∣y) is proportional to q(y)p(y)​, not to p(y). The missing step is multiplying by the proposal probability q(y) (slide 5's joint probability), which turns qp​ into p. And for a pmf the conclusion also needs the normalisation by P(accept)=c1​.

Pointwise and uniform convergence

B11. Decide pointwise and uniform convergence on the given domain. (a) fn​(x)=nsin(nx)​ on R. (b) fn​(x)=1+nxnx​ on [0,∞). (c) Fn​(x)=1{x≥n1​} on R, the CDF of the constant random variable n1​.

Solution

Solution. (a) ∣fn​(x)∣≤n1​ for every x, so sup∣fn​∣≤n1​→0: uniform convergence to 0. (b) fn​(0)=0; for x>0, fn​(x)=1+1/(nx)1​→1. So the pointwise limit is 1{x>0}. Not uniform: at x=n1​, fn​=21​, so sup∣fn​−f∣≥21​ for every n. (c) For x≤0: Fn​(x)=0 for all n, since n1​>0≥x. For x>0: Fn​(x)=1 once n≥x1​. So Fn​→1{x>0} pointwise. Not uniform: at x=2n1​ the gap is 1. Look ahead. The constants n1​ obviously converge to the constant 0, whose CDF is 1{x≥0}. The pointwise limit 1{x>0} agrees with it everywhere except at x=0, the jump point. This is why L18 defines convergence in distribution by asking for Fn​(x)→F(x) only at points where F is continuous.
