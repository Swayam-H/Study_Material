Lecture 2

Σ-algebras, Borel sets and consequences of the axioms

Deck 1, slides 21 to 36⏱ 16 min read✓ 6 practice problems
Solutions stay hidden until you click them.

Big picture. Lecture 1 showed that P cannot always measure every subset of Ω. The fix is to choose a collection F of subsets, the events, that is closed under the operations we need (complements, countable unions). Such a collection is a σ-algebra, and (Ω,F,P) is a probability space. For finite Ω we just take all subsets. For [0,1] and R we take the Borel σ-algebra, generated by intervals. The second half derives the everyday rules (P(Ac)=1−P(A), inclusion–exclusion, the union bound) from the axioms, and separates "probability zero" from "impossible".

Part A: the slides

Slides 21 and 22 (the counter-example) are covered at the end of Lecture 1.

Slide 23/73: Towards a σ-algebra

On the slide. F={∅,Ω,R−​,R+​}. The domain should have nice, obvious properties: ∅,Ω∈F; if B∈F then Bc∈F; if A1​,A2​∈F then A1​∪A2​ and A1​∩A2​∈F. A domain with such properties is a σ-algebra.

Slide 24/73: σ-algebra as the domain of P

On the slide. A σ-algebra (event space) F on Ω is a collection of subsets with (1) ∅∈F and Ω∈F; (2) A∈F⇒Ac∈F; (3) A1​,A2​,⋯∈F⇒⋃n=1∞​An​∈F. It is closed under complements and countable unions. Is it also closed under countable intersections? When Ω is finite, is P(Ω) a σ-algebra? Yes, and then we use P(Ω) as the domain.

 F is a σ-algebra⟺Ω∈F,A∈F⇒Ac∈F,A1​,A2​,…∈F⇒⋃n​An​∈F ​

Closed under countable intersections (the question). Yes, by De Morgan:

n⋂​An​=(n⋃​Anc​)c.

Each Anc​∈F (rule 2), their union is in F (rule 3), and its complement is in F (rule 2). □ The same trick gives differences, A∖B=A∩Bc, and symmetric differences, AΔB=(A∖B)∪(B∖A). These three facts are last year's Tutorial 2 Q4.

Finite unions are included. Take A3​=A4​=⋯=∅ in rule 3.

P(Ω) is always a σ-algebra (for any Ω): every set you can form is a subset of Ω. The problem in Lecture 1 was never that P(R) fails to be a σ-algebra. It is that no sensible P exists on it.

Slides 25/73 and 26/73: Formal definition of a probability measure

On the slides. A probability measure on the measurable space (Ω,F) is a function P:F→[0,1] with (1) P(∅)=0, P(Ω)=1; (2) countable additivity for disjoint A1​,A2​,⋯∈F. The trio (Ω,F,P) is a probability space. When ∣Ω∣<∞ we take F=2Ω. Identify the probability space in the coin and dice experiments.

Coin. Ω={H,T}, F={∅,{H},{T},{H,T}}, and P(∅)=0, P({H})=P({T})=21​, P(Ω)=1. (For a biased coin, P({H})=p and P({T})=1−p.)

Die. Ω={1,…,6}, F=2Ω (64 events), and P(A)=∣A∣/6 for every A∈F. You do not list 64 numbers. You give P on the six single outcomes and additivity gives the rest.

What changed from slide 19. The domain is now F, not "all subsets". Axiom 2 (0≤P≤1) is built into the codomain [0,1].

Slides 27/73 to 29/73: Building a σ-algebra for U[0,1]

On the slides. Ω=[0,1]. Try F={∅,[0,1],[0,0.5),[0.5,1]}. Problem: we cannot measure [0.25,0.75], though we know its probability is 0.5. Add it: F+=F∪{[0.25,0.75]}. Is F+ a σ-algebra? No. Adding intersections, unions and complements gives F++ = {∅, [0,1], [0,.5), [.5,1], [.25,.75], [.25,.5), [.5,.75], [.25,1], [0,.75], [0,.25)∪[.5,1], [0,.5)∪(.75,1], [0,.25), (.75,1]}. Still not a σ-algebra: each new set demands more. This process is generating a σ-algebra, and continuing it gives the Borel σ-algebra B[0,1].

Why F is fine but too small. {∅,[0,1],[0,0.5),[0.5,1]} is a σ-algebra (check complements and unions). But it can only answer "is the number below one half?".

Why F+ fails. [0.25,0.75]c=[0,0.25)∪(0.75,1] is not in F+.

Finishing the job. Here is a cleaner way to see what the σ-algebra generated by [0,0.5) and [0.25,0.75] looks like. These two sets cut [0,1] into four disjoint atoms:

a1​=[0,0.25),a2​=[0.25,0.5),a3​=[0.5,0.75],a4​=(0.75,1].

Every set in the generated σ-algebra is a union of some of the atoms, and every such union is in it. So it has exactly 24=16 sets. The 13 sets in F++ are all unions of atoms. The three missing ones are

a1​∪a3​=[0,0.25)∪[0.5,0.75],a2​∪a4​=[0.25,0.5)∪(0.75,1],a1​∪a4​=[0,0.25)∪(0.75,1],

the last being [0.25,0.75]c. Adding them closes the collection. (This is the "partition generates all unions of its blocks" fact, which is last year's HW 2 Q6, solved in Part B.)

Slide 30/73: The Borel σ-algebra B[0,1]

On the slide. B[0,1] is the σ-algebra generated by closed intervals [a,b]⊆[0,1]. Does it contain (a,b), [a,b), (a,b]? Yes: (a,b)=⋃n=1∞​[a+n1​,b−n1​] and (a,b]=⋂n=1∞​(a,b+n1​). So B[0,1] is equally generated by intervals of any type.

"Generated by" means: the smallest σ-algebra containing all the given sets. It exists because the intersection of any family of σ-algebras is again a σ-algebra, so you intersect all σ-algebras that contain the given sets.

Checking the two identities.

  • x∈(a,b) means a<x<b. For large n, a+n1​≤x≤b−n1​, so x is in the union. Conversely, every point of every [a+n1​,b−n1​] lies strictly between a and b. (For small n these intervals may be empty, which is harmless.)
  • x∈(a,b] iff a<x<b+n1​ for every n. That is the intersection.

More conversions you should be able to do.

{a}=n⋂​(a−n1​,a+n1​),[a,b)=n⋃​[a,b−n1​],[a,b]=n⋂​(a−n1​,b+n1​).

So once one type of interval is in, all types are in, and so is every single point and every countable set (a countable union of points). The rationals in [0,1] are a Borel set.

Slide 31/73: The Borel σ-algebra B(R)

On the slide. If Ω=R, B(R) is generated by open intervals (a,b). It contains [a,b], [a,b), (a,∞), [a,∞), (−∞,b], (−∞,b), {a}. How would you define B(R2)?

Unbounded intervals come from countable unions: (a,∞)=⋃n​(a,a+n) and (−∞,b]=⋃n​[b−n,b].

The generator that matters most later: B(R) is also generated by the half-lines {(−∞,x]:x∈R} (last year's HW 2 Q5, proved in Part B). That is why the CDF FX​(x)=P(X≤x) is enough to pin down a whole distribution (Lecture 7).

Slide 32/73: Consequences of the axioms

On the slide. P(Ac)=1−P(A). P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). If A⊆B, prove P(A)≤P(B) ("A implies B"). P(⋃i=1∞​Bi​)≤∑i=1∞​P(Bi​) (Boole's inequality). HW: what is P(A∪B∪C)? State and prove inclusion–exclusion for P(⋃i=1n​Ai​).

Every proof follows one recipe: write the set as a disjoint union, then use additivity.

Complement. A and Ac are disjoint with union Ω, so P(A)+P(Ac)=1.

Monotonicity. If A⊆B, then B=A∪(B∖A), a disjoint union. So

P(B)=P(A)+P(B∖A) ≥ P(A),

and as a bonus P(B∖A)=P(B)−P(A) when A⊆B​.

Union of two. Split A∪B into three disjoint pieces: A∖B, A∩B, B∖A. Using P(A∖B)=P(A)−P(A∩B) (since A=(A∖B)∪(A∩B)):

P(A∪B)=[P(A)−P(A∩B)]+P(A∩B)+[P(B)−P(A∩B)]=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).

Boole's inequality (union bound). "Disjointify": let C1​=B1​ and Ci​=Bi​∖(B1​∪⋯∪Bi−1​). The Ci​ are disjoint, Ci​⊆Bi​, and ⋃i​Ci​=⋃i​Bi​ (each point is put in the Ci​ for the first Bi​ containing it). So

P(i⋃​Bi​)=i∑​P(Ci​)≤i∑​P(Bi​).□

Use it as a quick upper bound: if each of 10 components fails with probability 0.01, the probability that some component fails is at most 0.1, whatever the dependence.

HW: three sets. Apply the two-set rule twice, with D=B∪C:

P(A∪D)=P(A)+P(D)−P(A∩D),A∩D=(A∩B)∪(A∩C).

Expanding P(D) and P((A∩B)∪(A∩C)) with the two-set rule (note (A∩B)∩(A∩C)=A∩B∩C):

P(A∪B∪C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)−P(AB)−P(AC)−P(BC)+P(ABC)​

HW: inclusion–exclusion for n events.

P(i=1⋃n​Ai​)=i∑​P(Ai​)−i<j∑​P(Ai​Aj​)+i<j<k∑​P(Ai​Aj​Ak​)−⋯+(−1)n+1P(A1​⋯An​).

Proof. By induction on n, applying the two-set rule to (⋃i≤n​Ai​)∪An+1​; or reuse the counting argument under Lecture 1, slide 13: an outcome lying in exactly m≥1 of the events is counted (1m​)−(2m​)+⋯=1 time, so weighting each outcome by its probability gives the formula. □

Bonferroni's inequality (last year's Tutorial 1, Sets Q1). P(A∩B)≥P(A)+P(B)−1, and in general

P(A1​∩⋯∩An​)≥i=1∑n​P(Ai​)−(n−1).

Proof. Apply the union bound to the complements and use De Morgan:

1−P(i⋂​Ai​)=P(i⋃​Aic​)≤i∑​(1−P(Ai​))=n−i∑​P(Ai​).

Rearrange. □ (Slide 32 calls the union bound "Boole's/Bonferroni's inequality"; both names are used.)

Slide 33/73: Impossible event vs zero-probability event

On the slide. In U[0,1], P(ω=0.5)=0: a point has zero length. If P([a,b])=b−a, then P({0.5})=P([0.5,0.5])=0. Every outcome of this experiment has probability zero. So events of zero probability can happen; they are not impossible. P(∅)=0 too, but ∅ never happens. Example: P([0,0.25]∩[0.75,1])=P(∅)=0 will never happen.

impossible (A=∅) ⟹ P(A)=0,butP(A)=0 ⟹ A=∅.

Probability zero means "happens with negligible frequency", not "cannot happen". In the uniform experiment some number is picked, and whichever one it is had probability zero. Likewise, P(A)=1 ("almost surely") does not mean A=Ω. These ideas return in L17 (almost sure convergence).

Slide 34/73: The apparent paradox

On the slide. Ω=⋃ω∈Ω​{ω}, so P(Ω)=∑ω​P({ω})=0? What is the problem? Ω is uncountable and P is only countably additive. ⋃ω​{ω} is an uncountable disjoint union.

Axiom 3 says nothing about uncountable unions, so the second equality is not allowed. This is exactly why the axiom says countable: if it allowed arbitrary unions, the uniform distribution on [0,1] could not exist.

Slides 35/73 and 36/73: Limits and continuity

On the slides. How do you define limn→∞​an​=L, limx→c​f(x)=L, and continuity at c? The (ϵ,δ) definition. In terms of sequences: f is continuous if x→c implies f(x)→f(c). A set function S is continuous if An​→A implies S(An​)→S(A). Is P continuous? Proof in Lecture 3.

The definitions.

  • an​→L: for every ϵ>0 there is N with ∣an​−L∣<ϵ for all n≥N.
  • limx→c​f(x)=L: for every ϵ>0 there is δ>0 with ∣f(x)−L∣<ϵ whenever 0<∣x−c∣<δ.
  • f is continuous at c if limx→c​f(x)=f(c). Equivalently (sequential form): for every sequence xn​→c, f(xn​)→f(c).

The sequential form is the one that carries over to sets: replace "xn​→c" with "An​→A" (made precise for monotone sequences on slide 37) and ask whether P(An​)→P(A). The answer is yes, and it follows from countable additivity (Lecture 3).

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's HW 2 Q1; Bertsekas Problem 1.7). A four-sided die is rolled until the first even number appears. Give the sample space and a σ-algebra.

Solution

Solution. Record the sequence of rolls. Every roll before the last is odd (1 or 3) and the last is even (2 or 4):

Ω={2,4, 12,14,32,34, 112,114,132,…}∪{infinite sequences of 1s and 3s}.

The infinite sequences ("an even number never appears") must be included for Ω to be exhaustive, even though they have probability 0 in total. If you only care about the finite sequences, Ω is countable and you can take F=P(Ω). With the infinite sequences included, Ω is uncountable. The standard choice is then the σ-algebra generated by the "cylinder" events "the first k rolls are r1​,…,rk​", which contains every event you would ask about (such as "the game lasts exactly n rolls").

B2 (last year's HW 2 Q6). {A1​,A2​,…} is a finite or countable partition of Ω. What σ-algebra does it generate?

Solution

Solution. σ({An​})={⋃i∈I​Ai​:I⊆{1,2,…}}, all unions of blocks (with I=∅ giving ∅).

  • It is a σ-algebra. Ω is the union of all blocks. The complement of ⋃i∈I​Ai​ is ⋃i∈/I​Ai​ (the blocks partition Ω). A countable union of unions of blocks is a union of blocks.
  • It is the smallest. Any σ-algebra containing every Ai​ contains all their countable unions, and I is countable.

With n blocks it has 2n sets. This is the "atoms" picture under slides 27 to 29.

B3 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q2). Ω=N and F={A⊆N:A finite or Ac finite}. Show F is not a σ-algebra.

Solution

Solution. F contains Ω (Ωc=∅ is finite) and is closed under complements (the definition is symmetric). It is even closed under finite unions. But take An​={2n}: each is finite, so in F. Their countable union is the even numbers E. Neither E nor Ec (the odd numbers) is finite, so E∈/F. It fails rule 3. (Such a collection is called an algebra: closed under finite but not countable unions.)

B4 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q7). On (Ω,F,P) let G={A∈F:P(A)=0 or 1}. Show G is a σ-algebra.

Solution

Solution.

  1. P(Ω)=1, so Ω∈G.
  2. If P(A)∈{0,1} then P(Ac)=1−P(A)∈{0,1}.
  3. Let A1​,A2​,⋯∈G and A=⋃n​An​. If some P(Ak​)=1, then 1≥P(A)≥P(Ak​)=1 by monotonicity. Otherwise every P(An​)=0, and by the union bound P(A)≤∑n​0=0.

Either way P(A)∈{0,1}. □

B5 (last year's HW 2 Q5). Show that the σ-algebra generated by A={(−∞,x]:x∈R} is B(R).

Solution

Solution. Write σ(A) for it. Two inclusions.

  • σ(A)⊆B(R): each (−∞,x] is Borel (slide 31), and B(R) is a σ-algebra, so it contains the smallest σ-algebra containing A.
  • B(R)⊆σ(A): it is enough to show every open interval (a,b) is in σ(A). First, (a,b]=(−∞,b]∩(−∞,a]c∈σ(A). Then
(a,b)=n≥1⋃​(a,b−n1​]∈σ(A).

Since σ(A) is a σ-algebra containing all the generators of B(R), it contains B(R). □

B6 (inclusion–exclusion, last year's Tutorial 2 Q1). A fair die is rolled n times. Find the probability that all six faces appear.

Solution

Solution. Let Ai​ = "face i never appears". We want 1−P(A1​∪⋯∪A6​). Any k specified faces are all missing with probability (66−k​)n, and there are (k6​) ways to choose them. Inclusion–exclusion:

P(all faces appear)=k=0∑6​(−1)k(k6​)(1−6k​)n.

Check n=6: the formula gives 666!​=46656720​≈0.0154, the probability that six rolls are a permutation of 1,…,6. ✓
