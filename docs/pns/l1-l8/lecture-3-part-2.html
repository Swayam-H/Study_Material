Lecture 3, part 2

Independence

Deck 1, slides 47 to 56 · 17 min read · 12 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other: P(A∣B)=P(A), or equivalently P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). The slides then sort out four ideas that students often confuse: mutual vs pairwise independence, independent vs mutually exclusive, the fact that zero-probability events are independent of everything, and conditional independence, which can hold without independence and vice versa.

Part A: the slides

Slide 47/73: Plan

On the slide. Independence of events; mutually exclusive events; conditional independence; zero-probability events and independence; counting (HW); motivation for random variables.

Slide 48/73: Independence

On the slide. Toss a coin and roll a die together. Identify the probability space. What is P({H,6})? What is P({T,odd})=P(⋃i=1,3,5​{(T,i)})? In both cases P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B), which implies P(A∣B)=P(A). A,B are independent iff P(A∣B)=P(A) and P(B∣A)=P(B), iff P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).

Probability space. Ω={H,T}×{1,…,6}, 12 equally likely outcomes; F=2Ω; P(E)=∣E∣/12.

The two computations.

P({(H,6)})=121​=21​⋅61​,P({(T,1),(T,3),(T,5)})=123​=41​=21​⋅21​.

In each case the probability of "coin event and die event" is the product of the separate probabilities.

A,B independent⟺P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)​

Why use the product form as the definition. It is symmetric in A and B, and it still makes sense when P(A)=0 or P(B)=0, where P(A∣B) is undefined. When P(B)>0 it is equivalent to P(A∣B)=P(A): divide by P(B).

Slide 49/73: Complements of independent events

On the slide. If A and B are independent, are Ac and Bc independent? What about A and Bc? If A1​,…,An​ are independent, prove P(⋃i=1n​Ai​)=1−∏i=1n​[1−P(Ai​)].

A and Bc (yes). A=(A∩B)∪(A∩Bc) disjointly, so

P(A∩Bc)=P(A)−P(A)P(B)=P(A)(1−P(B))=P(A)P(Bc).

Ac and Bc (yes). Apply the same step again, now to the independent pair (Bc,A), to get that Bc and Ac are independent. Or directly with De Morgan:

P(AcBc)=1−P(A∪B)=1−P(A)−P(B)+P(A)P(B)=(1−P(A))(1−P(B)).

So if A and B are independent, so is every pair from {A,Ac}×{B,Bc}. Learning that B did not happen is also information, and it is equally useless for predicting A.

Union of independent events. "At least one" is the complement of "none":

P(i⋃​Ai​)=1−P(i⋂​Aic​)=1−i=1∏n​P(Aic​)=1−i=1∏n​(1−P(Ai​))​.

The middle step uses that the complements of (mutually) independent events are mutually independent (the same argument as above, applied to each subcollection). Typical use: three independent alarms, each working with probability 0.9. At least one works with probability 1−0.13=0.999.

Slide 50/73: Mutual and pairwise independence

On the slide. {Ai​,i∈I} are mutually independent if P(⋂j∈J​Aj​)=∏j∈J​P(Aj​) for every finite subset J⊆I. Pairwise independent: every pair is independent. Mutual implies pairwise, but not conversely. HW: find an example where pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence.

For three events, mutual independence means four conditions:

P(AB)=P(A)P(B),P(AC)=P(A)P(C),P(BC)=P(B)P(C),P(ABC)=P(A)P(B)P(C).

Pairwise independence is only the first three.

HW: the standard counter-example (Bertsekas Example 1.22). Toss two fair coins. Let

A={1st toss H},B={2nd toss H},C={the two tosses agree}={HH,TT}.

Each has probability 21​. Each pairwise intersection is the single outcome HH (for example A∩C={HH}), with probability 41​=21​⋅21​. So they are pairwise independent. But

P(A∩B∩C)=P({HH})=41​ = 81​=P(A)P(B)P(C).

Not mutually independent: once you know A and B, you know C for sure.

Slide 51/73: Independence example

On the slide. Pick a number at random from {1,…,10}. A: less than 7. B: less than 8. C: even. Are the events mutually independent? Which pairs are independent?

A={1,…,6}, B={1,…,7}, C={2,4,6,8,10}, so P(A)=0.6, P(B)=0.7, P(C)=0.5.

Pair Intersection P(intersection) Product Independent?
A,B A (since A⊆B) 0.6 0.42 no
A,C {2,4,6} 0.3 0.3 yes
B,C {2,4,6} 0.3 0.35 no

Only A and C are independent, so the three are not mutually independent (also P(ABC)=0.3=0.21). Note that A,C are independent "by accident": exactly half of {1,…,6} is even, matching the overall proportion. Change A to "less than 8" (that is, B) and it breaks.

Slide 52/73: Correlation between events

On the slide. A,B are positively correlated iff P(A∣B)>P(A), negatively correlated iff P(A∣B)<P(A). A and B have the same correlation as Ac and Bc. A and B have the opposite correlation to A and Bc.

Multiply through by P(B): positive correlation means P(AB)>P(A)P(B). This form is symmetric, so "B makes A more likely" is the same as "A makes B more likely".

Proof of the two claims. Let d=P(AB)−P(A)P(B) (the "covariance" of the events). Then

P(ABc)−P(A)P(Bc)=[P(A)−P(AB)]−P(A)[1−P(B)]=−d,
P(AcBc)−P(Ac)P(Bc)=[1−P(A)−P(B)+P(AB)]−[1−P(A)][1−P(B)]=d.

So (A,Bc) has the opposite sign and (Ac,Bc) the same sign. With d=0 this re-proves slide 49. In slide 51, A and B are positively correlated (0.6>0.42), and B and C negatively (0.3<0.35).

Slide 53/73: Mutually exclusive vs independent

On the slide. If A and B are mutually exclusive, they are not independent (and vice versa). Mutually exclusive: P(A∩B)=0, P(A∣B)=0, P(A∣Bc)=P(Bc)P(A)​. Independent: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B), P(A∣B)=P(A), P(A∣Bc)=P(A). If A⊆B, the events are neither mutually exclusive nor independent.

The precise statement needs positive probabilities: if P(A)>0 and P(B)>0, disjoint events are never independent, since P(A∩B)=0<P(A)P(B).

Intuition: disjoint events are strongly (negatively) dependent. Learning B happened tells you for certain that A did not. Independence means learning B tells you nothing.

The A⊆B case. With 0<P(A) and P(B)<1: A∩B=A=∅ (not exclusive) and P(A∩B)=P(A)>P(A)P(B) (positively correlated, not independent).

Slide 54/73: Zero-probability events are independent of everything

On the slide. Let P(E)=0. For any F we want P(E∩F)=0. E∩F⊆E, so P(E∩F)≤P(E).

Completing it: 0≤P(E∩F)≤P(E)=0, so P(E∩F)=0=P(E)P(F). Independent. □

Similarly an event with P(E)=1 is independent of everything (its complement has probability 0, then use slide 49). So an event can be both disjoint from and independent of another one, as long as one has probability 0. This is the loophole in slide 53.

Slide 55/73: Conditional independence

On the slide. P(A∣BC)=P(B∣C)P(AB∣C)​, so P(AB∣C)=P(A∣BC)P(B∣C). A and B are conditionally independent given C (P(C)>0) if P(AB∣C)=P(A∣C)P(B∣C). As a consequence P(A∣BC)=P(A∣C). HW: verify whether A and B are conditionally independent given C in the experiment of picking a number from {1,…,10}.

P(A∩B∣C)=P(A∣C)P(B∣C)​

It is ordinary independence, but under the measure P(⋅∣C). Equivalent form: once you know C, also learning B does not change the probability of A.

The identity on the slide. P(A∣BC)=P(BC)P(ABC)​=P(BC)/P(C)P(ABC)/P(C)​=P(B∣C)P(AB∣C)​. This is the chain rule inside the conditional world.

HW. With A={1,…,6}, B={1,…,7}, C={2,4,6,8,10} from slide 51:

P(A∣C)=5∣{2,4,6}∣​=53​,P(B∣C)=53​,P(AB∣C)=5∣{2,4,6}∣​=53​.

Since 53​=259​, A and B are not conditionally independent given C. This is not surprising: A⊆B, so given C, knowing A guarantees B.

Slide 56/73: Conditional independence example

On the slide. Two coins, one fair and one fake (both sides heads). Choose a coin uniformly and toss it twice. A: first toss H, P(A)=43​. B: second toss H, P(B)=43​. C: coin 1 (the fair one) is chosen. P(A∣C)=P(B∣C)=21​ and P(A∩B∣C)=41​, so A and B are conditionally independent given C. HW: are A and B independent?

Where 43​ comes from (total probability over the coin): P(A)=21​⋅21​+21​⋅1=43​.

HW. Again total probability:

P(A∩B)=P(AB∣C)P(C)+P(AB∣Cc)P(Cc)=41​⋅21​+1⋅21​=85​.

But P(A)P(B)=169​. Since 85​=1610​>169​, A and B are not independent (they are positively correlated).

Why. The first toss is evidence about which coin you hold. A head on toss 1 raises the chance you have the fake coin from 21​ to P(Cc∣A)=3/41/2​=32​, which in turn makes a head on toss 2 more likely: P(B∣A)=3/45/8​=65​>43​. Given the coin, the tosses are independent. Without knowing the coin, they are linked through it.

Takeaway. Conditional independence does not imply independence, and independence does not imply conditional independence. (Example of the second: two independent fair coins, A = first is H, B = second is H, C = "exactly one head". Then P(AB∣C)=0 but P(A∣C)P(B∣C)=41​.)

Part B: extra worked examples and problems (conditional probability and independence)

B1 (last year's HW 2 Q9; Bayes). The alarm at a power plant goes off with probability 0.99 if something is wrong and 0.02 if nothing is wrong. Something is wrong on 1 day in 100. (a) P(alarm)? (b) P(wrong∣alarm)?

Solution

Solution. W = something wrong, L = alarm. (a) P(L)=0.99(0.01)+0.02(0.99)=0.0099+0.0198=0.0297​. (b) P(W∣L)=0.02970.0099​=31​​.

B2 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q3). A disease affects 1 in 10,000. False positive rate 2%, false negative rate 1%. A random person tests positive. Probability they have the disease?

Solution

Solution. P(D)=10−4, P(+∣D)=0.99, P(+∣Dc)=0.02.

P(D∣+)=0.99×10−4+0.02×0.99990.99×10−4​=0.0200970.000099​≈0.0049​.

Under half a percent. Same lesson as the robot on slide 44.

B3 (last year's HW 2 Q7). The channel is good with probability 0.8. An error occurs with probability 0.1 if good and 0.3 if bad. Find P(E) and P(G∣Ec).

Solution

Solution. Tree: P(GE)=0.08, P(GEc)=0.72, P(GcE)=0.06, P(GcEc)=0.14. So P(E)=0.08+0.06=0.14​ and P(G∣Ec)=0.860.72​=4336​≈0.837​.

B4 (last year's HW 2 Q4). It rains with probability 0.3 and VC is crowded with probability 0.5. (a) Rain and crowding cannot happen together. You are late with probability 0.5 if it rains and 0.3 if crowded (and never otherwise). Find P(late). (b) Given late, which is more likely, rain or crowding? (c) Now rain and crowding are independent; you are late with probability 0.75 if both, 0.5 if rain only, 0.3 if crowded only. Find P(late).

Solution

Solution. (a) Partition: R, C, neither. P(L)=0.5(0.3)+0.3(0.5)+0=0.30​. (b) P(R∣L)=0.300.15​=0.5 and P(C∣L)=0.5: equally likely. (c) Partition into four cells: P(RC)=0.15, P(RCc)=0.15, P(RcC)=0.35, P(RcCc)=0.35:

P(L)=0.75(0.15)+0.5(0.15)+0.3(0.35)+0=0.1125+0.075+0.105=0.2925​.

B5 (last year's HW 2 Q3; Bertsekas Problem 1.19). Alice's paper is in drawer j with probability pj​. If it is in drawer i and she searches there, she finds it with probability di​. She searches drawer i and fails. Find the probability that the paper is in drawer j.

Solution

Solution. Let F = "search of drawer i fails". By total probability, P(F)=pi​(1−di​)+(1−pi​)⋅1=1−pi​di​. Bayes:

P(in j∣F)=⎩⎨⎧​1−pi​di​pj​​,1−pi​di​pi​(1−di​)​,​j=i,j=i.​

A failed search lowers belief in drawer i and raises every other drawer by the same factor.

B6 (last year's HW 2 Q2, the jerseys). Bag 1 has 100−k CSK and k RCB jerseys; Bag 2 has k CSK and 100−k RCB. Bag 1 is chosen with probability p. P(RCB)=0.4. (a) Find p. (b) Which k are valid? (c) k=30. (d) Given CSK, probability it came from Bag 1.

Solution

Solution. (a) 0.4=p100k​+(1−p)100100−k​, so 40=(100−k)−p(100−2k) and

p=100−2k60−k​(k=50).

(b) Need 0≤p≤1. If k<50 the denominator is positive: p≤1⟺60−k≤100−2k⟺k≤40. If k>50 it is negative: p≥0⟺k≥60, and then p≤1 holds. Valid: k∈{0,…,40}∪{60,…,100}. (c) p=4030​=0.75. (d) P(CSK)=0.75(0.7)+0.25(0.3)=0.6, so P(Bag 1∣CSK)=0.60.525​=0.875​.

B7 (last year's HW 2 Q10). 56% have a retirement plan (R), 68% health insurance (H), 49% both. (a) Neither? (b) P(H∣R)? (c) Independent? Mutually exclusive?

Solution

Solution. (a) 1−(0.56+0.68−0.49)=0.25. (b) 0.560.49​=0.875. (c) P(R)P(H)=0.3808=0.49: not independent (positively correlated, 0.875>0.68). Not exclusive either, since P(RH)=0.49>0.

B8 (last year's HW 2 Q11). Independent projects, P(A)=0.4, P(B)=0.7. (a) P(Bc∣Ac). (b) At least one succeeds. (c) Only A succeeds, given at least one does.

Solution

Solution. (a) Independence of complements: P(Bc∣Ac)=P(Bc)=0.3. (b) 1−0.6⋅0.3=0.82. (c) P(ABc)=0.4⋅0.3=0.12, and ABc⊆ "at least one", so the answer is 0.820.12​=416​.

B9 (last year's HW 2 Q12; reliability). C1​,C2​ in parallel form S1​; C3​,C4​ in series form S2​; S1​ and S2​ are in parallel. Each component works independently with probability 0.9. P(system works)?

Solution

Solution. P(S1​)=1−0.12=0.99 and P(S2​)=0.92=0.81. In parallel: 1−(1−0.99)(1−0.81)=1−0.01⋅0.19=0.9981​. Rule of thumb: series multiplies working probabilities; parallel multiplies failure probabilities.

B10 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q8). Each wing has 2 engines, each failing independently with probability 0.2. The plane survives if each wing has at least one working engine. (a) P(survive). (b) Engines per wing needed for P≥0.99. (c) Given survival, the probability all four engines are fine.

Solution

Solution. (a) A wing works with probability 1−0.22=0.96, so P=0.962=0.9216​. (b) With n per wing: (1−0.2n)2≥0.99. n=3 gives 0.9922=0.984 (not enough); n=4 gives 0.99842≈0.9968. So n=4​. (c) "All four fine" implies survival, so P=0.92160.84​=0.92160.4096​=94​​.

B11 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q5). P(T≥t)=4+t5​ for t≥1, where T is the toss of the first win. After 4 tosses without a win, what is the probability of winning on toss 5?

Solution

Solution. "4 tosses without winning" is {T≥5}. We want

P(T=5∣T≥5)=P(T≥5)P(T≥5)−P(T≥6)​=1−5/95/10​=1−109​=0.1​.

B12 (last year's Tutorial 2 Q6). A and B alternately roll two dice, A first. A wins on a sum of 6, B on a sum of 7. P(A wins)?

Solution

Solution. a=P(sum 6)=365​, b=P(sum 7)=366​. A round (A then B) ends without a winner with probability (1−a)(1−b). Condition on the first round (or sum a geometric series):

P(A)=a+(1−a)(1−b)P(A) ⟹ P(A)=1−(1−a)(1−b)a​=1−3631​⋅3630​5/36​=366180​=6130​​≈0.492.

Going first almost compensates for B's better target.
