Lecture 6

Functions of a random variable, variance, and the standard discrete families

Deck 2, slides 18 to 27 · 16 min read · 7 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. Three tools and four distributions. The tools: the PMF of g(X) (add the probabilities of all x that map to the same y), the expected value rule (LOTUS: E[g(X)]=∑g(x)pX​(x), no need for the PMF of g(X)), and the variance (the mean squared distance from the mean). The distributions: indicator/Bernoulli, binomial, geometric and Poisson. For each you should know the story (what it counts), the PMF, the mean and the variance, and be able to derive the last two.

Part A: the slides

Slide 18/31: A function of a random variable

On the slide. Y=∣X∣, where X is an integer chosen uniformly from −4 to 4: pX​(x)=91​ for x∈{−4,…,4}. What is the range of Y? {0,…,4}. What is pY​(2)? pY​(2)=∑{x:∣x∣=2}​pX​(x)=pX​(−2)+pX​(2)=92​.

The full PMF: 0 comes only from x=0, and every other y comes from ±y:

pY​(0)=91​,pY​(y)=92​(y=1,2,3,4).

Check: 91​+4⋅92​=1. ✓ (This is Bertsekas Example 2.1.)

Slide 19/31: The general rule

On the slide. Picture: bars of pX​ at −4,…,4, each 91​, folding onto pY​ at 0,…,4. Suppose Y=g(X) with X discrete. Then pY​(y)=∑{x:g(x)=y}​pX​(x). (Proof is HW.)

pY​(y)=x:g(x)=y∑​pX​(x)​

HW proof. The event {Y=y} is {ω:g(X(ω))=y}=⋃x:g(x)=y​{X=x}. These events are disjoint (different values of X) and there are countably many, so additivity gives the formula. □

Recipe. For each value y, list all x with g(x)=y and add their probabilities. This answers the question left open at the end of Lecture 5. When g is one-to-one the sum has one term and pY​(g(x))=pX​(x).

Slide 20/31: E[g(X)], the law of the unconscious statistician

On the slide. Theorem. If Y=g(X) with X discrete with PMF pX​, then E[Y]=∑x​g(x)pX​(x). Proof. E[Y]=∑y​ypY​(y)=∑y​∑{x:g(x)=y}​g(x)pX​(x)=∑x​g(x)pX​(x). □ (Wikipedia: Law of the unconscious statistician.)

E[g(X)]=x∑​g(x)pX​(x)​

Reading the proof. Start from the definition of E[Y]. Replace pY​(y) by the sum from slide 19, and move y inside, where y=g(x) for every x in that group. The double sum runs over all x, grouped by their value of g(x). Every x appears exactly once, so the grouping can be dropped.

Why it matters. Lecture 5 defined E[g(X)] this way. This theorem shows that the definition agrees with "compute the PMF of Y=g(X), then its mean". You never need the PMF of g(X) to find its expectation. The name: people use the formula "unconsciously", without noticing it needs a proof. (Last year's HW 4 Q6 asks for the continuous version, solved in Lecture 7, Part B.)

Check with slide 18. E[∣X∣] via Y: 0⋅91​+(1+2+3+4)92​=920​. Via LOTUS: 91​∑x=−44​∣x∣=920​. ✓

Slide 21/31: Towards variance

On the slide. Take g(X)=(X−E[X])2, the square of the deviation from the mean. It cannot tell if the deviation is positive or negative. E[g(X)] is the mean square deviation. In fact E[g(X)]​ quantifies the deviation.

Slide 22/31: Variance

On the slide. Var(X):=E[(X−E[X])2]. HW: prove E[(X−E[X])2]=E[X2]−E[X]2. σX​=Var(X)​ is the standard deviation. For a fair coin, using Ω′={+100,−100} instead of {1,−1} gives more variance. HW: what is Var(Y) for Y=aX+b?

Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−μ2,μ=E[X]​

HW 1: the shortcut formula. Expand the square and use linearity (μ is a constant):

E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2−2μX+μ2]=E[X2]−2μE[X]+μ2=E[X2]−2μ2+μ2=E[X2]−μ2.□

Two consequences: E[X2]≥(E[X])2 always (variance is non-negative), and Var(X)=0 iff X equals its mean with probability 1.

The coin. With values ±1: μ=0, Var=1. With ±100: μ=0, Var=1002=10,000, σ=100. Same probabilities, wider spread.

HW 2: Var(aX+b) (also last year's HW 3 Q12). E[aX+b]=aμ+b, so

Var(aX+b)=E[(aX+b−aμ−b)2]=E[a2(X−μ)2]=a2Var(X)​,σaX+b​=∣a∣σX​.

Shifting by b moves the distribution without changing its spread. Scaling by a scales the spread by ∣a∣.

Slide 23/31: The indicator random variable

On the slide. 1A​(ω)=1 if ω∈A⊆Ω, else 0. Its PMF: P(A) at x=1, 1−P(A) at x=0. It is discrete even if Ω is continuous; for example A = "the number picked uniformly on the real line is positive". What are its CDF and mean E[1A​]? Its variance and moments?

With q=P(A):

F(x)=⎩⎨⎧​01−q1​x<00≤x<1x≥1​,E[1A​]=q=P(A),E[1An​]=q,Var(1A​)=q(1−q).

The moments are all q because 1An​=1A​ (as 0n=0 and 1n=1). Then Var=E[1A2​]−q2=q−q2.

E[1A​]=P(A)​

This tiny fact is one of the most useful in the course: any probability is an expectation, and a count of events is a sum of indicators, whose mean is the sum of the probabilities (by linearity, proved for sums later). Part B uses it for matching and coupon problems.

Slide 24/31: Bernoulli random variable

On the slide. X=1 with probability p, else 0. Same as an indicator, but we do not specify A. From now on we start ignoring Ω. Used in binary classification (X=1 if the image has a cat) and Bernoulli bandits. E[X]=p, E[Xn]=p.

X∼Bernoulli(p):pX​(1)=p, pX​(0)=1−p,E[X]=p,Var(X)=p(1−p).

The variance is largest at p=21​ (most uncertain) and zero at p∈{0,1} (no randomness).

Slide 25/31: Binomial B(n,p)

On the slide. Toss a p-coin n times; heads = 1, tails = 0; N = number of heads. PMF: pN​(k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k. HW: what are E[N], E[N2], Var(N)?

pN​(k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k, k=0,…,n;E[N]=np,Var(N)=np(1−p)​

Consistency. ∑k​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k=(p+1−p)n=1 by the binomial theorem.

HW: E[N] (this is last year's Quiz 1 Q2). Use k(kn​)=n(k−1n−1​) (Lecture 3, slide 58):

E[N]=k=1∑n​k(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k=npk=1∑n​(k−1n−1​)pk−1(1−p)(n−1)−(k−1)=np(p+1−p)n−1=np.

HW: E[N2]. Compute the factorial moment E[N(N−1)] first, because k(k−1)(kn​)=n(n−1)(k−2n−2​):

E[N(N−1)]=n(n−1)p2k=2∑n​(k−2n−2​)pk−2(1−p)(n−2)−(k−2)=n(n−1)p2.

So E[N2]=E[N(N−1)]+E[N]=n(n−1)p2+np.

HW: Var(N). n(n−1)p2+np−n2p2=np−np2=np(1−p). (The slide writes Var(X); it means Var(N).)

The quick way (once independence of random variables is covered): N=X1​+⋯+Xn​ with i.i.d. Bernoulli(p) Xi​. Means add, and for independent variables variances add: np and np(1−p). Last year's Quiz 1 solutions show both methods.

Slide 26/31: Geometric random variable

On the slide. Toss a p-coin until the first head; N = number of tosses needed. PMF? pN​(k)=(1−p)k−1p. HW: E[N], E[N2], Var(N)? What is FˉN​(k):=1−FN​(k)=P(N>k)? What is P(N>k+m∣N>k)? (=P(N>m), the memoryless property.)

pN​(k)=(1−p)k−1p, k=1,2,…;E[N]=p1​,Var(N)=p21−p​,P(N>k)=(1−p)k​

Write q=1−p. Consistency: ∑k≥1​qk−1p=1−qp​=1.

Tail. N>k iff the first k tosses are all tails: P(N>k)=qk. No sum needed.

HW: E[N]. Differentiate the geometric series ∑k≥0​xk=1−x1​ to get ∑k≥1​kxk−1=(1−x)21​ for ∣x∣<1:

E[N]=k≥1∑​kqk−1p=(1−q)2p​=p1​.

(Or by the tail-sum formula: E[N]=∑k≥0​qk=p1​.)

HW: E[N2]. Differentiate again: ∑k≥2​k(k−1)xk−2=(1−x)32​. So

E[N(N−1)]=k∑​k(k−1)qk−1p=pq⋅(1−q)32​=p22q​,E[N2]=p22q​+p1​=p22−p​,
Var(N)=p22−p​−p21​=p21−p​.

Example: waiting for a 6 on a die (p=61​): mean 6 rolls, variance 30, standard deviation ≈5.5. The waiting time is very spread out.

Memorylessness. For integers k,m≥0, {N>k+m}⊆{N>k}, so

P(N>k+m∣N>k)=P(N>k)P(N>k+m)​=qkqk+m​=qm=P(N>m).

Having already waited k tosses without a head, the remaining wait has the same distribution as a fresh start. The coin has no memory. The geometric is the only memoryless distribution on {1,2,…}, and the exponential (Lecture 7) is its continuous counterpart.

Slide 27/31: Poisson random variable

On the slide. Parameter λ, Ω′=Z≥0​, PMF pX​(k)=e−λk!λk​. Intuitively, it is the limit of the binomial as n grows and p shrinks with np→λ. The binomial mean is np, so p must decrease as n increases. Read the Wikipedia page on the Poisson limit theorem. More with Poisson processes.

pX​(k)=e−λk!λk​, k=0,1,2,…;E[X]=λ,Var(X)=λ​

Consistency, mean and variance (last year's Tutorial 3 Q1). Using ∑k≥0​k!λk​=eλ:

k∑​pX​(k)=e−λeλ=1,E[X]=k≥1∑​ke−λk!λk​=λe−λk≥1∑​(k−1)!λk−1​=λ.

For the variance use the factorial moment again:

E[X(X−1)]=λ2e−λk≥2∑​(k−2)!λk−2​=λ2,Var(X)=λ2+λ−λ2=λ.

The Poisson limit theorem. Let Nn​∼B(n,pn​) with npn​=λ. For fixed k:

(kn​)pnk​(1−pn​)n−k=→1nkn(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)​​​⋅k!λk​⋅→e−λ(1−nλ​)n​​⋅→1(1−nλ​)−k​​ ⟶ e−λk!λk​.

The first factor is a product of k terms nn−i​→1 (k is fixed). The third uses (1+nx​)n→ex.

When to use it. Counts of many independent rare events: typos on a page, calls per minute, defects in a batch, decays per second. If n is large and p small, B(n,p)≈Poisson(np). Example: n=100, p=0.01. Exact P(0)=0.99100=0.3660; Poisson e−1=0.3679. Exact P(2)=0.1849; Poisson 2e−1​=0.1839. Very close.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's HW 3 Q5). X∼ Geometric(41​) and Y=∣X−4∣. Find the range and PMF of Y.

Solution

Solution. X∈{1,2,…}, so Y∈{0,1,2,…}. Use slide 19 with pX​(k)=41​(43​)k−1:

  • y=0: only x=4: pY​(0)=41​(43​)3=25627​.
  • y=1,2,3: x=4+y and x=4−y≥1: pY​(y)=41​[(43​)3+y+(43​)3−y].
  • y≥4: only x=4+y (since 4−y≤0): pY​(y)=41​(43​)3+y.

B2 (last year's HW 3 Q2). A batch of 20 chips: 5 are flawless for sure, and each of the other 15 is defective independently with probability 0.1. Z = number of flawless chips. (a) PMF of Z. (b) P(Z≥18).

Solution

Solution. Z=5+W with W∼B(15,0.9). (a) pZ​(k)=(k−515​)0.9k−50.120−k for k=5,…,20. (b) P(W≥13)=(1315​)0.9130.12+(1415​)0.9140.1+0.915≈0.816​.

B3 (last year's HW 3 Q11, the St. Petersburg game). The pot starts at 1 and doubles on each head; at the first tail you win the pot, so you win 2k−1 if the first tail is on toss k. (a) E[X]. (b) P(X>65). (c) The payout is capped at 230; find E[Y].

Solution

Solution. P(first tail on toss k)=2−k. (a) E[X]=∑k≥1​2k−12−k=∑k​21​=∞. (b) X>65 iff X≥128=27 iff k≥8: P=∑k≥8​2−k=2−7=1281​. (c) For k≤31 the payout 2k−1≤230 is uncapped and contributes 21​ each: 231​. For k≥32 the payout is 230, with total probability 2−31: contributes 21​. So E[Y]=16. A billion-rupee cap makes the "infinitely valuable" game worth 16 rupees.

B4 (last year's HW 3 Q13). An urn has 10 black and 1 white ball, drawn without replacement until the white appears. (a) You win $5 per black ball drawn. Expected winnings? (b) The payout is 2k for k black balls. Expected payout?

Solution

Solution. The white ball's position is uniform on {1,…,11} (every order is equally likely), so K = number of blacks before it is uniform on {0,…,10}. (a) E[5K]=5⋅5=25​. (b) LOTUS: E[2K]=111​∑k=010​2k=11211−1​=112047​≈186.09​. Note E[2K]=2E[K]=32.

B5 (last year's HW 3 Q3, the umbrella or matching problem). N people pick umbrellas at random. (a) P(at least one gets their own). (b) Its limit. (c) The PMF of XN​, the number of matches.

Solution

Solution. (a) Ai​ = person i gets their own. Any k specified people all match with probability N!(N−k)!​. Inclusion–exclusion:
