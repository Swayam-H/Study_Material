Lecture 3, part 1

Continuity of P, conditional probability and Bayes' rule

Deck 1, slides 36 to 46⏱ 9 min read

Big picture. First, a technical result: P is continuous along increasing or decreasing sequences of events. It is the bridge between finite reasoning and limits, and it is used again in the CDF properties (L7) and in convergence (L16 to L18). Then the most used ideas of the whole course: conditional probability (updating on information), the multiplication (chain) rule, the law of total probability (divide and conquer), and Bayes' rule (reasoning backwards from effect to cause).

Part A: the slides

Slide 37/73: Sequences of sets

On the slide. If A1​⊂A2​⊂… are events and ⋃n​An​=A∈F, we say An​ increases to A, written An​↑A. If A1​⊃A2​⊃… and ⋂n​An​=A, then An​↓A. Notation: limn​An​ means ⋃n​An​ for increasing and ⋂n​An​ for decreasing sequences.

For a monotone sequence the "limit" is the obvious one: an increasing sequence grows to its union, a decreasing one shrinks to its intersection. The examples from Lecture 1, slide 14: [0,1−n1​]↑[0,1) and [0,n1​]↓{0}.

Slide 38/73: Continuity of P

On the slide. Lemma. If An​↑A or An​↓A, then limn→∞​P(An​)=P(A). Proof (increasing case). Define Fn​=An​−An−1​. Then ⋃n​An​=⋃n​Fn​, so P(A)=P(⋃n​Fn​)=limn​∑i=1n​P(Fi​)=limn​P(An​). Equivalently, if An​↓∅ then P(An​)→0.

An​↑A  or  An​↓A⟹P(An​)→P(A)​

The proof in full (increasing case). Set A0​=∅ and Fn​=An​∖An−1​, the "new part" added at step n.

  1. The Fn​ are disjoint: a point belongs only to the Fn​ for the first An​ that contains it.
  2. An​=F1​∪⋯∪Fn​ (disjoint), so P(An​)=∑i=1n​P(Fi​).
  3. ⋃n​An​=⋃n​Fn​, so by countable additivity
P(A)=i=1∑∞​P(Fi​)=n→∞lim​i=1∑n​P(Fi​)=n→∞lim​P(An​).

Decreasing case. If An​↓A then Anc​↑Ac (De Morgan). By the increasing case, 1−P(An​)=P(Anc​)→P(Ac)=1−P(A).

Why it is useful. It lets you compute the probability of a limiting event as a limit of easy probabilities.

  • Never getting a head. Let An​ = "first n tosses are tails". Then An​↓ "all tosses are tails", and P(An​)=(1−p)n→0. So P(no head ever)=0.
  • Uniform on [0,1]: P([0,n1​])=n1​→0=P({0}), which agrees with "a point has probability zero".

Slide 39/73: Conditional probability

On the slide. If the die rolls odd, what is the probability that it shows 1? If ωˉ∈[0,0.5], what is the probability that ωˉ∈[0,0.25]? Definition: P(B∣A):=P(A)P(A∩B)​ when P(A)>0.

P(B∣A)=P(A)P(A∩B)​,P(A)>0​

The two questions.

P({1}∣odd)=P({1,3,5})P({1})​=1/21/6​=31​,P([0,0.25]∣[0,0.5])=0.50.25​=21​.

How to think about it. Knowing that A happened shrinks the sample space to A. Outcomes outside A are ruled out, and the probabilities of outcomes inside A are rescaled by 1/P(A) so they add to 1. The relative odds of outcomes inside A do not change.

P(⋅∣A) is itself a probability measure (on the same F). It is non-negative, P(Ω∣A)=1, and it is countably additive. So every rule from Lecture 2 holds conditionally:

P(Bc∣A)=1−P(B∣A),P(B∪C∣A)=P(B∣A)+P(C∣A)−P(BC∣A).

A common mistake is to "complement the condition": P(B∣Ac) is not 1−P(B∣A).

Slide 40/73: Bayes, conditioning on two events, the chain rule

On the slide. Show P(A∣B)P(B)=P(B∣A)P(A). Bayes: P(B∣A)=P(A)P(A∣B)P(B)​. What is P(A∣B∩C)? (also written P(A∣BC)). Prove P(A∩B∩C)=P(A)P(B∣A)P(C∣AB). HW: prove P(A1​∩⋯∩An​)=P(A1​)P(A2​∣A1​)P(A3​∣A1​A2​)⋯P(An​∣A1​⋯An−1​).

The symmetric identity. Both sides equal P(A∩B), straight from the definition. Dividing by P(A) gives Bayes' rule.

Chain rule for three events. Write each factor as a ratio and watch them telescope:

P(A)⋅P(A)P(AB)​⋅P(AB)P(ABC)​=P(ABC).

HW: chain rule for n events (also last year's HW 2 Q8). Assume P(A1​⋯An−1​)>0 (so every conditioning event has positive probability, since A1​⋯Ak​⊇A1​⋯An−1​). Then

P(A1​)k=2∏n​P(Ak​∣A1​⋯Ak−1​)=P(A1​)k=2∏n​P(A1​⋯Ak−1​)P(A1​⋯Ak​)​=P(A1​⋯An​),

since every numerator cancels the next denominator. (Formally, induction on n: P(A1​⋯An​)=P(A1​⋯An−1​)P(An​∣A1​⋯An−1​).) □

When to use it. Whenever the experiment happens in stages (draw, then draw again; toss, then toss again). You multiply along a path of the tree diagram.

Slide 41/73: Conditional probability examples

On the slide. Draw 4 cards without replacement. Probability that, in order, they are 9♣, 8♦, K♠, K♣? What if with replacement? For a finite Ω with equally likely outcomes, P(B∣A)=∣A∣∣A∩B∣​.

Without replacement. Chain rule: the first card is right with probability 521​; given that, the second with 511​, and so on:

P=521​⋅511​⋅501​⋅491​=6,497,4001​≈1.54×10−7.

With replacement. Each draw is from the full deck: P=(521​)4=7,311,6161​. Slightly smaller, since a card already drawn can come back and spoil the sequence.

Equally likely outcomes. P(B∣A)=∣A∣/∣Ω∣∣A∩B∣/∣Ω∣​=∣A∣∣A∩B∣​. Conditioning on A is counting inside A. That is how slide 39's die answer 31​ comes out: one favourable outcome among three.

Slide 42/73: Law of total probability

On the slide. A=(A∩B)∪(A∩Bc), so P(A)=P(A∩B)+P(A∩Bc)=P(A∣B)P(B)+P(A∣Bc)P(Bc). Useful when P(A) is hard but P(B) and P(A∣B) are easy. In general, for a partition B1​,…,Bn​ of Ω: P(A)=∑i​P(A∩Bi​)=∑i​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​).

P(A)=i∑​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​)(B1​,B2​,… a partition of Ω)​

Why it is true. The A∩Bi​ are disjoint and their union is A (the Bi​ cover Ω). Additivity and the multiplication rule P(A∩Bi​)=P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​) do the rest. It also holds for countably many Bi​. You may drop any Bi​ with P(Bi​)=0.

How to use it. Choose the partition to be the thing you wish you knew: which bag, which coin, whether it rains. Conditional on it, the problem becomes easy.

Slide 43/73: Example 1, three bags of marbles

On the slide. 3 bags with M marbles each; bag i has Ri​ red and Bi​ blue. Choose a bag at random, then draw a marble. P(Red)=∑i​P(Red∣Bag i)P(Bag i).

Each bag is chosen with probability 31​ and P(Red∣Bag i)=MRi​​:

P(Red)=31​(MR1​​+MR2​​+MR3​​)=3MR1​+R2​+R3​​.

Because all bags have the same size, this equals "pick one of the 3M marbles uniformly". With unequal bag sizes it would not.

Slide 44/73: Example 2, the defect-spotting robot

On the slide. If an item is defective, a robot spots it with 98% accuracy. If not defective, the robot declares so with 99% accuracy. 0.1% of items are defective. If the robot says the item is defective, what is the probability that it is correct? P(def∣says def)=P(says def)P(says def∣def)P(def)​. What is P(says def)?

Let D = defective and S = robot says defective. Given: P(D)=0.001, P(S∣D)=0.98, P(Sc∣Dc)=0.99, so P(S∣Dc)=0.01.

Total probability for the denominator:

P(S)=P(S∣D)P(D)+P(S∣Dc)P(Dc)=0.98(0.001)+0.01(0.999)=0.00098+0.00999=0.01097.

Bayes:

P(D∣S)=0.010970.00098​≈0.089​.

Only about 9% of flagged items are really defective, despite a "98% accurate" robot.

Why so low. Picture 100,000 items: 100 defective, of which 98 are flagged. Among the 99,900 good ones, 1% (999) are wrongly flagged. Of the 98+999=1097 flags, only 98 are real. When the condition is rare, false positives from the huge "good" group swamp the true positives. This is the base-rate fallacy. The same structure appears in last year's HW 2 Q9 (alarm) and Tutorial 2 Q3 (disease test), both in Part B.

Slide 45/73: Bayes' rule revisited

On the slide. For a partition B1​,…,Bn​ and P(A)>0: P(Bj​∣A)=∑i=1n​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​)P(A∣Bj​)P(Bj​)​. In the marble example, given that the marble is red, what is the probability that bag 1 was chosen?

P(Bj​∣A)=∑i​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​)P(A∣Bj​)P(Bj​)​​

Vocabulary. P(Bj​) is the prior (belief before the evidence), P(A∣Bj​) the likelihood (how well cause j explains the evidence), and P(Bj​∣A) the posterior. The posterior is proportional to prior × likelihood, and the denominator just normalises.

Marble question.

P(Bag 1∣Red)=3MR1​+R2​+R3​​MR1​​⋅31​​=R1​+R2​+R3​R1​​.

Intuition: each red marble is equally likely to be the one drawn (equal bag sizes), and R1​ of them are in bag 1.

Slide 46/73: Recap

On the slide. Probability space (Ω,F,P); axioms; σ-algebras as domain; Borel σ-algebras; conditional probability; total probability and Bayes.

  • (Ω,F,P): outcomes, events you may ask about, and a countably additive measure with total mass 1.
  • σ-algebra: contains Ω, closed under complements and countable unions.
  • Borel: generated by intervals; contains every set you will meet.
  • P(B∣A)=P(AB)/P(A); chain rule; total probability; Bayes = prior × likelihood, normalised.
