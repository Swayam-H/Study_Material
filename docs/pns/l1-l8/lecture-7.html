Lecture 7

The CDF, continuous random variables, uniform and exponential

Deck 3, slides 1 to 11⏱ 17 min read✓ 11 practice problems
Solutions stay hidden until you click them.

Big picture. The CDF FX​(x)=P(X≤x) works for every random variable, discrete or continuous. For a continuous random variable the CDF has a derivative, the pdf fX​, and probabilities become areas under fX​. Single points carry no probability, so < and ≤ no longer matter. Sums become integrals: E[X]=∫xfX​(x)dx. Two families are introduced: the uniform (the continuous "equally likely") and the exponential (waiting times, and the only memoryless continuous distribution).

Part A: the slides

Slide 2/26: The induced measure PX​ and the CDF

On the slide. X maps (Ω,F,P) to (R,B(R),PX​). The CDF is FX​(x)=P{ω:X(ω)≤x}. It can be written with PX​: B(R) is made up of sets like (−∞,a], and FX​(x)=PX​((−∞,x])=P{ω:X(ω)≤x}. This is a general definition, for continuous and discrete alike.

FX​(x)=P(X≤x)=PX​((−∞,x]),x∈R​

Why one function is enough. Half-lines (−∞,x] generate B(R) (Lecture 2, Part B5). So knowing PX​ on half-lines (the CDF) determines PX​ on every Borel set. Two random variables with the same CDF have the same distribution.

The four properties of every CDF (not on the slide, but used everywhere; Bertsekas Section 3.2):

  1. Non-decreasing: x<y⇒FX​(x)≤FX​(y), because {X≤x}⊆{X≤y} (monotonicity of P).
  2. limx→−∞​FX​(x)=0, since {X≤−n}↓∅ (continuity of P, Lecture 3).
  3. limx→∞​FX​(x)=1, since {X≤n}↑Ω.
  4. Right-continuous: FX​(x+n1​)→FX​(x), since {X≤x+n1​}↓{X≤x}.

The left limit is FX​(x−)=P(X<x) (from {X≤x−n1​}↑{X<x}). So the jump at x is P(X=x):

P(X=x)=FX​(x)−FX​(x−).

Interval probabilities: P(a<X≤b)=FX​(b)−FX​(a). Any function with properties 1 to 4 is the CDF of some random variable.

Slide 3/26: Continuous random variables

On the slide. A random variable on R is discrete if FX​ is piecewise constant, continuous if FX​ is a continuous function. A continuous function need not be differentiable, but in this course we only consider continuous random variables whose FX​ is continuous and differentiable. Examples: a number picked uniformly from [a,b]; the water level in a dam; the pending workload on a server. (Handwritten: P(X∈(x,x+h])=∫xx+h​f(x)dx≈f(x)h, with a sketch of a thin strip of width h under a density curve.)

Discrete vs continuous, in terms of the CDF. Discrete: a staircase (jumps at the values, flat between). Continuous: no jumps at all, so P(X=x)=0 for every x.

There is a third kind. A CDF can have both jumps and continuous increase: a mixed random variable. Example (last year's Tutorial 4 Q7): your waiting time is exactly 0 if a taxi is waiting (probability 21​), otherwise it is continuous. Part B8 works it out. The CDF handles all three kinds; PMFs and pdfs each handle only one.

The handwritten note is the key intuition for a pdf: the probability of a short interval is approximately height × width:

P(x<X≤x+h)≈fX​(x)h(h small).

Slide 4/26: The probability density function

On the slide. A continuous random variable has a pdf fX​(x), x∈R, with units of probability per unit length: fX​(x):=limΔ→0+​ΔP(x<X≤x+Δ)​=limΔ→0+​ΔFX​(x+Δ)−FX​(x)​=dxdFX​(x)​ (if the derivative exists). Equivalently FX​(x)=∫−∞x​fX​(u)du.

fX​(x)=dxd​FX​(x),FX​(x)=∫−∞x​fX​(u)du​

"Density" is the right word. Like mass density (kg per metre), fX​ is probability per unit length. So fX​(x) can exceed 1: U[0,21​] has f=2 on its support. What must be at most 1 is the area over any set. And fX​(x) is not P(X=x), which is 0.

The two directions are the fundamental theorem of calculus: differentiate the CDF to get the pdf; integrate the pdf to get the CDF. At points where FX​ has a kink (as at the ends of a uniform), the pdf can be defined either way; changing f at finitely many points changes no probability.

Slide 5/26: Properties of the pdf

On the slide. PX​(R)=∫−∞∞​fX​(u)du=1. PX​([a,b])=FX​(b)−FX​(a)=∫ab​fX​(u)du (area under the curve). In general PX​(B)=∫u∈B​fX​(u)du. PX​({a})=0 (no mass at any point). PX​([a,b])=PX​((a,b))=PX​([a,b))=PX​((a,b]). (Handwritten: "pmf vs pdf"; the four intervals underlined.)

pmf vs pdf (the handwritten comparison):

Discrete (PMF pX​) Continuous (pdf fX​)
Meaning pX​(x)=P(X=x) fX​(x)dx≈P(x<X≤x+dx)
Range of values 0≤pX​≤1 fX​≥0, may exceed 1
Normalisation ∑x​pX​(x)=1 ∫fX​(x)dx=1
P(X∈B) ∑x∈B​pX​(x) ∫B​fX​(x)dx
P(X=a) pX​(a) 0
Endpoints matter do not matter

Why P(X=a)=0. {a}⊆(a−Δ,a], so P(X=a)≤FX​(a)−FX​(a−Δ)→0 as Δ→0, by continuity of FX​. Hence adding or removing endpoints changes nothing, and the four interval probabilities on the slide are equal. (For a discrete or mixed variable they can differ.)

Slide 6/26: Mean, variance, moments; linear transformations

On the slide. E[X]=∫−∞∞​ufX​(u)du; E[Xn]=∫unfX​(u)du; E[g(X)]=∫g(u)fX​(u)du; Var(X)=E[g(X)] with g(x)=(x−E[X])2. For Y=aX+b: E[Y]=aE[X]+b; FY​(y)=FX​(ay−b​) when a>0; FY​(y)=1−FX​(ay−b​) when a<0. (Handwritten: for a<0, dyd​(1−FX​(ay−b​))=−a1​fX​(ay−b​); for a>0, fY​(y)=fX​(ay−b​)a1​; a sketch of a CDF rising from 0 to 1.)

E[g(X)]=∫−∞∞​g(x)fX​(x)dx,Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2​

Every discrete formula carries over with ∑→∫ and pX​→fX​: LOTUS, linearity, Var(aX+b)=a2Var(X), and the shortcut formula for the variance. The proofs are the same (last year's HW 4 Q6 asks for LOTUS; see Part B9).

The CDF of Y=aX+b, both cases.

  • a>0: FY​(y)=P(aX+b≤y)=P(X≤ay−b​)=FX​(ay−b​).
  • a<0: dividing by a negative number flips the inequality: P(X≥ay−b​)=1−FX​(ay−b​). (For continuous X, ≥ vs > does not matter.)

Differentiating (the handwritten part) with the chain rule, dyd​ay−b​=a1​:

a>0: fY​(y)=a1​fX​(ay−b​),a<0: fY​(y)=−a1​fX​(ay−b​).

Both are fY​(y)=∣a∣1​fX​(ay−b​)​, since −a1​=∣a∣1​ when a<0. The ∣a∣1​ makes sense: stretching by ∣a∣ spreads the probability over a region ∣a∣ times longer, so the density drops by that factor. Lecture 8 (slides 17 to 22) generalises this to any monotone g.

Slide 7/26: Uniform random variable U[a,b]

On the slide. Real-valued. pdf fX​(x)=b−a1​ for x∈[a,b]. CDF: 0 for x<a, b−ax−a​ for x∈[a,b], 1 otherwise. HW: verify E[X]=2a+b​ and Var(X)=12(b−a)2​. (Handwritten: ∫ab​uf(u)du and ∫ab​u2du.)

X∼U[a,b]: fX​(x)=b−a1​ on [a,b],FX​(x)=b−ax−a​ on [a,b],E[X]=2a+b​,Var(X)=12(b−a)2​​

HW.

E[X]=∫ab​b−au​du=2(b−a)b2−a2​=2a+b​,E[X2]=∫ab​b−au2​du=3(b−a)b3−a3​=3a2+ab+b2​.
Var(X)=3a2+ab+b2​−4(a+b)2​=124a2+4ab+4b2−3a2−6ab−3b2​=12(b−a)2​.

Shortcut: X=a+(b−a)U with U∼U[0,1], which has E[U]=21​, E[U2]=31​, Var(U)=121​. Then Var(X)=(b−a)2⋅121​ by slide 6.

Probabilities are proportional to length: P(X∈[c,d])=b−ad−c​ for [c,d]⊆[a,b]. That is "probability = length" from Lecture 1, rescaled.

Slide 8/26: Pictures of U[a,b]

On the slide. The CDF (0, then a straight ramp from a to b, then 1) and the pdf (a flat box of height b−a1​). (Handwritten: U[0,1]; a coin "H 21​, T 21​" drawn as [0,1] split at 21​; and a die: [0,1] cut at 61​,62​,…,65​ with the pieces labelled 1 to 6.)

The handwritten idea: simulate anything discrete from one uniform. Given U∼U[0,1]:

  • Coin: say "H" if U<21​, else "T". P(H)=P(U<21​)=21​.
  • Die: report k if 6k−1​≤U<6k​. Each piece has length 61​, so each face has probability 61​. Compactly, X=⌈6U⌉ (ignoring the probability-0 event U=0).
  • Any PMF p1​,p2​,…: cut [0,1] into consecutive pieces of lengths p1​,p2​,… and report the piece U falls in.

This is the inverse transform method, taught properly in this year's L14 and L15 (and used in last year's Surprise Quiz Q3 to simulate a Markov chain).

Slide 9/26: Exponential random variable Exp(λ)

On the slide. Non-negative, parameter λ. pdf fX​(x)=λe−λx for x≥0. CDF FX​(x)=1−e−λx for x≥0. E[X]=λ1​, Var(X)=λ21​. E[Xn]=λnn!​.

X∼Exp(λ): fX​(x)=λe−λx, FX​(x)=1−e−λx, P(X>x)=e−λx (x≥0);E[X]=λ1​, Var(X)=λ21​​

CDF. ∫0x​λe−λudu=[−e−λu]0x​=1−e−λx. Remember the tail P(X>x)=e−λx: it is what you use most.

Moments (the red claims; last year's Quiz 1 Q4 asks for the mean). Integrate by parts with u=xn, dv=λe−λxdx (v=−e−λx):

E[Xn]=[−xne−λx]0∞​+∫0∞​nxn−1e−λxdx=0+λn​∫0∞​xn−1λe−λxdx=λn​E[Xn−1].

Starting from E[X0]=1: E[X]=λ1​, E[X2]=λ22​, and by induction E[Xn]=λnn!​. So Var(X)=λ22​−λ21​=λ21​.

Slide 11/26: Significance of the exponential; memorylessness

On the slide. Building block for continuous-time Markov chains. Memoryless property: P(X>a+h∣X>a)=e−λae−λ(a+h)​=e−λh=P(X>h). Used extensively in queueing theory for inter-arrival and service times. (Handwritten: P(X>a); ∫ℓℓ+h​fX​(x)dx=P(X∈(ℓ,ℓ+h])≈fX​(ℓ)h; "e−λh is independent of a"; "B happens after A"; "P(X=ℓ1​)=0 if ℓ1​ has point mass".)

P(X>a+h∣X>a)=P(X>h)for all a,h≥0​

Proof (last year's Quiz 1 Q4). {X>a+h}⊆{X>a} ("B happens after A": waiting past a+h requires waiting past a), so the intersection is just {X>a+h}:

P(X>a+h∣X>a)=P(X>a)P(X>a+h)​=e−λae−λ(a+h)​=e−λh=P(X>h).□

The answer does not depend on a (the circled annotation). A light bulb with an exponential lifetime that has already burnt for 1000 hours is "as good as new".

The handwritten point-mass remark. For a continuous X, P(X=ℓ)=0. If the distribution has a point mass at ℓ1​ (a mixed variable), then P(X=ℓ1​)=0 and endpoints matter again.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's HW 4 Q2; geometric to exponential). Y∼ Geometric(p) with p=λh, and X=Yh. Show FX​(x)→1−e−λx as h↓0.

Solution

Solution. Y counts time slots of length h until the first success, so X is a waiting time. For x>0,

P(X>x)=P(Y>hx​)=(1−λh)⌊x/h⌋.

Take logs: ⌊x/h⌋ln(1−λh). As h→0, ⌊x/h⌋≈hx​ and ln(1−λh)≈−λh, so the product →−λx. Hence P(X>x)→e−λx and FX​(x)→1−e−λx. Tossing a coin with success probability λh every h seconds becomes an exponential clock with rate λ.

B2 (last year's HW 4 Q7; Poisson counts give exponential gaps). Arrivals in [0,t] are Poisson(λt). Find the distribution of X, the first arrival time.

Solution

Solution. X>t iff there is no arrival in [0,t]: P(X>t)=e−λt0!(λt)0​=e−λt. So X∼Exp(λ). This links slide 27 of Deck 2 to slide 11 here: in a Poisson process the gaps are exponential.

B3 (last year's HW 4 Q5; the Laplace distribution). fX​(x)=2b1​e−∣x−μ∣/b. Show it is a pdf; find E[X] and Var(X).

Solution

Solution. Substitute y=x−μ: the density of Y is symmetric, two half-exponentials with rate b1​ glued at 0. ∫=2⋅2b1​∫0∞​e−y/bdy=1. ✓ By symmetry E[Y]=0, so E[X]=μ. Var(X)=E[Y2]=2⋅2b1​∫0∞​y2e−y/bdy=b1​⋅2b3=2b2 (using ∫0∞​y2e−y/bdy=(1/b)32!​).

B4 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q1; Gamma/Erlang). fX​(x)=cxn−1e−λx for x>0. Find c, E[X], Var(X), and a guess for a sum of i.i.d. variables with this distribution.

Solution

Solution. ∫0∞​xn−1e−λxdx=λn(n−1)!​, so c=(n−1)!λn​. Then E[X]=cλn+1n!​=λn​ and E[X2]=cλn+2(n+1)!​=λ2n(n+1)​, so Var(X)=λ2n​. Mean and variance are n times those of Exp(λ), which suggests X=X1​+⋯+Xn​ with i.i.d. Xi​∼Exp(λ). That is true (proved with MGFs in L14): the time of the n-th arrival.

B5 (last year's HW 4 Q11; Rayleigh). f(x)=xe−x2/2 for x>0. Find E[X] and E[X2].

Solution

Solution. E[X2]=∫0∞​x3e−x2/2dx. Substitute t=x2/2 (dt=xdx): ∫0∞​2te−tdt=2. For E[X]=∫0∞​x2e−x2/2dx, note this is half of ∫−∞∞​x2e−x2/2dx=2π​ (the variance of a standard normal times 2π​, Lecture 8). So E[X]=π/2​≈1.2533.

B6 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q2; rounding an exponential). X∼Exp(1) and N=⌈X⌉. Find the PMF and E[N] in two ways.

Solution

Solution. P(N=n)=P(n−1<X≤n)=e−(n−1)−e−n=(e−1)n−1(1−e−1), so N∼ Geometric(1−e−1) and E[N]=1−e−11​=e−1e​≈1.582. Tail-sum check: P(N>n)=P(X>n)=e−n, and ∑n≥0​e−n=1−e−11​. ✓ Rounding up an exponential gives a geometric: the discrete and continuous memoryless laws match.

B7 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q3; tail-integral formula). For X≥0 and p>0, show E[Xp]=∫0∞​psp−1P(X≥s)ds.

Solution

Solution. Write xp=∫0x​psp−1ds=∫0∞​psp−11{s≤x}ds. Then swap the order of integration (allowed since everything is non-negative):

E[Xp]=∫0∞​(∫0∞​psp−11{s≤x}ds)fX​(x)dx=∫0∞​psp−1P(X≥s)∫s∞​fX​(x)dx​​ds.

With p=1: E[X]=∫0∞​P(X>s)ds, the continuous tail-sum formula. For Exp(λ): ∫0∞​e−λsds=λ1​. ✓

B8 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q7; a mixed random variable). With probability 21​ a taxi is waiting (X=0). Otherwise the next taxi comes at T∼U[0,8] and a bus comes at exactly 5; you take the first. Find FX​, the point masses and E[X].

Solution

Solution. Without a waiting taxi, X=min(T,5). For 0≤x<5: FX​(x)=21​+21​⋅8x​. At x=5 the CDF jumps to 1, because min(T,5)=5 whenever T≥5: P(X=5)=21​⋅83​=163​. So

FX​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​021​+16x​1​x<00≤x<5x≥5​,point masses P(X=0)=21​, P(X=5)=163​,

and density 161​ on (0,5). Check: 21​+165​+163​=1. ✓ Mean: add the discrete and continuous parts:

E[X]=0⋅21​+5⋅163​+∫05​16x​dx=1615​+3225​=3255​≈1.72 minutes.

B9 (last year's HW 4 Q6; LOTUS for continuous X). Show E[g(X)]=∫g(x)fX​(x)dx, at least for g≥0.

Solution

Solution. Use the continuous tail formula (B7 with p=1) for the non-negative random variable Y=g(X), then swap integrals:

E[Y]=∫0∞​P(g(X)>y)dy=∫0∞​∫{x:g(x)>y}​fX​(x)dxdy=∫fX​(x)∫0g(x)​dydx=∫g(x)fX​(x)dx.

For general g, split g=g+−g− into its positive and negative parts.

B10 (last year's Midsem A Q3; minimum of exponentials). X,Y,Z independent, Exp(λ1​), Exp(λ2​), Exp(λ3​). Find the distribution of W=min(X,Y,Z).

Solution

Solution. The minimum exceeds w iff all three do. For w≥0:

P(W>w)=P(X>w)P(Y>w)P(Z>w)=e−(λ1​+λ2​+λ3​)w.

So W∼Exp(λ1​+λ2​+λ3​), with fW​(w)=(λ1​+λ2​+λ3​)e−(λ1​+λ2​+λ3​)w. Competing exponential clocks: rates add. (For the maximum use P(max≤w)=∏P(Xi​≤w) instead; see B11.)

B11 (last year's HW 4 Q9; the maximum). Daily rainfall X1​,X2​,X3​ i.i.d. Exp(λ). (a) P(at least one day exceeds 1/λ). (b) CDF, pdf and mean of M=maxXi​. (c) E[N], N = number of days above 1/λ.

Solution

Solution. (a) P(Xi​>λ1​)=e−1, so 1−(1−e−1)3≈0.747. (b) FM​(m)=(1−e−λm)3 and fM​(m)=3λe−λm(1−e−λm)2 for m≥0. Using the tail integral, E[M]=∫0∞​[1−(1−e−λm)3]dm=∫0∞​(3e−λm−3e−2λm+e−3λm)dm=λ1​(3−23​+31​)=6λ11​. (c) N∼B(3,e−1), so E[N]=e3​≈1.10.
