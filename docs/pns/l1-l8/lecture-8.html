Lecture 8

The Gaussian, and functions of a continuous random variable

Deck 3, slides 12 to 23 · 15 min read · 7 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. The Gaussian (normal) distribution is the most important in the course: it is what sums of many small independent effects look like (the CLT, L18). Its CDF has no closed form, so we standardise to N(0,1) and use the table of Φ. The second half answers: if Y=g(X), what is the pdf of Y? For monotone g there is a formula, fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣h′(y)∣ with h=g−1. For anything else, go through the CDF.

Part A: the slides

Slide 12/26: The Gaussian random variable N(μ,σ2)

On the slide. Real-valued with two parameters μ, σ. pdf fX​(x)=2πσ2​1​e−21​(σx−μ​)2 for x∈R. Verify: ∫fX​=1, E[X]=μ, Var(X)=σ2. Plots of pdfs and CDFs for (μ,σ2)=(0,0.2),(0,1),(0,5),(−2,0.5). (Handwritten: E[X2]−E[X]2; FX​(x)=∫−∞x​fX​(u)du; ∫xfX​(x)dx=μ; E[(X−μ)2]; the peak of each curve circled.)

fX​(x)=2π​σ1​exp(−2σ2(x−μ)2​),E[X]=μ,Var(X)=σ2​

Verifying the three claims. Substitute z=σx−μ​ (dx=σdz). This turns every integral into one about φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2:

  1. ∫fX​(x)dx=∫φ(z)dz=1 (slide 13 proves this).
  2. E[X]=∫(μ+σz)φ(z)dz=μ⋅1+σ∫zφ(z)dz=μ, because zφ(z) is an odd function, so its integral over R is 0.
  3. Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=σ2∫z2φ(z)dz. Integrate by parts with u=z and dv=zφ(z)dz (so v=−φ(z), since φ′(z)=−zφ(z)):
∫z2φ(z)dz=[−zφ(z)]−∞∞​+∫φ(z)dz=0+1=1.

So Var(X)=σ2. (Last year's Tutorial 1 Calculus Q3 is the same computation: I1​=∫x2e−x2dx=2π​​.)

Slide 13/26: The standard normal N(0,1)

On the slide. μ=0, σ=1: fX​(x)=2π​1​e−x2/2. What is ∫−∞∞​e−x2/2dx? How do you even solve this? (=2π​.) The CDF is Φ(x)=2π​1​∫−∞x​e−t2/2dt. Its values are tabulated (Fig. 3.10 in Bertsekas). Wikipedia: Gaussian function. (Handwritten: Φ=FX​; X∼N(μ,σ2).)

The integral (the polar-coordinates trick). Let I=∫−∞∞​e−x2/2dx. e−x2/2 has no elementary antiderivative, so square I and turn it into a double integral:

I2=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​e−(x2+y2)/2dxdy=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2/2rdrdθ=2π[−e−r2/2]0∞​=2π.

(Polar coordinates: x2+y2=r2 and dxdy=rdrdθ. The extra r is exactly what makes the inner integral easy.) So I=2π​ and φ integrates to 1.

Using the Φ table. Every normal reduces to the standard one:

X∼N(μ,σ2) ⟹ Z=σX−μ​∼N(0,1),P(X≤x)=Φ(σx−μ​)​

(by slide 14). The table only lists z≥0. For negative z use symmetry: Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z).

Values worth memorising. Φ(1)≈0.8413, Φ(1.645)≈0.95, Φ(1.96)≈0.975, Φ(2)≈0.9772, Φ(3)≈0.99865. The 68–95–99.7 rule:

P(∣X−μ∣≤σ)≈0.683,P(∣X−μ∣≤2σ)≈0.954,P(∣X−μ∣≤3σ)≈0.997.

These come from P(∣Z∣≤k)=2Φ(k)−1. The first is last year's Tutorial 4 Q4 (sensor readings within one σ are accepted with probability 0.6827).

Worked example (Bertsekas Example 3.7). Annual snowfall is N(60,202) inches. P(at least 80)=P(Z≥2080−60​)=1−Φ(1)=0.1587.

Slide 14/26: Normality is preserved under linear transformations

On the slide. If X∼N(μ,σ2) then Y=aX+b is normal with E[Y]=aμ+b and variance a2σ2 (to be proved later). (Handwritten: HW: Y∼N(aμ+b,a2σ2), find fY​(y); also "E[Y]=aμ+b, Var(Y)=a2σ2" and "U[a,b]" in the margin.)

X∼N(μ,σ2), a=0 ⟹ aX+b∼N(aμ+b, a2σ2)​

HW proof (this was last year's Midsem A Q5). From Lecture 7, slide 6, fY​(y)=∣a∣1​fX​(ay−b​). Substitute the Gaussian pdf:

fY​(y)=∣a∣1​⋅2π​σ1​exp(−2σ2(ay−b​−μ)2​)=2π​∣a∣σ1​exp(−2a2σ2(y−b−aμ)2​),

using (ay−b​−μ)2=a2(y−b−aμ)2​. This is exactly the N(aμ+b,a2σ2) pdf (with standard deviation ∣a∣σ). □

The mean and variance alone (aμ+b and a2σ2) follow for any X from linearity. What is special about the normal is that the shape stays normal. The margin note "U[a,b]" makes the same point for the uniform: a linear function of a uniform is uniform (a+(b−a)U∼U[a,b]). The exponential is preserved under scaling only: cX∼Exp(λ/c) for c>0, but X+1 is not exponential.

Slide 15/26: Significance of the Gaussian

On the slide. Key role in the CLT: n1​∑i=1n​Xi​∼N(μ,nσ2​) (approximately) for any Xi​ with mean μ and variance σ2. Building block for Gaussian vectors and Gaussian processes. GPs are used in Bayesian optimisation; Brownian motion (a GP) in finance; GP regression and Gaussian mixture models in ML.

Slide 16/26: A list of distributions

On the slide. Wikipedia: List of probability distributions. Important ones: Beta, Gamma, Erlang, Logistic, Weibull.

None of these is examined in Lectures 1 to 8, but two appear in last year's homework: the Gamma(n,λ) pdf (n−1)!λn​xn−1e−λx (Tutorial 4 Q1, Lecture 7 B4), which is the time of the n-th arrival (the Erlang), and the Weibull (a lifetime model; last year's HW 6 Q4). The Laplace and Rayleigh distributions of HW 4 are in Lecture 7, B3 and B5.

Slide 17/26: Functions of a continuous random variable, the linear case

On the slide. Y=aX+b, X continuous. What are FY​ and fY​? FY​(y)=P(aX+b≤y)=FX​(ay−b​) if a>0, so fY​(y)=a1​fX​(ay−b​). FY​(y)=1−FX​(ay−b​) if a<0, so fY​(y)=−a1​fX​(ay−b​). In general fY​(y)=∣a∣1​fX​(ay−b​).

This repeats Lecture 7, slide 6 (see the explanation there). The point of repeating it: the two-case CDF argument is the template for any monotone g.

Slide 18/26: A guess for monotone g

On the slide. Boxed: for Y=aX+b, fY​(y)=∣a∣1​fX​(ay−b​). What if Y=g(X) with g continuous, differentiable and monotone? Any guess? Since g is monotone and continuous it is invertible; let h be the inverse, so h(Y)=X. Boxed: fY​(y)=​dydh​(y)​fX​(h(y)). (Handwritten: an arrow matching ∣a∣1​ to ∣dydh​∣ and ay−b​ to h(y), with "h(y)=ay−b​"; sketches of increasing and non-monotone curves.)

The handwritten arrows show how the linear formula is the general one: for g(x)=ax+b, the inverse is h(y)=ay−b​, with h′(y)=a1​. So ∣a∣1​=∣h′(y)∣ and ay−b​=h(y).

Slide 19/26: The theorem and the start of the proof

On the slide. Theorem. If Y=g(X) with g monotone, continuous and differentiable, then fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣dydh​(y)∣, where h=g−1. Proof. g is monotone and continuous, so invertible with inverse h, and X=h(Y). FY​(y)=P(g(X)≤y). Is P(g(X)≤y)=P(X≤h(y)) always? Are the events {g(X)≤y} and {X≤h(y)} the same? If they are, the probabilities are equal. (Handwritten: "two events are same if g is ↑; they are complements if g is ↓".)

fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))​dydh​(y)​,h=g−1​

The whole proof rests on the question in the slide: when is g(X)≤y the same event as X≤h(y)? The handwritten answer is the key. If g is increasing, applying h (also increasing) to both sides preserves the inequality, so the events are identical. If g is decreasing, applying h flips it: g(X)≤y⟺X≥h(y), the complement of {X<h(y)}.

Slide 20/26: The picture

On the slide. Two graphs: for increasing g, the event {g(X)≤y} is {X≤h(y)} (the x-axis to the left of h(y)); for decreasing g, it is {X≥h(y)} (to the right).

Slide 21/26: Case 1, g non-decreasing

On the slide. FY​(y)=P(g(X)≤y)=P(X≤h(y))=FX​(h(y)). fY​(y)=dyd​FX​(h(y))=fX​(h(y))dydh​(y), where dydh​≥0 since h is also non-decreasing. Rewritten: fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣dydh​(y)∣.

The derivative step is the chain rule: dyd​FX​(h(y))=FX′​(h(y))h′(y)=fX​(h(y))h′(y). Since h′≥0, it equals ∣h′∣.

Slide 22/26: Case 2, g non-increasing; the non-monotone HW

On the slide. FY​(y)=P(g(X)≤y)=P(X>h(y))=1−FX​(h(y)). fY​(y)=−dyd​FX​(h(y))=−fX​(h(y))dydh​(y), where dydh​≤0. Rewritten: fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣dydh​(y)∣. □ HW: what if g is not monotone? Q: if Y=X2, what is fY​(y) in terms of fX​? (Handwritten: Y=log(X), Y=ax2+bx.)

Since h′≤0, −h′=∣h′∣, and both cases give the same formula. □

Q: Y=X2. g(x)=x2 is not monotone on R, so go back to the CDF. For y>0:

FY​(y)=P(X2≤y)=P(−y​≤X≤y​)=FX​(y​)−FX​(−y​).

Differentiate (chain rule, dyd​y​=2y​1​):

fY​(y)=2y​fX​(y​)+fX​(−y​)​,y>0​

and fY​(y)=0 for y<0. Example: X∼N(0,1) gives fY​(y)=2πy​1​e−y/2 for y>0, the chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom. Example: X∼U[−1,1] (fX​=21​) gives fY​(y)=2y​1​ on (0,1]. Check: ∫01​2y​dy​=1. ✓

HW: general non-monotone g (last year's Tutorial 4 Q6). If the equation g(x)=y has roots x1​(y),x2​(y),…, each with g′(xi​)=0, then

fY​(y)=i∑​∣g′(xi​)∣fX​(xi​)​​

Why. Near each root, g is monotone, and the event {y<Y≤y+dy} is a union of small disjoint intervals around the roots, of lengths ∣g′(xi​)∣dy​. Adding their probabilities fX​(xi​)∣g′(xi​)∣dy​ and dividing by dy gives the formula. (Note ∣g′(xi​)∣1​=∣hi′​(y)∣ for the local inverse hi​, so each term is the monotone formula on one branch.) Check with Y=X2: roots ±y​, ∣g′(±y​)∣=2y​. ✓

The handwritten examples.

  • Y=logX (for X>0) is monotone: h(y)=ey, h′(y)=ey, so fY​(y)=fX​(ey)ey. For X∼U(0,1]: fY​(y)=ey for y≤0, so −logX∼Exp(1).
  • Y=aX2+bX is a parabola, not monotone: two roots x±​=2a−b±b2+4ay​​ for y beyond the vertex, with ∣g′(x±​)∣=∣2ax±​+b∣=b2+4ay​. Use the sum formula, or complete the square and reduce to the X2 case.

Slide 23/26: The CDF of a discrete random variable (board work)

On the slide. Title "Induced measure PX​ and CDF" with handwriting only: X:Ω→Ω′. Option 1: Ω′={a1​,a2​,a3​}, a PMF drawn as three spikes. Option 2: Ω′⊆R, the CDF drawn as a staircase with jumps at a3​<a1​<a2​ and the question "P(X≤a3​)?".

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's Quiz 1 Q1). X∼U[a,b] and Y=e2X. Find the pdf and CDF of Y.

Solution

Solution. g(x)=e2x is increasing, so Y∈[e2a,e2b], with h(y)=2lny​ and h′(y)=2y1​. CDF route: for y in the range,

FY​(y)=P(X≤2lny​)=b−a2lny​−a​=2(b−a)lny−2a​.

Formula route: fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣h′(y)∣=b−a1​⋅2y1​. Both give

fY​(y)=2y(b−a)1​,e2a≤y≤e2b;FY​(y)=0 (y<e2a),2(b−a)lny−2a​ (in range),1 (y>e2b).

The official solution writes out the support for both; do the same.

B2 (inverse transform for the exponential). U∼U(0,1) and Y=−λ1​lnU. Find the distribution of Y.

Solution

Solution. g(u)=−λ1​lnu is decreasing on (0,1) with range (0,∞). Inverse h(y)=e−λy, ∣h′(y)∣=λe−λy. So fY​(y)=1⋅λe−λy for y>0: Y∼Exp(λ). In general, F−1(U) has CDF F. This is how computers generate exponential samples (this year's L14 and L15).

B3 (last year's HW 4 Q3; a projectile). d=gv2​sin2φ with φ∼U[0,2π​]. Find the pdf of d and P(d≤d0​).

Solution

Solution. Let D=gv2​ and θ=2φ∼U[0,π], so d=Dsinθ, which rises then falls: not monotone. Use the CDF. For 0≤d0​≤D, sinθ≤Dd0​​ iff θ≤α or θ≥π−α, where α=arcsinDd0​​:

P(d≤d0​)=πα+α​=π2​arcsinDd0​​,fd​(x)=π2​⋅D2−x2​1​,0≤x<D.

The density blows up near the maximum range D: many angles near 45° give nearly the maximum distance.

B4 (last year's HW 4 Q12; a call option). Z∼N(0,1) and c≥0. Find E[max(Z−c,0)] in terms of Φ and φ.

Solution

Solution. LOTUS, with the integrand zero for z<c:

E[(Z−c)+]=∫c∞​(z−c)φ(z)dz=∫c∞​zφ(z)dz−c(1−Φ(c))=φ(c)−c(1−Φ(c))​,

using ∫c∞​zφ(z)dz=[−φ(z)]c∞​=φ(c) (since φ′=−zφ). At c=0 this is φ(0)=2π​1​≈0.399.

B5 (last year's HW 4 Q10; breaking a stick). A unit stick is broken at U∼U[0,1]. (a) Distribution and mean of the longer piece L. (b) Distribution of R = shorter/longer; E[R], E[1/R]. (c) Break the longer piece again uniformly. Probability that the three pieces form a triangle?

Solution

Solution. (a) L=max(U,1−U)∈[21​,1]. P(L≤ℓ)=P(1−ℓ≤U≤ℓ)=2ℓ−1, so L∼U[21​,1], fL​=2, E[L]=43​. (b) R=L1−L​ is a decreasing function of L. P(R≤r)=P(L≥1+r1​)=2−1+r2​=1+r2r​ for 0≤r≤1, so fR​(r)=(1+r)22​. Then E[R]=∫01​(1+r)22r​dr=2ln2−1≈0.386, and E[1/R]=∫01​r(1+r)22​dr=∞ (the integrand behaves like r2​ near 0). (c) The pieces are 1−L, LV, L(1−V) with V∼U[0,1]. A triangle needs every piece below 21​. 1−L≤21​ always. The other two need 1−2L1​<V<2L1​, an interval of length L1​−1. Average over L:

P=∫1/21​(ℓ1​−1)2dℓ=2ln2−1≈0.386.
