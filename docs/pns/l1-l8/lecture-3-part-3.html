Lecture 3, part 3

Counting

Deck 1, slides 57 to 72 · 15 min read · 8 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. When all outcomes are equally likely, P(A)=∣Ω∣∣A∣​, so probability becomes counting. Everything rests on one principle (multiply the number of choices at each stage) and one correction (divide out the orderings you do not want to distinguish). The slides organise sampling problems into a 2×2 grid: ordered or not, with or without replacement. Memorise the grid and how to decide which cell a problem is in.

Part A: the slides

Slide 57/73: The first principle of counting

On the slide. (A picture: the basic counting principle.)

Multiplication principle. If an object is built in r stages, with n1​ choices at stage 1, and then (whatever happened before) n2​ choices at stage 2, ..., nr​ at stage r, the number of objects is

n1​n2​⋯nr​​

The number of choices at a stage must not depend on earlier choices, although which choices are available may. Example: a 3-letter "word" with distinct letters has 26⋅25⋅24 forms. The second letter's options depend on the first, but there are always 25 of them.

Slide 58/73: Permutations and combinations

On the slide. n objects can be arranged in n! ways. Distinct pairs from n objects: (2n​)=(n−2)!2!n!​. In general (kn​)=(n−k)!k!n!​ combinations of k objects; order within a group is immaterial. What if we also count the ordering? nPk​=nCk​×k!.

nPk​=(n−k)!n!​=n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1),(kn​)=k!nPk​​=k!(n−k)!n!​​

Why divide by k!. Count ordered selections in two stages: first choose which k objects ((kn​) ways), then order them (k! ways). So nPk​=(kn​)k!. This "count it two ways" trick is behind almost every formula below.

Slide 59/73: Experiments with sampling

On the slide. Sampling = choosing elements from a set; uniformly at random means all items equally likely. With or without replacement; ordered ((a,b,c)=(c,b,a)) or unordered. Four combinations.

Choosing k from n distinct objects:

Ordered Unordered
With replacement nk (kn+k−1​)
Without replacement nPk​=(n−k)!n!​ (kn​)

How to decide. Replacement: can the same object appear twice (a digit in a PIN, a die face)? Order: would swapping two positions give a different outcome (first and second prize, a password) or the same one (a committee, a hand of cards)?

A warning for probability. In the three cells nk, nPk​ and (kn​), uniform sampling makes all the counted outcomes equally likely. In the "unordered with replacement" cell it does not. Two dice give the unordered outcome {1,2} in 2 ways but {1,1} in only 1 way. So for probabilities with replacement, always count ordered outcomes.

Slide 60/73: Ordered sampling with replacement

On the slide. Repetition allowed and order matters. How many ways: a) nk, b) (kn​), c) kn, d) nk? There are k positions and n choices for each. Total nk.

Multiplication principle with n choices at each of k stages: nk​. Examples: 6k outcomes for k dice; 104 four-digit PINs; 2n binary strings of length n. The tempting wrong answer kn swaps the roles: it counts functions from the n objects to the k positions.

Slide 61/73: Ordered sampling without replacement

On the slide. No repetition, order matters. Position i has n−i+1 choices. Total n(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)=(n−k)!n!​=nPk​.

Example: first, second and third prize among 20 people: 20⋅19⋅18=6840 (last year's HW 1 Q5). With k=n this is n!, the number of arrangements of all n.

Slide 62/73: Unordered sampling without replacement

On the slide. No repetition, order does not matter. We count distinct k-sized subsets: (kn​).

Example: a team of 4 from 15 students: (415​)=1365 (last year's HW 1 Q2 and Tutorial 1 Q1). A poker hand: (552​)=2,598,960.

Slide 63/73: Unordered sampling with replacement

On the slide. Repetition allowed, order does not matter. Object i can appear up to k times. Let xi​ = number of times object i is chosen. Every sample satisfies ∑i=1n​xi​=k, so count the solutions of this equation. Answer (kn+k−1​) (think: HW).

HW: why (kn+k−1​) (stars and bars). An unordered sample with replacement is determined by the counts (x1​,…,xn​) with xi​≥0 and ∑xi​=k. Encode it as a row of k stars and n−1 bars: x1​ stars, a bar, x2​ stars, a bar, ..., xn​ stars. For n=4, k=5:

⋆⋆∣∣⋆∣⋆⋆⟷(x1​,x2​,x3​,x4​)=(2,0,1,2).

Each solution gives exactly one such row and each row gives one solution. A row is n+k−1 symbols, and choosing which k of them are stars decides it:

#solutions=(kn+k−1​)=(n−1n+k−1​).

Example: 3 scoops of ice cream from 5 flavours (repeats allowed, order irrelevant): (37​)=35. Slides 69 and 70 redo this argument.

Slide 64/73: Example 1, licence plates

On the slide. 7-place plates, first 3 letters, last 4 digits: 263⋅104. What if letters and digits may not repeat?

With repetition: 263⋅104=175,760,000. Without repetition (ordered, no replacement in each block):

26⋅25⋅24⋅10⋅9⋅8⋅7=15,600⋅5,040=78,624,000.

Slide 65/73: Example 2, binary functions

On the slide. How many functions on n points take values in {0,1}? 2n.

Each of the n points independently gets a value 0 or 1: ordered sampling with replacement from {0,1}. It is the same count as subsets of an n-set (the points where the function is 1), which is why the power set is written 2Ω (Lecture 1, slide 16).

Slide 66/73: Example 3, arrangements of PEPPER

On the slide. If the P's and E's were distinguishable (P1​P2​P3​E1​E2​R) there would be 6! arrangements. Each indistinguishable arrangement (such as PPPREE) corresponds to 3!×2! distinguished ones. So the count is 3!2!1!6!​.

3!2!1!6!​=12720​=60.

The principle: overcount, then divide by the number of times each object was counted. This only works when every object is overcounted by the same factor (here 3!2! for every word).

Slide 67/73: Example 4, multinomial coefficients

On the slide. Permutations of n objects, n1​ alike, n2​ alike, ..., nr​ alike: n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!n!​ with ∑ni​=n. For r=2 this is (n1​n​). Dividing n distinct items into r groups of sizes n1​,…,nr​: (n1​n​)(n2​n−n1​​)⋯(nr​n−n1​−⋯−nr−1​​), which equals the same n1​!⋯nr​!n!​.

(n1​,n2​,…,nr​n​)=n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!n!​​

Why the product of binomials telescopes. Choose group 1, then group 2 from what is left, and so on:

n1​!(n−n1​)!n!​⋅n2​!(n−n1​−n2​)!(n−n1​)!​⋯=n1​!n2​!⋯nr​!n!​.

Two problems, one answer. "Arrange letters with repeats" and "split people into labelled groups" are the same problem: assigning each of n positions (people) a letter (group label) with prescribed counts.

Labelled vs unlabelled groups. If the groups have no names and equal sizes, divide by the number of ways to permute the groups. Splitting 6 people into three unnamed pairs: 2!2!2!3!6!​=15.

Slide 68/73: Example 5, balls in bins with no bin empty

On the slide. n identical red balls into r bins with no bin empty: count solutions of ∑i=1r​xi​=n with xi​>0. Arrange the n balls in a line. There are n−1 gaps, and we place r−1 sticks in distinct gaps (two sticks in one gap would make an empty bin). Choose r−1 of n−1 gaps: (r−1n−1​).

#{xi​≥1, x1​+⋯+xr​=n}=(r−1n−1​)​

Example: 10 identical sweets to 4 children, each getting at least one: (39​)=84.

Slides 69/73 and 70/73: Example 6, bins may be empty

On the slides. Now count solutions of ∑i=1r​xi​=n with 0≤xi​≤n. Substitute yi​=xi​+1: then ∑yi​=n+r with yi​≥1, and Example 5 gives (r−1n+r−1​). Alternative: write each xi​ as that many vertical lines and separate bins by r−1 plus signs; that is n+r−1 symbols, n alike and r−1 alike, so n!(r−1)!(n+r−1)!​=(r−1n+r−1​).

#{xi​≥0, x1​+⋯+xr​=n}=(r−1n+r−1​)​

(The slide writes the new constraint as "0<yi​<n". The correct range is 1≤yi​≤n+1; only yi​≥1 matters for the count.)

This is slide 63 again with n balls ↔ k samples and r bins ↔ n objects. Example: 10 identical sweets to 4 children, any number each: (313​)=286.

Slide 71/73: Example 7, coin tosses

On the slide. Toss a biased coin (P(H)=p) n times. P(k heads)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k. Convention 00=1 (for p=1, k=n). Probability that the first head is on toss r≤n? (1−p)r−1p.

Why (kn​)pk(1−p)n−k. Any particular sequence with k heads and n−k tails has probability pk(1−p)n−k (independent tosses multiply). There are (kn​) such sequences (choose which positions are heads). They are disjoint, so add. This is the binomial PMF of Lecture 6.

The 00 convention. With p=1 and k=n the formula reads (nn​)1n00, which must equal 1 (all heads is certain). Defining 00=1 makes the formula valid for all p∈[0,1].

First head on toss r means r−1 tails then a head: (1−p)r−1p. This is the geometric PMF of Lecture 6. It does not depend on n as long as r≤n.

Slide 72/73: Example 8, dice

On the slide. Roll a die n times (n even). P(half show 1, half show 6)=(n/2n​)(61​)n. P(n1​ show 1 and n2​ show 6)=n1​!n2​!(n−n1​−n2​)!n!​(61​)n1​(61​)n2​(64​)n−n1​−n2​.

First part. Choose which n/2 rolls are 1s ((n/2n​) ways; the rest are 6s). Each such sequence has probability (61​)n.

Second part: the multinomial probability. Sort each roll into three categories: "1" (probability 61​), "6" (61​), "other" (64​). A particular sequence with n1​, n2​, n3​=n−n1​−n2​ in the categories has probability (61​)n1​(61​)n2​(64​)n3​, and the number of such sequences is the multinomial coefficient (slide 67). In general:

P(n1​,…,nr​)=n1​!⋯nr​!n!​p1n1​​⋯prnr​​.

With r=2 categories this is the binomial formula.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems (counting)

B1 (last year's HW 1 Q1 to Q5, quick fire). (Q1) Two distinct posts among 8 candidates: 8P2​=56. (Q2) 4 from 15: (415​)=1365. (Q3) 3 of 12 distinct prizes to 1st, 2nd, 3rd: 12⋅11⋅10=1320. (Q4) 5⋅3⋅4=60 meals. (Q5) President, VP, secretary from 20: 20⋅19⋅18=6840.

B2 (last year's HW 1 Q8). 10 white and 3 black balls are drawn one by one without replacement until all 3 blacks are out. Probability the last black is drawn 7th?

Solution

Solution. All (313​)=286 sets of positions for the black balls are equally likely (symmetry: a random order of the balls). "Last black at position 7" means one black at 7 and the other two among positions 1 to 6: (26​)=15 ways. Answer 28615​.

B3 (last year's HW 1 Q7). Evaluate ∑r=115​r2(r15​)/(r−115​).

Solution

Solution. (r15​)/(r−115​)=r!(15−r)!(r−1)!(16−r)!​=r16−r​. So the term is r(16−r) and the sum is 16∑r−∑r2=16⋅120−1240=680​.

B4 (last year's HW 1 Q6, derangements). Cards 1 to 6 go into envelopes 1 to 6, no card in its own envelope, and card 1 is in envelope 2. How many ways?

Solution

Solution. First count all derangements of 6 with inclusion–exclusion. Let Ai​ = card i in envelope i; ∣Ai1​​∩⋯∩Aik​​∣=(6−k)!:

D6​=k=0∑6​(−1)k(k6​)(6−k)!=6!k=0∑6​k!(−1)k​=720−720+360−120+30−6+1=265.

By symmetry card 1 is equally likely to be in each of envelopes 2 to 6, so the answer is 5265​=53​. Related: the probability that a random permutation has no fixed point is ∑k=0n​k!(−1)k​→e−1≈0.368. That is the umbrella problem, last year's HW 3 Q3 (Lecture 6, Part B).

B5 (last year's Tutorial 1 Q2, pigeonhole). A drawer has 100 red, 80 green, 60 blue and 40 black socks. Minimum number drawn (in the dark) to guarantee at least 10 pairs?

Solution

Solution. With s socks the number of pairs is 2s−u​, where u is the number of colours with an odd count. Since u≤4, the worst case leaves 4 unpaired socks. Need 2s−u​≥10 in the worst case. s=22 fails (counts like 5,5,5,7 give u=4 and only 9 pairs). For s=23 (odd), u is odd so u≤3, giving at least 223−3​=10 pairs. Answer 23​.

B6 (Bertsekas Problem 1.53). 90 students, including Joe and Jane, are split at random into three classes of 30. Probability Joe and Jane are in the same class?

Solution

Solution. Put Joe somewhere. Jane is equally likely to be in any of the remaining 89 seats, 29 of which are in Joe's class: 8929​. (A full count gives the same thing, but the "fix one person" trick is far quicker.)

B7 (Bertsekas Problem 1.17). A batch of 100 items has 5 defective. Testing 4 at random, the batch is accepted if none is defective. P(accept)?

Solution

Solution. Unordered, without replacement: (4100​)(495​)​=100⋅99⋅98⋅9795⋅94⋅93⋅92​≈0.812. (Sequential view: 10095​⋅9994​⋅9893​⋅9792​, the chain rule. Same number.)

B8 (Bertsekas Problem 1.50, the birthday problem). n people, 365 equally likely birthdays. Probability all birthdays differ?

Solution

Solution. Ordered with replacement for Ω (365n); ordered without replacement for the event (365⋅364⋯(365−n+1)):

P(all distinct)=365n365⋅364⋯(365−n+1)​=i=0∏n−1​(1−365i​).

For n=23, P(some shared birthday)=1−0.4927≈0.507. For n=10 it is 0.117.

B9 (last year's Tutorial 1 Q3: geometric probability). Two friends arrive independently and uniformly between 5 and 6 pm; whoever arrives first waits 15 minutes. Probability they meet?

Solution

Solution. In hours after 5 pm, (X,Y) is uniform on the unit square (Lecture 1, slide 15), so probability = area. They meet iff ∣X−Y∣≤41​. The complement is two corner triangles with legs 43​, total area 2⋅21​⋅(43​)2=169​. So P=1−169​=167​​.
