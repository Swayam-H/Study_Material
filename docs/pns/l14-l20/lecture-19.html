Lecture 19

Random vectors, the covariance matrix and the Jacobian

Deck C, slides 1 to 8⏱ 25 min read✓ 12 practice problems
Solutions stay hidden until you click them.

Big picture. So far we have handled one or two random variables at a time. Many problems involve several at once: the coordinates of a point, the readings of several sensors, the returns of several stocks. This lecture packs them into a random vector and builds the vector versions of three familiar tools:

One variable (L1 to L13) Random vector (this lecture)
mean E[X] mean vector E[X]
variance Var(X) covariance matrix CX​
Var(aX+b)=a2Var(X) CAX+b​=ACX​AT
fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣h′(y)∣ fY​(y)=fX​(H(y))∣J∣

These are exactly the tools L20 needs for Gaussian vectors.

Part A: the slides

Slide 1/36: Random vectors

On the slide. We are now moving from a univariate random variable to multivariate random variables, also called random vectors. An n-dimensional random vector is a column vector X=(X1​,…,Xn​)T whose components Xi​ are scalar-valued random variables defined on the same space (Ω,F,P). Since the components are on the same space, they may be correlated with each other. Example: X=(X1​,X2​)T where X1​=Z1​ and X2​=Z1​+Z2​, where Z1​ and Z2​ are independent standard normal. What is the pdf, cdf, marginals, mean, variance/covariance of X?

Why "the same space" matters. All components are functions of the same outcome ω, so they can be related. In the example, X1​ and X2​ share Z1​: if Z1​ happens to be large, both are pushed up. A random vector is just a convenient package for several jointly distributed random variables (L10 to L13), with matrix notation to keep the algebra short.

The red question, fully solved. Everything follows from writing X in terms of the independent Z's.

Mean. E[X1​]=E[Z1​]=0 and E[X2​]=E[Z1​]+E[Z2​]=0, so E[X]=(0,0)T.

Variances and covariance.

Var(X1​)=1,Var(X2​)=Var(Z1​)+Var(Z2​)=2,
Cov(X1​,X2​)=Cov(Z1​,Z1​+Z2​)=Var(Z1​)+Cov(Z1​,Z2​)=1+0=1.

So

CX​=[11​12​],ρX1​,X2​​=1⋅2​1​=2​1​≈0.71.

Marginals. X1​=Z1​∼N(0,1). X2​=Z1​+Z2​ is a sum of independent normals, so X2​∼N(0,2) (L14 slide 5).

Joint pdf. The map (z1​,z2​)↦(x1​,x2​)=(z1​,z1​+z2​) is invertible: z1​=x1​ and z2​=x2​−x1​. Then for any region B,

P(X∈B)=P((Z1​,Z2​)∈{(x1​,x2​−x1​):(x1​,x2​)∈B}).

This map is a "shear" that preserves areas (its Jacobian, slide 7, is 1), so the density just moves along with it:

fX​(x1​,x2​)=fZ1​​(x1​)fZ2​​(x2​−x1​)=2π1​exp(−2x12​+(x2​−x1​)2​).

(L20 shows that this is the bivariate normal density with covariance CX​: indeed CX−1​=[2−1​−11​] and xTCX−1​x=2x12​−2x1​x2​+x22​=x12​+(x2​−x1​)2.)

Joint CDF. Condition on Z1​=z:

FX​(x1​,x2​)=P(Z1​≤x1​, Z1​+Z2​≤x2​)=∫−∞x1​​φ(z)P(Z2​≤x2​−z)dz=∫−∞x1​​φ(z)Φ(x2​−z)dz.

This has no closed form, which is typical. Densities are usually easier to work with than CDFs for vectors.

Slide 2/36: Notation

On the slide. The CDF and pdf of X are FX​(x)=FX1​,…,Xn​​(x1​,…,xn​) and fX​(x)=fX1​,…,Xn​​(x1​,…,xn​). The joint CDF/pdf captures the correlation between components. The expected value vector is E[X]=(E[X1​],…,E[Xn​])T. Linearity of expectation holds here, so for any deterministic matrix A and vector b, and Y=AX+b, we have E[Y]=AE[X]+b.

FX​(x)=P(X1​≤x1​,…,Xn​≤xn​),P(X∈B)=∫B​fX​(x)dx.

Why E[AX+b]=AE[X]+b. Look at one row. The i-th component of Y is Yi​=∑j​aij​Xj​+bi​, an ordinary linear combination, so by ordinary linearity E[Yi​]=∑j​aij​E[Xj​]+bi​, which is the i-th component of AE[X]+b. A can be any m×n matrix: Y may have a different dimension from X.

Marginals from the joint (recall L11). Integrate out the other variables: fX1​​(x1​)=∫⋯∫fX​(x1​,x2​,…,xn​)dx2​⋯dxn​. The joint determines the marginals, but not the other way round, except when the components are independent, in which case fX​=∏i​fXi​​.

Slide 3/36: The covariance matrix

On the slide. The covariance matrix CX​ captures the covariance between components and is defined by

CX​=E[(X−E[X])(X−E[X])T]=​Var(X1​)Cov(X2​,X1​)⋮Cov(Xn​,X1​)​Cov(X1​,X2​)Var(X2​)Cov(Xn​,X2​)​………​Cov(X1​,Xn​)Cov(X2​,Xn​)⋮Var(Xn​)​​

Why the outer product gives this matrix. Let D=X−E[X], a column vector. Then DDT is an n×n matrix whose (i,j) entry is Di​Dj​=(Xi​−EXi​)(Xj​−EXj​). Taking expectations entry by entry gives Cov(Xi​,Xj​), and on the diagonal Cov(Xi​,Xi​)=Var(Xi​).

Basic facts.

  • CX​ is symmetric, because Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=Cov(Xj​,Xi​).
  • Shortcut (like Var(X)=E[X2]−(EX)2): CX​=E[XXT]−E[X]E[X]T.
  • Independent components give a diagonal CX​. (A diagonal CX​ does not imply independence in general, but it does for Gaussian vectors; see L20.)
  • The correlation coefficients are read off as ρij​=Cii​Cjj​​Cij​​.

Slide 4/36: Properties, and functions of random vectors

On the slide. The covariance matrix CX​ is always positive semi-definite, i.e., for any vector a=0 we have aTCX​a≥0. Why? Let u=aT(X−E[X]); then aTCX​a=E[uuT]=E[u2]≥0. HW: if Y=AX+b, show that CY​=ACX​AT. Now recall how we obtained the pdf of Y from the pdf of X when Y=g(X): for g monotone, continuous and differentiable, fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣dydh​(y)∣ where h is the inverse function of g. How does this generalise to Y=G(X)? How do we get fY​ from fX​?

Positive semi-definite (PSD), in words. For any fixed vector a, the scalar aTX=∑ai​Xi​ is a random variable, and

Var(aTX)=aTCX​a​

A variance is never negative, so aTCX​a≥0 for every a. That is the PSD property. The slide's proof is exactly this: u=aT(X−EX) is a scalar with mean 0, so E[u2]=Var(aTX), and

E[u2]=E[aT(X−EX)(X−EX)Ta]=aTE[(X−EX)(X−EX)T]a=aTCX​a.

(The constant vectors aT and a come out of the expectation by linearity.)

When is it strictly positive? aTCX​a=0 means Var(aTX)=0, so aTX is a constant: the components satisfy an exact linear relation. Example (probabilitycourse 6.1.5): for X,Y independent U(0,1) and V=(X,Y,X+Y)T, the vector a=(1,1,−1) gives aTV=0, so CV​ is singular (detCV​=0). Without such exact relations, CX​ is positive definite: all eigenvalues >0 and detCX​>0, so CX−1​ exists. (L20's Gaussian density needs this.)

HW: CY​=ACX​AT. Since E[Y]=AE[X]+b, the b cancels: Y−E[Y]=A(X−E[X]). Then, using (AD)T=DTAT:

CY​=E[A(X−EX)(X−EX)TAT]=AE[(X−EX)(X−EX)T]AT=ACX​AT.□
Y=AX+b ⟹ E[Y]=AE[X]+b,CY​=ACX​AT​

This is the vector form of Var(aX+b)=a2Var(X): the shift b disappears, and the scaling appears "twice". The single-entry versions are worth remembering:

Var(aTX)=aTCX​a,Cov(aTX,bTX)=aTCX​b.

Check with slide 1. X=AZ with A=[11​01​] and CZ​=I, so CX​=AAT=[11​12​]. ✓ Same answer as before, with no covariance algebra.

The 1D change of variables, recalled. For Y=g(X) with g monotone and h=g−1: fY​(y)=fX​(h(y))∣h′(y)∣. The factor ∣h′(y)∣ is the "stretch factor": probability is conserved, so where g stretches intervals, the density must thin out by the same factor. Slides 5 to 8 generalise this; the derivative becomes a matrix of partial derivatives, and its determinant measures how volumes are stretched.

Slide 5/36: Functions of random vectors

On the slide. Let Y=G(X) where G:Rn→Rn is continuous and invertible with continuous partial derivatives. Then one can write Yi​=Gi​(X1​,…,Xn​) for i=1,…,n. For example, if [Y1​Y2​​]=[2X1​X1​+X2​​] then G1​(X1​,X2​)=2X1​ and G2​(X1​,X2​)=X1​+X2​. What does continuity of G mean? Continuity of components?

The blue question. A vector-valued function G=(G1​,…,Gn​) is continuous exactly when every component Gi​:Rn→R is continuous. (A vector is close to another exactly when every coordinate is close.) Similarly, "continuous partial derivatives" means every ∂xj​∂Gi​​ exists and is continuous. In the example, G1​=2x1​ and G2​=x1​+x2​ are polynomials, so all is well.

Two requirements to check in every problem.

  1. Same dimension (Rn→Rn), so that the Jacobian matrix is square and has a determinant. If you want the density of fewer variables, for example only Y1​=X1​+X2​, add a convenient extra variable such as Y2​=X2​, apply the theorem, and then integrate Y2​ out.
  2. Invertible (one-to-one), at least on the region where fX​>0, so that each y comes from exactly one x.

Slide 6/36: The inverse map

On the slide. Let H denote the inverse of G. We similarly have Xi​=Hi​(Y1​,…,Yn​). For the example we have X1​=H1​(Y1​,Y2​)=2X1​​ [should read 2Y1​​] and X2​=H2​(Y1​,Y2​)=Y2​−2Y1​​.

Finding H: solve for the x's. From y1​=2x1​, x1​=2y1​​. Substitute into y2​=x1​+x2​: x2​=y2​−2y1​​. So

H(y)=[y1​/2y2​−y1​/2​].

(The slide's first formula has a typo: H1​ must be written in terms of Y, so X1​=2Y1​​.)

Check: G(H(y))=(2⋅2y1​​, 2y1​​+y2​−2y1​​)=(y1​,y2​). ✓

Slide 7/36: The Jacobian theorem

On the slide. Let Y=G(X) where G:Rn→Rn is continuous and invertible with continuous partial derivatives, and let H denote its inverse. Then

fY​(y)=fX​(H(y))∣J∣

where J is the determinant of the Jacobian matrix

​∂y1​∂H1​​∂y1​∂H2​​⋮∂y1​∂Hn​​​∂y2​∂H1​​∂y2​∂H2​​∂y2​∂Hn​​​………​∂yn​∂H1​​∂yn​∂H2​​⋮∂yn​∂Hn​​​​
fY​(y)=fX​(H(y))∣J∣,J=det[∂yj​∂Hi​​]i,j​​

Row i of the matrix holds the partial derivatives of the i-th inverse component Hi​. Note that J is computed from the inverse map H, as functions of y, and that the formula uses its absolute value.

The recipe.

  1. Solve y=G(x) for x=H(y).
  2. Compute the matrix of partials of H and its determinant J.
  3. Write fY​(y)=fX​(H(y))∣J∣.
  4. Find the support: the set of y for which H(y) lies where fX​>0. Students lose most marks on this step.

A useful shortcut. The Jacobian of the inverse is the reciprocal of the Jacobian of the forward map: J=det[∂Gi​/∂xj​]1​, evaluated at x=H(y). Use whichever is easier to differentiate.

With n=1 the matrix is the single entry h′(y), and the theorem is the 1D formula of slide 4.

Slide 8/36: The Jacobian determinant, and two HWs

On the slide. From vector calculus: the Jacobian gives the ratio of the incremental areas dx1​dx2​⋯dxn​ and dy1​⋯dyn​. (Links: Wikipedia, Jacobian matrix and determinant; Khan Academy.) HW1: for the running example, find fY​(y). HW2: when Y=AX+b, show that fY​(y)=∣det(A)∣1​fX​(A−1(y−b)).

Why the determinant (the "ratio of areas"). Probability is conserved: the small box dy around y comes from a small region around x=H(y), and both have the same probability:

fY​(y)vol(dy)=fX​(x)vol(dx)⟹fY​(y)=fX​(x)vol(dy)vol(dx)​.

Near y, the map H is approximately linear, with matrix [∂yj​∂Hi​​]. A linear map multiplies all volumes by the absolute value of its determinant. So vol(dy)vol(dx)​=∣J∣. For example, the matrix [a0​0d​] turns a unit square into an a×d rectangle, of area ∣ad∣.

HW1: the running example. H(y)=(2y1​​, y2​−2y1​​), so

[∂yj​∂Hi​​]=[21​−21​​01​],J=21​⋅1−0⋅(−21​)=21​.
fY​(y1​,y2​)=21​fX​(2y1​​, y2​−2y1​​)​

(Forward check: det[21​01​]=2, and 21​ is its reciprocal. The map doubles areas, so the density halves.)

With specific inputs.

  • If X1​,X2​ are i.i.d. N(0,1): fY​(y)=4π1​exp(−21​[4y12​​+(y2​−2y1​​)2]).
  • If X is slide 1's vector: fY​(y)=4π1​exp(−21​[4y12​​+(y2​−y1​)2]).

(Both are bivariate normal densities, with covariance matrices AAT=[42​22​] and ACX​AT=[44​45​], where A=[21​01​]. Both have determinant 4, matching the constant 2π4​1​=4π1​.)

HW2: affine maps. If A is an invertible n×n matrix, then y=Ax+b inverts to H(y)=A−1(y−b). This is linear in y, with matrix of partial derivatives A−1 (constant), so J=det(A−1)=detA1​. Hence

fY​(y)=∣detA∣1​fX​(A−1(y−b))​

(probabilitycourse Example 6.15.) The running example is the case A=[21​01​], detA=2, b=0. L20 uses this with X a standard normal vector to get the multivariate Gaussian density.

B1 (last year's HW9 Q3). Y1​=X1​+X2​, Y2​=X1​−X2​. (a) Find H. (b) Find ∣J∣. (c) Write fY​ in terms of fX​.

Solution

Solution. (a) Adding and subtracting: X1​=2Y1​+Y2​​, X2​=2Y1​−Y2​​. (b) J=det[21​21​​21​−21​​]=−41​−41​=−21​, so ∣J∣=21​. (c) fY​(y1​,y2​)=21​fX​(2y1​+y2​​,2y1​−y2​​). Example. If X1​,X2​ are i.i.d. N(0,1), then (2y1​+y2​​)2+(2y1​−y2​​)2=2y12​+y22​​, so

fY​(y)=21​⋅2π1​e−(y12​+y22​)/4=4π​1​e−y12​/4⋅4π​1​e−y22​/4.

The density factorises: the sum and difference are independent N(0,2) variables. (Only for normals is this true; L20 explains why.)

B2 (last year's Quiz 2 Q4). (X1​,X2​) uniform on the unit square, Y1​=X1​−X2​, Y2​=X1​+X2​. Find fY​.

Solution

Solution. X1​=2Y1​+Y2​​, X2​=2Y2​−Y1​​, ∣J∣=21​. So fY​=21​ wherever 0<2y1​+y2​​<1 and 0<2y2​−y1​​<1, that is,

∣y1​∣<y2​  (0<y2​≤1),∣y1​∣<2−y2​  (1<y2​<2).

The support is the square with corners (0,0),(1,1),(0,2),(−1,1): the unit square rotated by 45° and scaled by 2​, which has area 2 (so the density 21​ integrates to 1). Cross-check: integrating out y1​ gives fY2​​(y2​)=y2​ on (0,1] and 2−y2​ on (1,2), the triangular density of a sum of two uniforms (L13 convolution).

B11 (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Example 8): the Box–Muller method, proved with the Jacobian. Let U1​,U2​ be independent U(0,1) and

Z1​=−2lnU1​​cos(2πU2​),Z2​=−2lnU1​​sin(2πU2​).

Show that Z1​,Z2​ are independent N(0,1). (This is how computers make normal samples, which the inverse transform of L15 cannot do in closed form.)

Solution

Solution. Think in polar coordinates: the radius is R=−2lnU1​​ and the angle is Θ=2πU2​. The inverse map is

u1​=e−(z12​+z22​)/2,u2​=2π1​θ(z1​,z2​),
