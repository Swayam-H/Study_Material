Lectures 14 and 15

Generating samples from a uniform

Deck A, slides 7 to 18 · 31 min read · 8 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Big picture. A computer gives you one kind of randomness: (pseudo-)random numbers U1​,U2​,… that behave like i.i.d. U[0,1] samples. How these are produced is not part of the course (slide 13 says so). The question for these two lectures is:

Given i.i.d. uniforms, how do you produce i.i.d. samples of any random variable X you like, and how do you use samples to compute things such as integrals and expectations?

Four tools answer this:

Tool Slides What it does
Inverse transform (discrete) 8 to 12 U↦X by cutting [0,1] into intervals of lengths pj​
Inverse transform (continuous) 13 to 15 X=FX−1​(U)
Probability integral transform 16 the reverse direction: FX​(X)∼U[0,1]
Monte Carlo and importance sampling 17, 18 estimate integrals and expectations by sample averages

Accept–reject (L16) is a fifth tool for when FX−1​ is hard to compute.

Part A: the slides

Slide 7/23: "Generate samples using uniform distribution"

This is the section title. Everything from here to slide 18 builds samples of other distributions out of uniform samples.

Slide 8/23: Our aim, and 5000 uniform samples

On the slide. Suppose you have access to samples from a uniform random variable U over [0,1]. The code uni_samples = np.random.uniform(0, 1, 5000) and plt.hist(uni_samples, bins = 10, density = True) give a flat histogram of height about 1. uni_samples is a vector of 5000 realizations of U. You can also see it as a realization of U1​,U2​,…,U5000​, i.i.d. uniform variables.

Two ways to read the same vector. This distinction comes back in the convergence lectures, so it is worth fixing now.

  • 5000 realizations of one random variable U. You ran the same experiment 5000 times and wrote down the outcome each time.
  • One realization of 5000 i.i.d. random variables U1​,…,U5000​. A single outcome ω of a big experiment gives the whole vector (U1​(ω),…,U5000​(ω)).

The second view is the one used for sample means: n1​∑Ui​ is a random variable, and the SLLN (slide 17, and L17) is a statement about the sequence U1​,U2​,….

Why the histogram has height about 1. With density = True, matplotlib scales the bars so their total area is 1. There are 10 bins of width 0.1, each holding about 500 of the 5000 samples, so each bar has height about 5000×0.1500​=1. That is the U[0,1] density. The bars wobble around 1 because 5000 is finite. The count in each bar is Binomial(5000,0.1), with standard deviation 5000×0.1×0.9​≈21, about 4% of 500, which is the size of the wobble in the picture.

Slide 9/23: How to simulate a die using these samples

On the slide. Can you convert the 5000 uniform samples into outcomes of a die? The code assigns dice_sample = 1 if u < 1/6, 2 if 1/6 < u < 2/6, and so on up to 6 if 5/6 < u < 6/6, then plots a histogram with 6 bins. Example: uniforms [0.02, 0.8, 0.6, 0.03] give dice [1, 5, 4, 1]. The histogram is flat at about 0.2.

The idea. Cut [0,1] into 6 pieces of equal length 61​. The face is the number of the piece that u falls into:

face=kif6k−1​≤u<6k​,soP(face=k)=P(6k−1​≤U<6k​)=61​.

The last step uses toolkit fact 6: the probability that U lands in an interval is the interval's length.

Checking the slide's example.

u piece face
0.02 [0,61​)=[0,0.167) 1
0.8 [64​,65​)=[0.667,0.833) 5
0.6 [63​,64​)=[0.5,0.667) 4
0.03 [0,61​) 1

Two details in the code.

  1. One-line version. The face is ⌊6u⌋+1, which in numpy is np.floor(6*uni_samples) + 1.
  2. Boundaries. The slide's if conditions use strict inequalities on both sides, so a value such as u=61​ exactly matches no branch. That has probability 0 for a continuous U, so it does not affect the distribution. In code, the half-open intervals [6k−1​,6k​) are the clean choice.

Why the histogram has height 0.2, not 61​. The 6 bins span [1,6], so each has width 65​. Each holds about 61​ of the samples, and density = True makes height × width = fraction, so the height is 5/61/6​=0.2. The flatness is what matters: every face is equally likely.

Slide 10/23: Our aim for a general discrete random variable

On the slide. Consider a discrete random variable X with support set {x0​,x1​,…} and pmf pX​(xj​)=pj​ for j=0,1,… such that ∑j​pj​=1. The support could be finite or infinite. Our aim: create i.i.d. samples of X using i.i.d. random samples of U. We shall now see the inverse transform method to do this.

The die is the special case xj​=j+1 and pj​=61​. Now the pieces of [0,1] have unequal lengths p0​,p1​,…, and there may be infinitely many of them (for example, Poisson or geometric).

"i.i.d. samples of X from i.i.d. samples of U" means: apply the same function to each Ui​. If Xi​=h(Ui​) with U1​,U2​,… i.i.d., then X1​,X2​,… are automatically i.i.d. (functions of independent variables are independent). So the only thing to design is the function h that gives the right pmf.

Slide 11/23: The inverse transform method

On the slide. Let u∈[0,1] be a realization of U. The corresponding sample of X is

X=⎩⎨⎧​x0​x1​x2​ ⋮xj​ ⋮​if u<p0​if p0​≤u<p0​+p1​if p0​+p1​≤u<p0​+p1​+p2​if ∑i=0j−1​pi​≤u<∑i=0j​pi​​

Why is this method correct? Why call it the inverse transform method?

Picture. Lay the probabilities end to end along [0,1]:

0        p0        p0+p1          p0+p1+p2                 1
|---x0---|----x1----|------x2-------|---- ... ----------------|

Throw U at the stick. Whichever segment it lands in, output that segment's label.

Algorithm (as code). Walk along the segments until the running total passes u:

def sample_discrete(xs, ps, u):
    cum = 0.0
    for x, p in zip(xs, ps):
        cum += p
        if u < cum:
            return x
    return xs[-1]   # guards against round-off when u is very close to 1

Slide 12/23: Why it is correct, and why "inverse transform"

On the slide. X=xj​ if ∑i=0j−1​pi​≤U<∑i=0j​pi​. Now P(X=xj​)=pj​, hence the method is correct. Recall FX​(xj​)=∑i=0j​pi​. This implies X=xj​ if FX​(xj−1​)≤U<FX​(xj​). After generating U, we determine X by finding the interval [FX​(xj−1​),FX​(xj​)) in which u lies. At a high level we are performing X=FX−1​(U), but FX​ is discontinuous, so its inverse has to be cleverly defined.

Correctness. The segment for xj​ has length exactly pj​:

P(X=xj​)=P(i=0∑j−1​pi​≤U<i=0∑j​pi​)=i=0∑j​pi​−i=0∑j−1​pi​=pj​.✓

The name. The cut points are the values of the CDF (here we list the support in increasing order, x0​<x1​<⋯): ∑i≤j​pi​=FX​(xj​). So the rule reads

X=xj​⟺FX​(xj−1​)≤U<FX​(xj​)​.

Look at the graph of the staircase FX​. You start from the height U on the vertical axis, move right until you hit the staircase, and drop down to the x value where the step happens. That is reading the graph of FX​ backwards, from y to x, which is what an inverse function does. Hence "inverse transform".

The "cleverly defined" inverse. A staircase has no inverse in the usual sense: it is flat between jumps (many x for one height) and it jumps (heights it never takes). The standard fix, which works for every CDF, is the generalized inverse

F−1(u):=inf{x:F(x)≥u}​0<u<1,

"the smallest x at which the CDF reaches height u". For a staircase it returns xj​ for every u∈(F(xj−1​),F(xj​)], which is the slide's rule. (The only difference is at the single points u=F(xj​), which have probability 0.) For a continuous strictly increasing F it is the ordinary inverse. The proof that it always works is on the next page, after slide 14.

Efficiency note (Ross). The loop checks segments in order, so the expected number of comparisons is ∑j​(j+1)pj​. Listing the values in decreasing order of probability makes the loop stop sooner on average. (The method stays correct in any order, since the segment lengths are still pj​, but then the cut points are no longer CDF values.)

Slide 13/23: Our aim for a continuous random variable

On the slide. Suppose you have access to samples from U over [0,1] (we will not study how to generate such samples). Consider a continuous random variable X with support set X and CDF FX​(x). The support of X could be arbitrary. Our aim: create i.i.d. samples of X using i.i.d. samples of U. We shall again see the inverse transform method.

The discrete method found the x where the CDF crosses height U. For a continuous X, the CDF has no jumps, so "the x where FX​ reaches height U" is just the solution of FX​(x)=U.

Slide 14/23: The lemma and its proof

On the slide. Lemma. Let U be uniform over [0,1]. Consider a continuous random variable X with CDF FX​(⋅), and define X^:=FX−1​(U). Then the CDF of X^ is FX​(⋅). Proof. FX^​(x)=P[FX−1​(U)≤x]=P[U≤FX​(x)]=FX​(x).

The proof line by line.

  1. FX^​(x)=P(X^≤x)=P(FX−1​(U)≤x). This is the definition of a CDF.
  2. FX−1​(U)≤x⟺U≤FX​(x). Apply the increasing function FX​ to both sides of the inequality. Because FX​ is increasing it keeps the direction of the inequality, and FX​(FX−1​(U))=U.
  3. P(U≤FX​(x))=FX​(x), because FX​(x) is a number in [0,1] and P(U≤c)=c for such c (toolkit fact 6).

Making step 2 airtight, for any CDF. Step 2 is immediate when FX​ is continuous and strictly increasing. With the generalized inverse G(u)=inf{x:F(x)≥u} it holds for every CDF F:

G(u)≤x⟺u≤F(x).

Proof. (⇐) If u≤F(x), then x belongs to the set {y:F(y)≥u}, so the infimum G(u) of that set is at most x. (⇒) Because F is non-decreasing and right-continuous, the set {y:F(y)≥u} is an interval [G(u),∞) that contains its left endpoint, so F(G(u))≥u. If G(u)≤x, then F(x)≥F(G(u))≥u. □

So P(G(U)≤x)=P(U≤F(x))=F(x) for every CDF. One method covers discrete, continuous and mixed distributions, and slide 12's rule is the discrete case.

Slide 15/23: How to use the lemma, and what is special about it

On the slide. Using this lemma, how do you generate samples of a continuous X from samples of U? Answer: draw u∼U and compute FX−1​(u). This is a sample of X^, which has the same distribution as X. (Link: Wikipedia, inverse transform sampling.) Do you observe anything "special" about this lemma?

Recipe.

  1. Write down the CDF FX​(x).
  2. Solve FX​(x)=u for x. The answer is x=FX−1​(u).
  3. For each uniform sample ui​, output xi​=FX−1​(ui​).

Example (the classic): Exp(λ). F(x)=1−e−λx for x≥0. Solve 1−e−λx=u:

e−λx=1−u ⇒ x=−λ1​ln(1−u).

So X=−λ1​ln(1−U)∼ Exp(λ). Since 1−U is also U[0,1], the simpler X=−λ1​lnU works too (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Example 5).

What is special. Three things.

  1. One uniform is enough for any distribution. The same single source of randomness produces exponential, Cauchy, normal (in principle), anything. This is called the universality of the uniform.
  2. The construction is explicit and exact. There is no approximation, unlike Monte Carlo. Each u gives exactly one sample, and none are wasted, unlike accept–reject in L16.
  3. It runs both ways. If F−1(U) has CDF F, then applying F to an F-distributed variable should give back a uniform. That is slide 16.

Its limitation is also worth knowing: you need FX−1​ in a usable form. For the normal distribution, Φ has no closed form, so its inverse has none either. That is why other methods exist (accept–reject in L16, and Box–Muller in L19).

Slide 16/23: Application in data analysis

On the slide. Lemma: X^=FX−1​(U) has distribution FX​(⋅). What will be the CDF of Y=FX​(X^)? Uniform! A consequence of this lemma is that FX​(X) is a uniform distribution. This property is known as the "probability integral transform" or "universality of the uniform". This property is used to test whether a set of observations can be modelled as arising from a specified distribution G(⋅) or not.

Why FX​(X) is uniform. Two short proofs.

Via the lemma (the slide's route). X and X^=FX−1​(U) have the same distribution, so FX​(X) and FX​(X^) have the same distribution. But FX​(X^)=FX​(FX−1​(U))=U when FX​ is continuous. So FX​(X)∼U[0,1].

Directly (when FX​ is continuous and strictly increasing). For 0<y<1:

P(FX​(X)≤y)=P(X≤FX−1​(y))=FX​(FX−1​(y))=y.

That is the U[0,1] CDF.

X continuous with CDF FX​ ⟹ FX​(X)∼U[0,1]​

Continuity is needed. For X∼ Bernoulli(21​), FX​(X) equals 21​ (when X=0) or 1 (when X=1). It takes only two values, so it is not uniform.

Example. If X∼ Exp(λ) then 1−e−λX∼U[0,1], and so is e−λX (as 1−U is uniform).

Goodness-of-fit testing, the red bullet. You have data x1​,…,xn​ and a hypothesis "the data come from CDF G". Transform each point: ui​=G(xi​).

  • If the hypothesis is right, the ui​ are i.i.d. U[0,1] samples, so their histogram should be flat, and their sorted values should lie close to the line ni​.
  • If the ui​ bunch up (for example, most of them near 0 or near 1), the data did not come from G.

Formal tests such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test measure how far the ui​ are from uniform. All of them rest on this one fact: the question "is the data from G?" becomes the standard question "is the data uniform?".

Slide 17/23: Evaluating integrals via the Monte Carlo approach

On the slide. Suppose you want to compute θ=∫01​g(x)dx using only samples from U[0,1]. How? θ=E[g(U)]. Use i.i.d. samples of U and invoke the strong law of large numbers (SLLN): suppose Xi​ are i.i.d. and Sn​=∑i=1n​Xi​; then nSn​​→E[X]. As n→∞, ∑i=1n​ng(Ui​)​→E[g(U)]=θ. HW: how will you compute ∫ab​g(x)dx or ∫0∞​g(x)dx?

Step 1: write the integral as an expectation. The U[0,1] density is 1 on [0,1], so by LOTUS

E[g(U)]=∫01​g(x)⋅1dx=θ.

Step 2: estimate the expectation by a sample average. Let Xi​=g(Ui​). These are i.i.d. (functions of i.i.d. variables) with mean θ. The SLLN says the average converges to the mean:

θ^n​=n1​i=1∑n​g(Ui​)  n→∞ ​ θ​

L17 makes "→" precise: it holds with probability 1.

How accurate is it? (Using L17 and L18.) θ^n​ is unbiased, E[θ^n​]=θ, and

Var(θ^n​)=nVar(g(U))​,so the typical error isn​σg​​,σg2​=Var(g(U)).

Ten times more accuracy costs a hundred times more samples. The n​1​ rate does not depend on the dimension, which is why Monte Carlo is the method of choice for high-dimensional integrals.

Worked example. θ=∫01​exdx=e−1≈1.7183. Here Var(eU)=E[e2U]−(e−1)2=2e2−1​−(e−1)2≈0.2420, so with n=1000 samples the typical error is 0.2420/1000​≈0.016.

Worked example with no closed form. θ=∫01​ex2dx≈1.46265 has no antiderivative in elementary functions, but the estimator n1​∑eUi2​ is one line of code. A run with n=105 gave 1.4635.

u = np.random.uniform(0, 1, 100000)
theta_hat = np.mean(np.exp(u**2))

HW part 1: ∫ab​g(x)dx. Two equivalent ways.

  • Substitute x=a+(b−a)u, so dx=(b−a)du:
∫ab​g(x)dx=(b−a)∫01​g(a+(b−a)u)du=(b−a)E[g(a+(b−a)U)].
  • Equivalently, V=a+(b−a)U∼U[a,b] has density b−a1​, so ∫ab​g=(b−a)E[g(V)].

Estimator: θ^n​=nb−a​∑i=1n​g(a+(b−a)Ui​).

HW part 2: ∫0∞​g(x)dx. Map (0,∞) onto (0,1) with a substitution.

  • Ross's substitution y=1+x1​, so x=y1​−1 and dx=−y2dy​:
∫0∞​g(x)dx=∫01​y2g(y1​−1)​dy=E[U2g(1/U−1)​].
  • Log substitution x=−lny, so dx=−ydy​:
∫0∞​g(x)dx=∫01​yg(−lny)​dy=E[Ug(−lnU)​].
  • Or use a density on (0,∞) directly (this is importance sampling, slide 18). With Y∼ Exp(1): ∫0∞​g(x)dx=E[g(Y)eY].

Check: ∫0∞​e−x2dx=2π​​≈0.8862. The first substitution with 2×105 samples gave 0.8871.

Last year's HW6 Q8: I2​=∫0∞​e−xdx with N uniform samples. With the log substitution, ug(−lnu)​=uelnu​=uu​=1. Every sample gives exactly 1, so the estimate is I^2​=1 with zero variance. This happens because the substitution's density matches the integrand exactly, which is the ideal case of importance sampling below. Ross's substitution also works, I^2​=N1​∑Ui2​e−(1/Ui​−1)​, but with non-zero variance.

Slide 18/23: Importance sampling

On the slide. Suppose you want to compute E[h(X)] where X has pdf f(⋅). Assume you do not have samples from X but you know f(⋅). Now suppose you have access to samples from a random variable Y with pdf g(⋅). How will you use i.i.d. samples of Y to compute E[h(X)]?

E[h(X)]=∫h(x)f(x)dx=∫g(y)h(y)f(y)​g(y)dy=EY​[g(Y)h(Y)f(Y)​].

Now use the LLN and samples of Y to estimate E[h(X)].

The trick in the middle line. Multiply and divide by g. The integral is unchanged, but it now reads as the expectation of the function g(y)h(y)f(y)​ under the density g, which we can sample from. (The dummy variable is renamed from x to y to stress that the samples come from Y.)

The estimator. Draw Y1​,…,Yn​ i.i.d. from g and average:

E[h(X)]​=n1​i=1∑n​h(Yi​)w(Yi​),w(y)=g(y)f(y)​​

The w(Yi​) are the importance weights (or likelihood ratios). They correct for sampling from the "wrong" distribution: a point that g over-samples relative to f gets weight below 1, and a point it under-samples gets weight above 1. By the SLLN the estimator converges to EY​[h(Y)w(Y)]=E[h(X)].

The one condition you must check: support. The middle step divides by g(y), so we need

g(y)>0whereverh(y)f(y)=0.

If g is zero on part of the region where hf lives, that part of the integral is simply never sampled, and the estimator converges to the wrong number. Last year's Tutorial 6 Q7, in Part B below, is exactly this trap.

Why it is called "importance" sampling. Even when you can sample from f, sampling from a different g can give a far smaller variance. The idea is to put the samples where ∣h(x)∣f(x) is large, the "important" region, and correct with the weights. The best possible choice is g∝∣h∣f. When h≥0 this gives zero variance: with g=hf/E[h(X)], every term h(Yi​)w(Yi​) equals E[h(X)]. You cannot use that g in practice, because it needs the answer to normalise it, but it tells you what to aim for. HW6 Q8 above was an instance of it.

Worked example: a rare event. Estimate θ=P(Z>5) for Z∼N(0,1). The true value is θ≈2.87×10−7.

  • Plain Monte Carlo averages 1{Zi​>5}. With a million samples you expect 0.29 hits, so the estimate is almost always 0. Useless.
  • Importance sampling with Y∼N(5,1), centred on the important region. With h(y)=1{y>5}:
w(y)=g(y)f(y)​=e−(y−5)2/2e−y2/2​=e−5y+12.5,θ^=n1​i=1∑n​1{Yi​>5}e−5Yi​+12.5.

About half of the Yi​ exceed 5, and each contributes a tiny weight. A run with n=105 gave 2.88×10−7, within 1% of the truth.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

Discrete inverse transform

B1 (last year's Tutorial 6 Q5). pX​(1)=0.4, pX​(5)=0.3, pX​(10)=0.3. Describe the inverse transform method and give the ranges of u.

Solution

Solution. The CDF values are F(1)=0.4, F(5)=0.7, F(10)=1. Draw u∼U[0,1] and set

X=⎩⎨⎧​1510​0≤u<0.40.4≤u<0.70.7≤u≤1​

Each interval's length equals the probability of its value. For example, u=0.55 gives X=5.

B2 (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Example 4). P(X=1)=0.35, P(X=2)=0.15, P(X=3)=0.4, P(X=4)=0.1.

Solution

Solution. The intervals are [0,0.35)→1, [0.35,0.5)→2, [0.5,0.9)→3, [0.9,1)→4. (Ordering for speed: checking 3 first, then 1, then 2, then 4 cuts the expected number of comparisons from 0.35+2(0.15)+3(0.4)+4(0.1)=2.25 to 0.4+2(0.35)+3(0.15)+4(0.1)=1.95.)

B3 (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Examples 1 and 3): Bernoulli and binomial. Bernoulli(p): X=1{U<p}, so P(X=1)=P(U<p)=p. Binomial(n,p): add n independent Bernoullis, X=∑i=1n​1{Ui​<p}. This uses L14's fact that a binomial is a sum of i.i.d. Bernoullis.

B4 (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Exercise 1): Geometric(p) in closed form. The inverse transform for a geometric can be solved exactly. Since P(X>k)=(1−p)k, we have F(k)=1−(1−p)k. The rule "smallest k with F(k)≥u" becomes "smallest k with (1−p)k≤1−u", that is, k≥ln(1−p)ln(1−u)​ (dividing by ln(1−p)<0 flips the inequality). So

X=⌈ln(1−p)ln(1−U)​⌉,or equivalently (with probability 1)X=⌊ln(1−p)ln(1−U)​⌋+1.

Check of the second form: X>k⟺ln(1−p)ln(1−U)​≥k⟺1−U≤(1−p)k, which has probability (1−p)k. ✓ A negative binomial (trials until the r-th success) is a sum of r independent geometrics.

B5 (probabilitycourse Ch. 14, Exercise 2 and Example 7): Poisson(λ), two ways.

(a) Inverse transform with an infinite support. Walk along p0​=e−λ, p1​,p2​,…, adding up the CDF until it exceeds u. The recursion pi+1​=i+1λ​pi​ avoids computing factorials.

(b) Counting exponential arrivals. Arrivals of a Poisson process of rate λ have i.i.d. Exp(λ) gaps Ti​=−λ1​lnUi​ (slide 15). The number of arrivals in [0,1] is Poisson(λ), so

X=max{j:T1​+⋯+Tj​≤1}=max{j:U1​U2​⋯Uj​≥e−λ}.

The second form holds because −λ1​ln(U1​⋯Uj​)≤1⟺U1​⋯Uj​≥e−λ. In words: keep multiplying uniforms until the product drops below e−λ; the number of factors before that is X. (A simulation with λ=2 matched the Poisson pmf to 3 decimals.)

Continuous inverse transform

B16 (last year's Tutorial 6 Q7): the support trap. Estimate θ=E[X2] for X∼ Exp(1) by importance sampling with N samples Yi​∼U[0,5].

B18: choosing the proposal (exam-style reasoning). To estimate P(X>20) for X∼ Exp(1) (true value e−20≈2×10−9), a good proposal is the shifted exponential Y=20+E with E∼ Exp(1).
