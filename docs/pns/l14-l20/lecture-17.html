Lecture 17

Sure and almost sure convergence, the SLLN and the sample mean

Deck B, slides 11 to 19 · 23 min read · 11 practice problems

Solutions stay hidden until you open them.

Big picture. L16 ended with the question "what does Xn​→X mean when each Xn​ is random?". This lecture gives the first two answers, both of which look at the sequence outcome by outcome:

  • Sure convergence: Xn​(ω)→X(ω) for every outcome ω.
  • Almost sure convergence: the outcomes where it fails have probability 0.

The headline example of almost sure convergence is the strong law of large numbers (SLLN), which justifies Monte Carlo (L15) and the use of the sample mean as an estimator. The lecture ends with the main tool for proving almost sure convergence (Borel–Cantelli) and a warning (almost sure convergence does not make the means converge).

Part A: the slides

Slide 11/30: Recap

On the slide. Convergence of Xn​→X. X could even be a deterministic number. The Xn​'s could be dependent on each other. Each Xn​ could have a different law (pmf/pdf).

This repeats the last slide of L16; see the examples there.

Slide 12/30: Pointwise or sure convergence

On the slide. {Xn​,n≥0} converges to X pointwise or surely if for all ω∈Ω we have limn→∞​Xn​(ω)=X(ω). Example: Ω={H,T},

Xn​={n1​1+n1​​if ω=Hif ω=T​andX={01​if ω=Hif ω=T.​

The definition is slide 8 of L16, applied on Ω. A random variable is a function on Ω. Sure convergence is pointwise convergence of the functions Xn​:Ω→R to the function X:Ω→R. Probability plays no role at all.

The example. One coin toss decides everything. There are only two outcomes, so check both.

  • ω=H: the realized sequence is 1,21​,31​,⋯→0=X(H). ✓
  • ω=T: the realized sequence is 2,23​,34​,⋯→1=X(T). ✓

Every outcome gives a convergent sequence with the right limit, so Xn​→X surely. Here the convergence is even uniform on Ω: ∣Xn​(ω)−X(ω)∣=n1​ for both outcomes.

Note the structure. Xn​=X+n1​. The randomness is decided once (by the toss), and then the sequence moves deterministically towards X. Whether the coin is fair does not matter, which shows that sure convergence ignores probabilities.

Slide 13/30: Almost sure convergence

On the slide. Xn​ converges to X almost surely if P(ω∈Ω:limn→∞​Xn​(ω)=X(ω))=1. The set of outcomes where convergence does not happen has measure 0: P{ω∈Ω:limn​Xn​(ω)=X(ω)}=0. Example: Ω=[0,1], pick a number uniformly in [0,1]. Let Xn​(ω)=ωn and X(ω)=0 for all ω. Xn​(ω)→X(ω) for ω∈[0,1). Xn​(ω)→X(ω) for ω=1, and P{ω=1} [=0]. This is almost sure convergence as P{[0,1)}=1.

(The slide's line "and P{ω=1}" is missing "=0".)

Reading the definition. Collect all the lucky outcomes into one event,

C={ω:Xn​(ω)→X(ω)}.

Almost sure convergence says P(C)=1, or equivalently that the unlucky outcomes Cc form an event of probability 0. Unlucky outcomes may exist; they just carry no probability. Notation: Xn​a.s.​X, also called convergence with probability 1 (w.p.1).

The example. This is the function xn from L16 slide 8, now viewed as a random variable on Ω=[0,1].

  • For ω∈[0,1), ωn→0. These outcomes are lucky.
  • For ω=1, 1n=1→0. This outcome is unlucky.
  • So C=[0,1), and with the uniform probability P(C)=1−0=1.

Hence Xn​a.s.​0, but Xn​ does not converge to 0 surely, because of the single outcome ω=1.

Sure versus almost sure. Sure convergence implies almost sure convergence (C=Ω has probability 1), and the example shows the converse fails. In probability, almost sure is the natural notion, because changing random variables on an event of probability 0 changes no probability, expectation or distribution. That is why sure convergence is rarely used after this slide.

A useful equivalent form. Xn​a.s.​X means: with probability 1, for every ϵ>0, ∣Xn​−X∣<ϵ for all large enough n. Equivalently, for each ϵ>0, the event "∣Xn​−X∣≥ϵ happens for infinitely many n" has probability 0. Slide 18 builds on this form.

Slide 14/30: Example 2, the strong law of large numbers

On the slide. Let {Xn​,n≥0} be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with mean μ and Sn​=∑i=1n​Xi​. Then nSn​​→μ a.s. Toss a biased coin (probability of heads μ) repeatedly. What is ω and Ω? Let Xi​ be the outcome of the i-th toss and Sn​ the number of heads in n tosses. The empirical mean is nSn​​.

X1​,X2​,… i.i.d., E∣X1​∣<∞, μ=E[X1​]⟹μ^​n​=nSn​​a.s.​μ​

The precise condition is that the mean exists and is finite, E∣X1​∣<∞. No variance is needed.

What is ω and Ω? (the blue question). The SLLN is a statement about the whole infinite sequence of tosses, so an outcome must record all of them.

  • An outcome ω is one infinite sequence of tosses, for example ω=(H,T,T,H,H,…).
  • The sample space is the set of all such sequences, Ω={H,T}N={(ω1​,ω2​,…):ωi​∈{H,T}}. This set is uncountable.
  • The random variables read off coordinates: Xi​(ω)=1 if ωi​=H, else 0. Sn​(ω) is the number of H's among the first n coordinates.

The SLLN then says

P({ω:nSn​(ω)​→μ})=1.

The unlucky outcomes. ω=(H,H,H,…) has nSn​​=1 for every n, so it is unlucky if μ<1. The block sequence from L16 (alternating blocks of doubling length) has no limit at all. There are uncountably many unlucky sequences, and yet their total probability is 0. (Indeed, for 0<μ<1 every single sequence has probability 0, since P(first n tosses match)≤max(μ,1−μ)n→0. "Probability 0" does not mean "impossible".)

Why the SLLN matters in this course.

  • It is the justification of Monte Carlo (L15 slide 17): the i.i.d. values g(Ui​) have finite mean θ, so their average converges to θ with probability 1.
  • It makes "probability = long-run frequency" a theorem: with Xi​=1{A happens on trial i}, the fraction of trials on which A happens converges to P(A) almost surely.
  • It justifies the sample mean as an estimator (slide 15).

When it fails. Without a finite mean, the SLLN can fail. For i.i.d. Cauchy samples, nSn​​ is again standard Cauchy for every n (a known property of the Cauchy distribution), so it never settles down. This is the simulation of L15 Part B, B10.

Slide 15/30: Detour, the sample mean as an estimator

On the slide. Now that we know nSn​​→μ, we can use μ^​n​:=nSn​​ as an "estimator" for the mean, especially when the underlying distribution is not known. The estimator μ^​n​ is a random variable. What is its CDF? What are its mean and variance? μ^​n​ is an "unbiased estimator" since E[μ^​n​]=μ. Var(μ^​n​)=nσ2​. We will soon see the CLT, which will tell us the CDF of μ^​n​ without any information on the law of the Xi​.

The estimator is random. Each data set gives one number μ^​n​(ω); a different data set gives a different number. So it makes sense to ask for its distribution.

Mean. By linearity of expectation (no independence needed):

E[μ^​n​]=n1​i=1∑n​E[Xi​]=n1​⋅nμ=μ.

An estimator whose expected value equals the quantity it estimates is called unbiased: it is right on average.

Variance. Now use independence (variances of independent variables add; toolkit fact 5) and Var(aY)=a2Var(Y):

Var(μ^​n​)=n21​Var(i=1∑n​Xi​)=n21​⋅nσ2=nσ2​.

So the standard error is n​σ​. To halve the typical error you need four times as much data.

E[μ^​n​]=μ,Var(μ^​n​)=nσ2​,SD(μ^​n​)=n​σ​​

What is its CDF? Exactly, it depends on the law of the Xi​, and it is known only in special cases:

Xi​ distribution of Sn​=nμ^​n​ (from)
Bernoulli(p) Binomial(n,p) L14 slide 6
Poisson(λ) Poisson(nλ) L14 slide 5
Exp(λ) Gamma(n,λ) L14 slide 6
N(μ,σ2) N(nμ,nσ2) L14 slide 5

The CLT (L18) gives the approximate answer in every case: μ^​n​≈N(μ,nσ2​) for large n.

Slide 16/30: Detour, the incremental formula for the sample mean

On the slide. Given μ^​n​, suppose you see an additional sample Xn+1​. How will you compute μ^​n+1​? Naive way: μ^​n+1​=n+1∑i=1n+1​Xi​​. There is an incremental formula that uses μ^​n​:

μ^​n+1​=μ^​n​+n+11​[Xn+1​−μ^​n​].

Such averaging formulas are used extensively in reinforcement learning.

Derivation. Write the new sum in terms of the old mean, Sn+1​=Sn​+Xn+1​=nμ^​n​+Xn+1​:

μ^​n+1​=n+1nμ^​n​+Xn+1​​=n+1(n+1)μ^​n​−μ^​n​+Xn+1​​=μ^​n​+n+11​(Xn+1​−μ^​n​).

Reading it. new estimate = old estimate + step size × (new observation − old estimate). The bracket is the "surprise" or error. The estimate moves a fraction n+11​ of the way towards the new data point. As n grows the steps shrink, so the estimate settles down (consistent with the SLLN).

Why it is useful. The naive way stores all the data and costs O(n) per update. The incremental way stores two numbers (μ^​n​ and n) and costs O(1) per update. In reinforcement learning, value estimates are updated as Q←Q+α(target−Q). With α=n+11​ this is exactly the running mean. With a constant α it becomes an exponentially weighted average, which forgets old data and can track a changing environment.

Quick example. Data 4,8,6,10: μ^​1​=4; μ^​2​=4+21​(8−4)=6; μ^​3​=6+31​(6−6)=6; μ^​4​=6+41​(10−6)=7. Check: 44+8+6+10​=7. ✓

Slide 17/30: Recap, modes of convergence

On the slide. Sure convergence (box). Almost sure convergence (box). SLLN: {Xn​} i.i.d. with mean μ and Sn​=∑Xi​; then μ^​n​:=nSn​​→μ a.s. The estimator μ^​n​ has mean μ and variance nσ2​. μ^​n+1​=μ^​n​+n+11​[Xn+1​−μ^​n​].

Nothing new; the summary is that sure ⇒ almost sure, the SLLN is an almost sure statement, and the sample mean is unbiased with variance nσ2​.

Slide 18/30: The Borel–Cantelli lemma

On the slide. Self-study: Theorem 7.5 (probabilitycourse.com). Consider a sequence of random variables X1​,X2​,…. If for all ϵ we have ∑n=1∞​P(∣Xn​−X∣>ϵ)<∞, then Xn​→X a.s. This is only a sufficient condition for almost sure convergence! Thm 7.6 (HW) gives necessary and sufficient conditions. Lots of problems in probabilitycourse; practice them!

Theorem 7.5 in words. If the probabilities of an ϵ-deviation are summable, for every ϵ, the sequence converges almost surely. Summable means they shrink fast enough, for example like n21​ or e−n, but not like n1​.

n=1∑∞​P(∣Xn​−X∣>ϵ)<∞  for every ϵ>0⟹Xn​a.s.​X​

Why it is true (the self-study proof). Fix ϵ and let En​={∣Xn​−X∣>ϵ}.

  1. Tail bound. For any m, by the union bound (toolkit fact 8),
P(n≥m⋃​En​)≤n≥m∑​P(En​)m→∞​0,

because the tail of a convergent series goes to 0. 2. "Infinitely often" has probability 0. If En​ happens for infinitely many n, then for every m some En​ with n≥m happens. So P(En​ infinitely often)≤P(⋃n≥m​En​) for every m, and letting m→∞ gives 0. This step is the (first) Borel–Cantelli lemma: if ∑n​P(En​)<∞ then, with probability 1, only finitely many of the En​ happen. 3. From one ϵ to all. So, with probability 1, ∣Xn​−X∣≤ϵ for all large n. Do this for ϵ=1,21​,31​,…. A countable union of probability-0 events has probability 0, so with probability 1 the statement holds for every ϵ=k1​ at once, which is exactly Xn​(ω)→X(ω). □

Only sufficient. Slide 19's example converges almost surely although ∑n​P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=∑n1​=∞. The condition can fail and the conclusion still hold.

Theorem 7.6 (the HW): a necessary and sufficient condition. For ϵ>0 define

Am​={∣Xn​−X∣<ϵ for all n≥m},

the event that from time m on, the sequence stays within ϵ of X. Then

Xn​a.s.​X⟺m→∞lim​P(Am​)=1  for every ϵ>0​

Proof. The events grow with m: A1​⊆A2​⊆⋯ (if you stay close from time m, you stay close from time m+1). Their union ⋃m​Am​ is the event "eventually always within ϵ". By continuity of probability (toolkit fact 8),

m→∞lim​P(Am​)=P(m⋃​Am​)=P(eventually always within ϵ).

(⇒) On the event C where Xn​→X, the sequence is eventually within ϵ, so C⊆⋃m​Am​ and the probability is 1. (⇐) If the probability is 1 for each ϵ=k1​, intersect these countably many probability-1 events. On the intersection, for every k the sequence is eventually within k1​, so it converges. □

A companion fact: the second Borel–Cantelli lemma. If the events En​ are independent and ∑n​P(En​)=∞, then with probability 1 infinitely many of them happen. This is the tool for proving that a sequence does not converge almost surely; it is used on slide 20 (L18) and in Part B.

Slide 19/30: Another example of a.s. convergence

On the slide. Consider a uniform random variable U and define Xn​=n1{U≤n1​}​: Xn​=n when U≤n1​ and Xn​=0 otherwise. Given a realization of U, what can you say about the sequence {Xn​}? Once an Xn​ is zero, all higher-indexed variables are also zero! This happens for all realizations other than U=0. In that case, since 0≤n1​ for all n, the Xn​'s run off to infinity and we don't see convergence to 0. But P(U=0)=0. Does E[Xn​]→0? Almost sure convergence does not imply that the means converge!

The realized sequences. Fix U=u.

  • If u>0: Xn​=n while n≤u1​, and Xn​=0 for every n>u1​. For example, u=0.3 gives 1,2,3,0,0,0,… So Xn​(u)→0.
  • If u=0: Xn​=n for all n, so Xn​→∞.

The only unlucky outcome is u=0, and P(U=0)=0, so Xn​a.s.​0. (It is not sure convergence, because of u=0.)

The means. E[Xn​]=n⋅P(U≤n1​)=n⋅n1​=1 for every n. So

E[Xn​]=1→0=E[limXn​].

Why the means do not follow. Each Xn​ is 0 with probability 1−n1​ but takes a huge value n with the small probability n1​. The rare large value keeps the mean at 1 ("mass escaping to infinity"). Almost sure convergence looks at each outcome separately and does not see this, because for each fixed u>0 the big values stop after a while. Convergence in mean (L18) is the mode that does control this, and it fails here.

Connection with slide 18. ∑n​P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=∑n​n1​=∞ (for 0<ϵ<1), so Theorem 7.5 cannot be used, and yet Xn​→0 a.s. Theorem 7.6 does work: Am​={Xn​=0 for all n≥m}={U>m1​}, so P(Am​)=1−m1​→1. ✓

The same formula with independent uniforms Un​ (one per n) behaves completely differently; that is the next slide, at the start of L18.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (probabilitycourse 7.2.7, Example 1). A fair coin is tossed once. Xn​=n+1n​ if H, and Xn​=(−1)n if T. Does Xn​ converge almost surely?

Solution

Solution. On H: 21​,32​,43​,⋯→1. On T: −1,1,−1,… has no limit. So P(limXn​=1)=P(H)=21​. The set where the sequence converges has probability 21​<1, so Xn​ does not converge almost surely (to anything).

B2 (probabilitycourse 7.2.7, Example 2). Ω=[0,1] with the uniform probability. Xn​(s)=1 if 0≤s<2nn+1​ and 0 otherwise; X(s)=1 if 0≤s<21​ and 0 otherwise. Show Xn​a.s.​X.

Solution

Solution. Note 2nn+1​=21​+2n1​, which decreases to 21​.

  • s<21​: Xn​(s)=1=X(s) for all n. ✓
  • s>21​: Xn​(s)=0=X(s) as soon as 21​+2n1​≤s, that is, n≥2s−11​. ✓
  • s=21​: Xn​(21​)=1 for all n, but X(21​)=0. ✗

The failure set is {21​}, of probability 0, so Xn​→X almost surely.

B3 (probabilitycourse 7.2.7, Example 3). Xn​=±n1​ with probability 21​ each. Show Xn​a.s.​0.

Solution

Solution. ∣Xn​∣=n1​ always, so P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=0 once n≥ϵ1​. The series ∑n​P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ) has at most ⌊ϵ1​⌋ non-zero terms, so it is finite, and Theorem 7.5 applies. (Here the convergence is even sure: ∣Xn​(ω)∣≤n1​ for every ω.)

B4 (probabilitycourse 7.2.7, Example 4; last year's HW8 Q1(b)). Xn​ independent, Xn​∼ Bernoulli(n1​). Does Xn​→0 almost surely?

Solution

Solution. Theorem 7.5 does not apply: for 0<ϵ<1, ∑n​P(Xn​=1)=∑n​n1​=∞. Use Theorem 7.6. For 0<ϵ<1, Am​={Xn​=0 for all n≥m}. For any N≥m, by independence,

P(Am​)≤P(Xm​=0,…,XN​=0)=mm−1​⋅m+1m​⋯NN−1​=Nm−1​N→∞​0.

The product telescopes. So P(Am​)=0 for every m, and limm​P(Am​)=0=1: no almost sure convergence. With probability 1, the sequence contains infinitely many 1s. (The second Borel–Cantelli lemma gives the same answer in one line: the events {Xn​=1} are independent with ∑n1​=∞.) Compare slide 19: the same marginal probabilities n1​, but there the events were nested (all driven by one U), and that made the difference.

B5 (last year's HW8 Q10 and HW7 Q4). Yn​∼ Bernoulli(n+1n​) independent, and Xn+1​=Y1​Y2​⋯Yn​. Show Xn​a.s.​0 (and hence in probability).

Solution

Solution. Xn+1​=1 only if all of Y1​,…,Yn​ equal 1:

P(Xn+1​=1)=k=1∏n​k+1k​=21​⋅32​⋯n+1n​=n+11​→0.

The sequence is non-increasing (multiplying by 0 or 1), so once it hits 0 it stays at 0. Therefore Am​={Xn​=0 for all n≥m}={Xm​=0}, and P(Am​)=1−m1​→1. By Theorem 7.6, Xn​→0 almost surely. (Equivalently, the only way to avoid convergence is Yk​=1 for every k, which has probability limn+11​=0.) Again ∑n​P(Xn​=1)=∑n1​=∞, so Theorem 7.5 is no help.

B6 (probabilitycourse 7.2.8, Problem 2). X1​,X2​,… i.i.d. U(0,1) and Yn​=min(X1​,…,Xn​). Show Yn​a.s.​0.

Solution

Solution. For 0<ϵ<1: P(Yn​>ϵ)=P(all Xi​>ϵ)=(1−ϵ)n. This is a geometric series, ∑n​(1−ϵ)n=ϵ1−ϵ​<∞, so Theorem 7.5 gives Yn​→0 almost surely. (For ϵ≥1 the probabilities are 0.)

B7 (probabilitycourse 7.2.8, Problem 3). Xn​∼N(0,n1​). Show Xn​a.s.​0.

Solution

Solution. Xn​ has the same law as n​Z​, so P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=2(1−Φ(ϵn​)). Use the Gaussian tail bound 1−Φ(x)≤21​e−x2/2 for x≥0:

n∑​P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)≤n∑​e−ϵ2n/2=n∑​(e−ϵ2/2)n<∞,

a geometric series. Theorem 7.5 gives almost sure convergence. Note that no independence between the Xn​ was needed; Theorem 7.5 never needs it.

B8 (probabilitycourse 7.2.8, Problem 4). On Ω=[0,1] with uniform probability, Xn​(s)=n+1n​s+(1−s)n and X(s)=s. Show Xn​a.s.​X.

Solution

Solution. For 0<s≤1: n+1n​s→s and (1−s)n→0, so Xn​(s)→s. At s=0: Xn​(0)=0+1=1→1=0=X(0). The failure set {0} has probability 0.

B9 (Bertsekas Problem 13; last year's Tutorial 8 Q7). (a) If Xn​→a and Yn​→b almost surely, show Xn​+Yn​→a+b almost surely. (b) Find limn→∞​∑i=1n​Xi​∑i=1n​Xi2​​ for i.i.d. Xi​∼U[0,1].

Solution

Solution. (a) Let A and B be the events where Xn​→a and Yn​→b. Both have probability 0. Outside A∪B both sequences converge, so the sum converges to a+b. The failure set of the sum is inside A∪B, and P(A∪B)≤P(A)+P(B)=0. The same argument works for products and for ratios (when b=0), or for any continuous function of the two limits. (b) Divide top and bottom by n. By the SLLN, n1​∑Xi2​→E[X2]=31​ and n1​∑Xi​→E[X]=21​, almost surely. By (a) applied to the ratio,

∑Xi​∑Xi2​​a.s.​1/21/3​=32​.

B10 (Bertsekas Problem 14). Xi​ i.i.d. and Yi​ i.i.d., with finite means and E[Y]=0. Does Zn​=Y1​+⋯+Yn​X1​+⋯+Xn​​ converge almost surely?

Solution

Solution. Yes: Zn​=(Y1​+⋯+Yn​)/n(X1​+⋯+Xn​)/n​→E[Y]E[X]​ almost surely, by the SLLN on the top and the bottom and B9(a). (No independence between the two sequences is needed.)

B11 (exam-style contrast with slide 19). Let U1​,U2​,… be independent uniforms and Xn​=n21{Un​≤n21​}. Does Xn​→0 almost surely? What is E[Xn​]?

Solution

Solution. P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=n21​ for 0<ϵ<1, and ∑n21​=6π2​<∞. By Theorem 7.5, Xn​→0 almost surely, even though the Un​ are independent. Yet E[Xn​]=n2⋅n21​=1 for every n. With n1​ instead of n21​ (slide 20 of L18), the sum diverges and, by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, almost sure convergence fails.

B12 (Bertsekas Problems 16 and 18, optional): the SLLN with a finite fourth moment. This is the standard short proof, assuming E[Xi4​]<∞ and (after subtracting the mean) E[Xi​]=0.

  1. A criterion (Problem 16). If Yn​≥0 and ∑n​E[Yn​]<∞, then Yn​→0 almost surely. Reason: E[∑n​Yn​]=∑n​E[Yn​]<∞, so ∑n​Yn​<∞ with probability 1, and the terms of a convergent series tend to 0.
  2. The fourth moment of the sum. Expand E[Sn4​]=∑i,j,k,l​E[Xi​Xj​Xk​Xl​]. Any term with an index that appears exactly once vanishes, because E[Xi​]=0 and independence lets you factor it out. What is left: n terms E[Xi4​] and 3n(n−1) terms E[Xi2​Xj2​] with i=j. So E[Sn4​]≤Cn2 for a constant C (Bertsekas gets C=3E[X4]).
  3. Apply the criterion to Yn​=(nSn​​)4: ∑n​E[Yn​]≤∑n​n4Cn2​=C∑n​n21​<∞. So (nSn​​)4→0, hence nSn​​→0, almost surely. □

The general SLLN (finite mean only) needs a harder proof, which is beyond the course.

B13 (concept check). True or false? (a) If Xn​→X surely then Xn​→X almost surely. (b) If ∑n​P(∣Xn​∣>ϵ)=∞ for some ϵ, then Xn​→0 almost surely. (c) If Xn​a.s.​X then E[Xn​]→E[X].

Answers. (a) True (C=Ω). (b) False in general: slide 19 and B5 are counterexamples; it becomes true when the events {∣Xn​∣>ϵ} are independent (second Borel–Cantelli). (c) False: slide 19.
