Part 1 — L21

What does "estimating" even mean?

6 min read

Slide 2: probability vs statistics

The slide's picture is the whole course in one diagram:

  • Probability: model → data. "If the coin has P(H)=0.6, what's the chance of 7 heads in 10 tosses?"
  • Statistics: data → model. "I saw 7 heads in 10 tosses. What's P(H)?"

The second question is harder, because the answer is never certain. Any P(H) strictly between 0 and 1 could have produced 7 heads. Statistics is about making a good guess and being honest about how good it is.

Slide 3: three kinds of answers

You want to learn some unknown θ∗ (a mean, a variance, a rate, whatever) from data D={x1​,…,xn​}. There are three kinds of answer you can give:

  1. Point estimate: one number. "θ∗≈0.7." This is what L21–L24 are about.
  2. Interval estimate: a range plus a confidence. "θ∗∈[0.55,0.85] with 95% confidence."
  3. Hypothesis test: a yes/no decision between candidate values. "Is θ=0.5 or θ=0.7?"

Slides 4–5: Bayesian vs frequentist

This is the philosophical split that the rest of the course is built on, so it's worth getting straight now.

Frequentist (classical). θ∗ is a fixed, unknown constant. There's nothing random about it. The randomness is all in the data. You build an estimator Θ^=h(X1​,…,Xn​), and because the Xi​ are random, Θ^ is a random variable. You then ask: on average, over many repeated datasets, how close is Θ^ to θ∗?

Bayesian. You don't know θ∗, so you model your uncertainty about it as a random variable Θ with a prior pΘ​(θ). You see data, and use Bayes' rule to update the prior into a posterior pΘ∣X​(θ∣x). The prior and posterior describe your state of knowledge, not some physical randomness in θ∗.

The quick way to remember it: frequentists put the randomness in the data, Bayesians put it in the parameter too.

L21–L22 are frequentist. L23–L24 are Bayesian.

Slide 6: estimator vs estimate

This distinction trips people up in proofs, so be careful with it:

  • Estimator Θ^=h(X1​,…,Xn​): a recipe, and a random variable. You can take its expectation, variance, and so on.
  • Estimate θ^=h(x1​,…,xn​): the number you get when you run the recipe on your actual data.

Example: the sample mean μ^​n​=n1​∑i=1n​Xi​ is an estimator of the true mean μ. If your data is {2,4,9}, the estimate is 5.

When a question says "is this estimator unbiased?", it's asking about the random variable, so capital letters.

Slide 7: bias

B(Θ^)=E[Θ^]−θ∗​

Bias asks: if I repeated the experiment a zillion times and averaged my estimates, would I land on the truth? If yes (B=0), the estimator is unbiased.

The slide then asks the key question: are all unbiased estimators good? Consider two estimators of the mean μ:

  • Θ^1​=X1​ (just use the first sample and throw the rest away)
  • Θ^2​=μ^​n​ (the sample mean)

Both are unbiased: E[X1​]=μ and E[μ^​n​]=μ. But obviously Θ^1​ is terrible: it ignores n−1 data points! The difference shows up in the variance:

Var(Θ^1​)=σ2,Var(Θ^2​)=nσ2​.

So "unbiased" alone tells you nothing about how spread out the guesses are. We need a measure that captures both.

Slide 8: mean squared error and the bias–variance decomposition

MSE(Θ^)=E[(Θ^−θ∗)2]=Var(Θ^)+B(Θ^)2​

Why the decomposition holds. This is exactly the identity E[Y2]=Var(Y)+(E[Y])2 with Y=Θ^−θ∗. Since θ∗ is a constant, Var(Θ^−θ∗)=Var(Θ^), and E[Θ^−θ∗]=B(Θ^). Done. (Last year's Endsem Q10(b) asked for exactly this, with the "explain the tradeoff" part. Know it cold.)

The punchline: a biased estimator can beat an unbiased one if its variance is small enough. That's the bias–variance tradeoff, and it's a big deal in ML.

Slide 8 exercise: MSE of Θ^1​=X1​ and Θ^2​=μ^​n​+1

  • Θ^1​=X1​: unbiased, so MSE=Var(X1​)=σ2.
  • Θ^2​=μ^​n​+1: E[Θ^2​]=μ+1, so the bias is 1. The variance is Var(μ^​n​)=σ2/n (adding a constant doesn't change variance). So
MSE(Θ^2​)=nσ2​+1.

So which wins? Θ^2​ beats Θ^1​ exactly when nσ2​+1<σ2, i.e. when σ2>n−1n​. If the data is very noisy, a biased-but-stable estimator wins. If σ2 is tiny, the bias of 1 dominates and you're better off with X1​. Neither is better in general; it depends on the true parameters. (That's a recurring annoyance in frequentist stats: the "best" estimator often depends on the thing you don't know.)

Slide 9: consistency

What happens as you collect more and more data? You'd hope your estimator closes in on the truth. That's consistency:

Θ^n​ is consistent if n→∞lim​P(∣Θ^n​−θ∗∣≥ϵ)=0 for every ϵ>0​

That's just Θ^n​p​θ∗, convergence in probability from L18. If you have Θ^n​a.s.​θ∗ instead, it's called strongly consistent (and since a.s. ⇒ in probability, strongly consistent ⇒ consistent).

  • μ^​n​ is strongly consistent for μ: that's literally the SLLN.
  • "Pick a random Xi​ from the data" is not consistent. Its distribution is just the distribution of X, no matter how big n gets, so P(∣Xi​−μ∣≥ϵ) stays at some fixed positive number (unless X is a constant).

Careful: unbiased and consistent are different properties. Neither implies the other.

  • Unbiased but not consistent: Θ^n​=X1​. Unbiased for every n, but it never improves.
  • Consistent but biased: μ^​n​+n1​ (slide 20, coming up). Biased for every n, but the bias dies off and it converges.
