Part 6 — Linear regression (slides 1–36)

9 min read · 3 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Endsem syllabus. Only slides 1–36 of linear_regression.pdf are examinable: the model, least squares, the intercept and slope, least squares as the Gaussian MLE, the MLE of σ2, and the Laplace / least-absolute-deviations slide. Slides 37–56 are NOT in the syllabus (bias and MSE of β^​0​,β^​1​, the Bayesian view, the MAP estimate, and the posterior / CE estimate), so they're left out here.

The PDF has 56 pages because each slide builds up over several pages. Page ranges below are the PDF page numbers, so you can find each one quickly. The good news is that this deck is a reunion: least squares is just MLE from L22 applied to a line.

The model (slides 1–6)

You get data pairs D={(xi​,yi​)}i=1n​, for example hours studied and marks. The model is

Yi​=β0∗​+β1∗​xi​+εi​,

where β0∗​ (intercept) and β1∗​ (slope) are unknown. They are our θ∗. The noise terms ε1​,…,εn​ are i.i.d., independent of the xi​, with E[εi​]=0 and Var(εi​)=σ2.

So at each xi​, Yi​ is a random variable centred on the line:

E[Yi​∣Xi​=xi​]=β0∗​+β1∗​xi​,Var(Yi​∣Xi​=xi​)=σ2.

The picture on the slide shows this: a little distribution (blue) sitting on the line at every xi​, and the data point (red) is one draw from each.

Least squares (slides 7–11)

For a candidate line β0​+β1​x, the residual of point i is

ei​=yi​−β0​−β1​xi​,

the vertical gap between the point and the line. We want all residuals small together, so we add up their squares:

J(β0​,β1​)=i=1∑n​ei2​=i=1∑n​(yi​−β0​−β1​xi​)2.

The least squares estimates (β^​0​,β^​1​) are the values that minimise J.

The intercept (slides 12–15)

Write xˉ=n1​∑xi​ and yˉ​=n1​∑yi​. Two facts you'll use constantly:

i=1∑n​(xi​−xˉ)=0,i=1∑n​(yi​−yˉ​)=0.

Set both partial derivatives of J to zero:

∂β0​∂J​=−2i=1∑n​(yi​−β0​−β1​xi​)=0,∂β1​∂J​=−2i=1∑n​xi​(yi​−β0​−β1​xi​)=0.

Divide the first equation by −2n:

yˉ​−β0​−β1​xˉ=0⟹β0​=yˉ​−β1​xˉ.

Reading it. The fitted line always passes through the point (xˉ,yˉ​).

The slope (slides 16–20)

Substitute β0​=yˉ​−β1​xˉ into the second equation. It becomes

i=1∑n​xi​ci​=0,where ci​=(yi​−yˉ​)−β1​(xi​−xˉ).

Now the trick on the slide. The ci​ add up to zero, because both brackets do:

i∑​ci​=i∑​(yi​−yˉ​)−β1​i∑​(xi​−xˉ)=0,soi∑​xˉci​=0.

Subtract this from ∑i​xi​ci​=0:

i=1∑n​(xi​−xˉ)ci​=0⟹i=1∑n​(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​)=β1​i=1∑n​(xi​−xˉ)2.

With Sxx​=∑i​(xi​−xˉ)2 and Sxy​=∑i​(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​):

β^​1​=Sxx​Sxy​​,β^​0​=yˉ​−β^​1​xˉ​

Handy forms for calculation. Sxx​=∑xi2​−nxˉ2 and Sxy​=∑xi​yi​−nxˉyˉ​. These are much quicker with numbers.

Least squares is the MLE under Gaussian noise (slides 21–26)

Now assume εi​∼N(0,σ2) i.i.d. Then Y1​,…,Yn​ are independent with Yi​∼N(β0∗​+β1∗​xi​,σ2). The likelihood is

L(y1​,…,yn​;β0​,β1​)=i=1∏n​2πσ2​1​exp(−2σ2(yi​−β0​−β1​xi​)2​),

and taking logs,

lnL=−2n​ln(2πσ2)−2σ21​J(β0​,β1​).

The first term doesn't involve (β0​,β1​), and 2σ21​>0. So maximising lnL is the same as minimising J:

(β^​0​,β^​1​)ML​=argβ0​,β1​max​lnL=argβ0​,β1​min​J(β0​,β1​)​

giving β^​1,ML​=Sxy​/Sxx​ and β^​0,ML​=yˉ​−β^​1,ML​xˉ. It's the same thing we saw for the normal mean in Part 3 (maximising the Gaussian likelihood minimises a sum of squares), now with a line instead of a single number.

MLE of the noise variance (slides 27–31)

If σ2 is unknown too, the log likelihood is the same expression with σ2 as a third parameter:

lnL(β0​,β1​,σ2)=−2n​ln(2πσ2)−2σ21​J(β0​,β1​).

For every fixed σ2, the best (β0​,β1​) is still (β^​0​,β^​1​), since σ2 only scales J. So plug those in and maximise over σ2:

∂σ2∂lnL​=−2σ2n​+2σ4J(β^​0​,β^​1​)​=0⟹σ^ML2​=nJ(β^​0​,β^​1​)​=n1​i=1∑n​ei2​​,ei​=yi​−β^​0​−β^​1​xi​.

Like the divide-by-n sample variance, this is biased. The slide states (without proof) that n−21​∑i​ei2​ is unbiased. The intuition is the same as n−1 before: we estimated two parameters from the data, so we lose two degrees of freedom.

A different noise model gives a different loss (slides 32–36)

Suppose the noise is Laplace instead: fε​(e)=2b1​e−∣e∣/b. Then

lnL=−nln(2b)−b1​i=1∑n​∣ei​∣,ei​=yi​−β0​−β1​xi​,

so the MLE minimises ∑i​∣ei​∣. That's least absolute deviations (LAD).

The takeaway is that the noise model decides the loss: Gaussian noise gives squared error, Laplace noise gives absolute error. On the slide's picture with one outlier, squaring punishes the outlier heavily and drags the least squares line towards it, while LAD stays with the rest of the data. It's the mean-vs-median story again.

Worked problems

Problem 1 [Least squares with numbers]

Fit y=β0​+β1​x by least squares to the points (1,2),(2,3),(3,5),(4,6). Then give σ^ML2​ and the unbiased estimate of σ2.

Solution

Solution. n=4, xˉ=2.5, yˉ​=4. Deviations: xi​−xˉ=−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5 and yi​−yˉ​=−2,−1,1,2. So

Sxx​=2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5,Sxy​=3+0.5+0.5+3=7.
β^​1​=57​=1.4,β^​0​=4−1.4×2.5=0.5.

Fitted values are 1.9,3.3,4.7,6.1, so the residuals are ei​=0.1,−0.3,0.3,−0.1, and ∑ei2​=0.01+0.09+0.09+0.01=0.2. Then

σ^ML2​=40.2​=0.05,n−20.2​=20.2​=0.1.

Check: the residuals add up to 0, as they must (that's the ∂J/∂β0​=0 equation).

Problem 2 [Regression through the origin]

Suppose the model has no intercept: Yi​=βxi​+εi​ with εi​∼N(0,σ2) i.i.d. Find the MLE of β.

Solution

Solution. As on slide 26, lnL=−2n​ln(2πσ2)−2σ21​∑i​(yi​−βxi​)2, so the MLE minimises J(β)=∑i​(yi​−βxi​)2.

dβdJ​=−2i∑​xi​(yi​−βxi​)=0⟹β^​ML​=∑i​xi2​∑i​xi​yi​​.

J′′(β)=2∑xi2​>0, so this is a minimum. Note it is not Sxy​/Sxx​: without an intercept the line is forced through the origin, not through (xˉ,yˉ​).

Problem 3 [Which loss does the MLE use?]

The noise is i.i.d. with pdf fε​(e)=2b1​e−∣e∣/b. Write down the log likelihood for (β0​,β1​) and say what the MLE minimises. Why might you prefer it to least squares?

Solution

Solution. lnL=∑i​ln(2b1​e−∣yi​−β0​−β1​xi​∣/b)=−nln(2b)−b1​∑i​∣yi​−β0​−β1​xi​∣. The first term is constant and b1​>0, so the MLE minimises ∑i​∣ei​∣ (least absolute deviations). It's preferable when the data has outliers: an absolute value grows linearly, so one far-off point can't drag the line the way a squared residual can.
