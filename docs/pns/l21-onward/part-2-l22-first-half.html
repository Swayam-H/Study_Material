Part 2 — L22 (first half)

Markov, consistency, and the famous n−1

9 min read

Slides 11–13: Markov's inequality

X≥0, a>0⟹P(X≥a)≤aE[X]​​

In words: a non-negative random variable can't be large too often, because that would drag its mean up.

The slide's proof (continuous case). Split the expectation at a and throw away the part below a (it's non-negative, so you're only making things smaller):

E[X]=∫0a​xfX​(x)dx+∫a∞​xfX​(x)dx ≥ ∫a∞​xfX​(x)dx ≥ ∫a∞​afX​(x)dx=aP(X≥a).

The second ≥ uses x≥a on [a,∞). Divide by a and you're done.

A slicker proof that works for any X≥0 (discrete, continuous, mixed). Notice that

a⋅1{X≥a}≤X

always holds: if X≥a the left side is a≤X, and otherwise it's 0≤X. Take expectations of both sides: aP(X≥a)≤E[X]. Two lines. This is the version I'd write in an exam if they don't specify.

Why do we care? Markov is weak on its own (e.g. if E[X]=5, it tells you P(X≥20)≤0.25), but it's a machine for producing other inequalities. Apply it to a cleverly chosen non-negative random variable:

  • Apply it to (X−μ)2 with a=ϵ2 and you get Chebyshev: P(∣X−μ∣≥ϵ)≤σ2/ϵ2.
  • Apply it to (Θ^n​−θ∗)2 and you get the next theorem.

Slide 14: MSE → 0 implies consistency

Theorem. If limn→∞​MSE(Θ^n​)=0, then Θ^n​ is consistent.

Proof.

P(∣Θ^n​−θ∗∣≥ϵ)=P((Θ^n​−θ∗)2≥ϵ2)≤ϵ2E[(Θ^n​−θ∗)2]​=ϵ2MSE(Θ^n​)​→0.

This is the workhorse for proving consistency in exams. The recipe is:

  1. Compute the bias and variance.
  2. Show both →0 (so MSE =Var+B2→0).
  3. Cite this theorem.

Is the converse true? No, and this is a good exam trap. Consider

Θ^n​={θ∗n​w.p. 1−n1​w.p. n1​​

It's consistent: P(∣Θ^n​−θ∗∣≥ϵ)≤n1​→0. But MSE=n(n−θ∗)2​→∞. Rare-but-huge errors don't stop convergence in probability, but they wreck the MSE. (Same story as "convergence in probability doesn't imply convergence in mean square" from L18.)

Slides 15–18: estimating the variance, and why we divide by n−1

The true variance is σ2=E[(X−μ)2]. The natural estimator would be n1​∑(Xi​−μ)2, which is perfectly unbiased... but it needs μ, which you don't know. So you plug in μ^​n​ instead:

S2=n1​i=1∑n​(Xi​−μ^​n​)2.

The slide's HW: is S2 unbiased? No. Here's the slide's derivation, step by step.

Step 1. Add and subtract μ inside the square:

E[(Xi​−μ^​n​)2]=E[(Xi​−μ)2]+E[(μ^​n​−μ)2]−2E[(Xi​−μ)(μ^​n​−μ)].

Step 2. Work out the three pieces.

  • E[(Xi​−μ)2]=σ2. That's the definition.
  • E[(μ^​n​−μ)2]=Var(μ^​n​)=σ2/n.
  • The cross term is the interesting one. Write μ^​n​−μ=n1​∑j​(Xj​−μ). Then
    E[(Xi​−μ)(μ^​n​−μ)]=n1​j∑​E[(Xi​−μ)(Xj​−μ)]=n1​j∑​Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=nσ2​,
    because independence kills every term except j=i.

Step 3. Put it together:

E[(Xi​−μ^​n​)2]=σ2+nσ2​−n2σ2​=σ2(1−n1​),

so

E[S2]=nn−1​σ2,B(S2)=−nσ2​.

Why is it biased low? Intuition: μ^​n​ is computed from the same data, so it sits closer to the data points than the true μ does. (In fact μ^​n​ is the value of c that minimises ∑(Xi​−c)2.) Deviations from μ^​n​ are systematically a bit smaller than deviations from μ.

The fix (slide 18). Rescale by n−1n​:

Sˉ2=n−11​i=1∑n​(Xi​−μ^​n​)2,E[Sˉ2]=σ2​

This is the "sample variance" your calculator uses.

Faster derivation (worth memorising). The identity ∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2=∑(Xi​−μ)2−n(Xˉ−μ)2 gives E[∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2]=nσ2−n⋅nσ2​=(n−1)σ2 in one line. To prove the identity, expand ∑((Xi​−μ)−(Xˉ−μ))2 and use ∑(Xi​−μ)=n(Xˉ−μ).

Slide 18 question: is Sˉ2​ unbiased for σ?

No (unless the data is constant). Clean proof using nothing but variance ≥0. Let Sˉ=Sˉ2​. Then

0≤Var(Sˉ)=E[Sˉ2]−(E[Sˉ])2=σ2−(E[Sˉ])2,

so E[Sˉ]≤σ. Equality would need Var(Sˉ)=0, i.e. Sˉ is a constant, which doesn't happen for non-degenerate data. So Sˉ underestimates σ on average.

The general lesson: unbiasedness does not survive nonlinear transformations. If Θ^ is unbiased for θ, then g(Θ^) is usually not unbiased for g(θ). (Jensen's inequality is the general reason.)

Another example of the same thing, which was on last year's HW 11 Q8 and Tutorial 11 Q4: Xˉ is unbiased for μ, but

E[Xˉ2]=Var(Xˉ)+(EXˉ)2=μ2+nσ2​,

so Xˉ2 is biased for μ2, with bias σ2/n. (It is asymptotically unbiased and consistent, though.) If you want an unbiased version: Xˉ2−nSˉ2​.

Slide 19: estimating θ in Uniform(0,θ)

This example shows up everywhere (it was on last year's HW 11 Q9, Tutorial 11 Q1, and in MLE form on the Endsem). Do it properly once and you'll own it.

Setup. X1​,…,Xn​∼U(0,θ) i.i.d. Estimator: θ^n​=M=max{X1​,…,Xn​}.

Intuitively it makes sense: θ is the upper end of the range, and the biggest thing you've seen is your best clue. But the max can never exceed θ, so it must undershoot on average.

Distribution of the max (L9–L10 skill). For 0≤t≤θ:

FM​(t)=P(all Xi​≤t)=(θt​)n,fM​(t)=θnntn−1​.

Mean:

E[M]=∫0θ​t⋅θnntn−1​dt=θnn​⋅n+1θn+1​=n+1n​θ.

So B(M)=−n+1θ​. Biased low, as expected.

Second moment and variance:

E[M2]=θnn​⋅n+2θn+2​=n+2n​θ2,Var(M)=n+2nθ2​−(n+1)2n2θ2​=(n+1)2(n+2)nθ2​.

MSE:

MSE(M)=(n+1)2(n+2)nθ2​+(n+1)2θ2​=(n+1)(n+2)2θ2​.

The unbiased version. θ~n​=nn+1​M has E[θ~n​]=θ, and

MSE(θ~n​)=Var(θ~n​)=n2(n+1)2​⋅(n+1)2(n+2)nθ2​=n(n+2)θ2​.

Consistency. For 0<ϵ<θ,

P(∣M−θ∣≥ϵ)=P(M≤θ−ϵ)=(θθ−ϵ​)n→0.

(It's actually a.s., since M is increasing in n and bounded by θ; that's what the slide says.) And θ~n​=nn+1​M is consistent too, since nn+1​→1.

Going further: three estimators compared (Tutorial 11 Q1)

Another unbiased estimator comes from the mean: E[X]=θ/2, so θ^mom​=2Xˉ is unbiased with

Var(2Xˉ)=4⋅nθ2/12​=3nθ2​.

Now compare:

Estimator Bias MSE
2Xˉ 0 3nθ2​
nn+1​M 0 n(n+2)θ2​
M −n+1θ​ (n+1)(n+2)2θ2​
n+1n+2​M −(n+1)2θ​ (n+1)2θ2​

Two things worth noticing:

  1. The max-based estimators have MSE of order 1/n2, while 2Xˉ is only order 1/n. So for large n the max is far better. The reason: for the uniform, the max is what actually tells you where the top end θ is, and the sample mean blurs that information by averaging in all the small values.
  2. The last row is the best possible multiple cM. Minimise MSE(cM)=c2E[M2]−2cθE[M]+θ2 over c: you get c=E[M2]E[M]θ​=n+1n+2​, with MSE (n+1)2θ2​. It's slightly biased, and it beats the unbiased one. That's the bias–variance tradeoff again, in an actual example.

Slide 20: consistent but biased

Xi​∼N(μ,σ2), and μ^​n′​=μ^​n​+n1​. Then E[μ^​n′​]=μ+n1​ (biased), but the bias →0 and the variance σ2/n→0, so MSE=nσ2​+n21​→0 and the estimator is consistent.

Vocabulary: an estimator whose bias →0 as n→∞ is called asymptotically unbiased. S2 (divide by n), M for the uniform, and μ^​n​+1/n are all asymptotically unbiased.
