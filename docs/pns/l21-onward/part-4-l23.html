Part 4 — L23

Bayesian inference

⏱ 9 min read

Slide 31: the setup

Now we switch philosophies. You're uncertain about θ∗, so you model it as a random variable Θ, and Bayes' rule does the rest:

posteriorfΘ∣X​(θ∣x)​​∝likelihoodfX∣Θ​(x∣θ)​​×priorfΘ​(θ)​​​
  • Prior: what you believe about θ before seeing data.
  • Likelihood: the same thing as in MLE: how well each θ explains the data.
  • Posterior: what you believe after seeing data.

As data accumulates, the posterior gets narrower and narrower around the truth.

Slide 32: four versions of Bayes' rule

The slide lists all four combinations of discrete/continuous for X and Θ. They all have the same shape:

posterior=marginal of the datalikelihood×prior​

You just use p for whichever variable is discrete and f for whichever is continuous. The denominator is always "sum/integrate the numerator over θ":

fX​(x)=∫fX∣Θ​(x∣θ)fΘ​(θ)dθorpX​(x)=θ∑​pX∣Θ​(x∣θ)pΘ​(θ).

The most important trick in this whole part. The denominator doesn't depend on θ. It's just whatever constant makes the posterior integrate to 1. So in practice, write likelihood × prior, drop every factor that doesn't involve θ, and recognise the shape. If it looks like θa−1(1−θ)b−1, it's a Beta. If it looks like λa−1e−bλ, it's a Gamma. If it's exp(−quadratic in θ), it's a Gaussian. You never need to do the integral.

The slides do the integral explicitly in each example (to show you it works), but in an exam, recognising the kernel is faster and less error-prone.

Slide 34: the Beta distribution

Θ∼Beta(α,β):fΘ​(θ)=B(α,β)1​θα−1(1−θ)β−1,0<θ<1,

where

B(α,β)=∫01​θα−1(1−θ)β−1dθ=Γ(α+β)Γ(α)Γ(β)​,Γ(n)=(n−1)! for integers.

It lives on (0,1), so it's the natural prior for a probability. The plot on slide 35 shows how flexible it is: α=β=1 is flat, α=β=2 is a hump at 21​, α>β leans right, α=β=0.5 is U-shaped.

Think of α−1 and β−1 as "imaginary heads and tails you've already seen". That intuition makes every Beta–Binomial result obvious.

Mean and variance (slide 36), derived. The trick is that any integral of the form ∫01​θa(1−θ)bdθ is a Beta function:

E[Θ]=B(α,β)1​∫01​θα(1−θ)β−1dθ=B(α,β)B(α+1,β)​=Γ(α)Γ(α+β+1)Γ(α+1)Γ(α+β)​=α+βα​,

using Γ(z+1)=zΓ(z). The same trick gives E[Θ2]=(α+β)(α+β+1)α(α+1)​, and so

E[Θ]=α+βα​,Var(Θ)=(α+β)2(α+β+1)αβ​,mode=α+β−2α−1​ (α,β>1)​

And yes, Beta(1,1) has pdf B(1,1)1​θ0(1−θ)0=1 on (0,1): it's the uniform.

Slides 33, 37–40: Example 1, Beta prior + Binomial likelihood

Setup. A coin with unknown P(H)=θ∗. You toss it n times and see k heads. Prior: Θ∼Beta(α,β).

The quick way (kernel recognition):

fΘ∣X​(θ∣k)∝likelihood(kn​)θk(1−θ)n−k​​⋅priorB(α,β)1​θα−1(1−θ)β−1​​∝θk+α−1(1−θ)n−k+β−1.

That's the kernel of a Beta. So

Θ∣X=k ∼ Beta(α+k, β+n−k)​

The slide's way (doing the integral). The marginal is

fX​(k)=(kn​)B(α,β)1​∫01​θk+α−1(1−θ)n−k+β−1dθ=(kn​)B(α,β)B(k+α,n−k+β)​,

and dividing, the (kn​) and B(α,β) cancel, leaving

fΘ∣X​(θ∣k)=B(k+α,n−k+β)θk+α−1(1−θ)n−k+β−1​.

Same answer. (Side note: fX​(k) is called the Beta–Binomial distribution. With a uniform prior, fX​(k)=(kn​)B(k+1,n−k+1)=(kn​)(n+1)!k!(n−k)!​=n+11​: every k from 0 to n is equally likely. Cute.)

Read it with the "imaginary tosses" intuition: you started with α−1 imaginary heads and β−1 imaginary tails, then added k real heads and n−k real tails. The posterior just adds the counts. That's why conjugate priors are so convenient.

Slide 36: uniform prior, and what happens as n→∞

With α=β=1, the posterior is Beta(k+1,n−k+1), so

E[Θ∣k]=n+2k+1​,Var(Θ∣k)=(n+2)2(n+3)(k+1)(n−k+1)​.

(The variance on the slide looks garbled; this is what the Beta formula gives with α′=k+1, β′=n−k+1, α′+β′=n+2.)

n+2k+1​ is Laplace's rule of succession: it's like the MLE nk​ but with one fake head and one fake tail mixed in. If you toss once and see a head, the MLE says P(H)=1 (overconfident!), while the posterior mean says 32​.

As n→∞: nk​→θ∗ a.s. by the SLLN, so n+2k+1​→θ∗ too. And the variance is at most (n+2)2(n+3)(n+2)2/4​=4(n+3)1​→0. So the posterior collapses to a spike (a "Dirac delta") at θ∗. With enough data, the prior stops mattering.

Slides 41–44: Example 2, Gaussian prior + Gaussian likelihood

Setup. X1​,…,Xn​ i.i.d. N(θ∗,σ2) with σ2 known and θ∗ unknown. Prior: Θ∼N(μ0​,σ2). (On the slide, the prior has the same variance σ2 as the noise. That's a simplification; I'll do the general case right after.)

The slide's HW: derive the posterior. This is a "complete the square" exercise. Write likelihood × prior and keep only what depends on θ:

fΘ∣X​(θ∣x)∝exp(−2σ21​i=1∑n​(xi​−θ)2)⋅exp(−2σ2(θ−μ0​)2​)=exp(−2σ21​Q(θ)),

where

Q(θ)=∑(xi​−θ)2+(θ−μ0​)2=(n+1)θ2−2θ(∑xi​+μ0​)+no θ∑xi2​+μ02​​​.

Complete the square in θ: with m=n+1∑xi​+μ0​​,

Q(θ)=(n+1)(θ−m)2+const.

So

fΘ∣X​(θ∣x)∝exp(−2σ2(n+1)​(θ−m)2),

which is a Gaussian kernel with mean m and variance n+1σ2​:

Θ∣X=x ∼ N(n+1∑i=1n​xi​+μ0​​, n+1σ2​)​

Heads-up: slide 41 has a typo. It says the posterior mean is n∑xi​+μ0​​. The correct denominator is n+1, as slides 43–44 show. The same typo is on slide 52 (MAP for Example 2). If an exam asks, use n+1.

Interpretation: the prior behaves exactly like one extra data point at μ0​. Posterior mean = average of n real points and one fake point. That's because the prior's variance equals the noise variance, so the prior is "worth" one observation.

As n→∞ (slide 44): the mean →xˉ (the MLE), the variance →0. The prior washes out again.

The general version (prior variance σ02​=σ2)

This is the version that shows up in problems (e.g. Tutorial 12 Q3), so it's worth knowing. Prior N(μ0​,σ02​), noise variance σ2. The same completing-the-square gives:

σpost2​1​=σ02​1​+σ2n​,μpost​=σpost2​(σ02​μ0​​+σ2∑xi​​)​

In words: precisions add (precision = 1/variance), and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data. Set σ0​=σ and you get the slide's answer back.

Slides 45–49: Example 3, Gamma prior + Poisson likelihood

The Gamma distribution (rate parametrisation, as in the slides):

Λ∼Gamma(α,β):fΛ​(λ)=Γ(α)βα​λα−1e−βλ, λ>0,E=βα​, Var=β2α​, mode=βα−1​ (α≥1).

The key integral (which is how the slides get the marginal):

∫0∞​λa−1e−bλdλ=baΓ(a)​.

Setup. Observe one count X=x from Poisson(λ). Prior Λ∼Gamma(α,β).

Kernel recognition:

fΛ∣X​(λ∣x)∝x!λxe−λ​⋅Γ(α)βα​λα−1e−βλ∝λx+α−1e−(1+β)λ.

That's a Gamma kernel:

Λ∣X=x ∼ Gamma(α+x, β+1)​

The slides confirm it by computing fX​(x)=Γ(α)x!(1+β)x+αβαΓ(x+α)​ and dividing.

Posterior mean and variance (slide 49): E[Λ∣x]=1+βx+α​, Var(Λ∣x)=(1+β)2x+α​.

Extending to n observations (this is what problems actually ask)

With x1​,…,xn​ i.i.d. Poisson(λ), the likelihood is ∝λ∑xi​e−nλ, so

Λ∣x1​,…,xn​ ∼ Gamma(α+∑xi​, β+n)​

Interpretation: α is like "total count seen before", β is like "number of intervals observed before". Each new interval adds its count to α and 1 to β.

And here's a really nice way to see the posterior mean:

E[Λ∣x]=β+nα+∑xi​​=weightβ+nβ​​​⋅prior meanβα​​​+weightβ+nn​​​⋅MLExˉ​​.

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the MLE, and the weight on the data →1 as n→∞. The Beta–Binomial and Gaussian–Gaussian cases have exactly the same structure. If you remember one sentence from L23, make it this one.

The same trick for an exponential likelihood (last year's Endsem Q9)

Xi​∼Exp(λ) i.i.d., prior Λ∼Gamma(α,β). Likelihood λne−λ∑xi​, so

fΛ∣X​∝λne−λ∑xi​⋅λα−1e−βλ=λα+n−1e−(β+∑xi​)λ⟹Λ∣x∼Gamma(α+n, β+∑xi​).

Gamma is conjugate for the exponential too.
