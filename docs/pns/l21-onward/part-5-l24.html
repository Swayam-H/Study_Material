Part 5 — L24

Conjugate priors, MAP and the CE estimator

⏱ 5 min read

Slide 50: conjugate priors

When the posterior lands in the same family as the prior, the prior is called conjugate to that likelihood. That's what happened in all three examples:

Likelihood Conjugate prior Posterior
Binomial(n,θ), k successes Beta(α,β) Beta(α+k, β+n−k)
Bernoulli, n trials, k ones Beta(α,β) Beta(α+k, β+n−k)
Geometric(θ), values y1​..yn​ Beta(α,β) Beta(α+n, β+∑yi​−n)
Poisson(λ), n counts Gamma(α,β) Gamma(α+∑xi​, β+n)
Exponential(λ), n values Gamma(α,β) Gamma(α+n, β+∑xi​)
N(θ,σ2), σ2 known N(μ0​,σ02​) N(μpost​,σpost2​) (precisions add)

(The geometric row uses the pmf θ(1−θ)y−1: likelihood θn(1−θ)∑yi​−n.)

Why it's convenient: updating your beliefs becomes "update two numbers", instead of "compute a new function". And with sequential data, yesterday's posterior is today's prior; you just keep adding.

Slide 51: MAP, the maximum a posteriori estimate

A posterior is a whole distribution. Sometimes you just want one number. The first option is the peak:

θ^MAP​=argθmax​fΘ∣X​(θ∣x)=argθmax​fX∣Θ​(x∣θ)fΘ​(θ)​

(The denominator fX​(x) doesn't depend on θ, so you can ignore it.) Compare with the MLE:

θ^ML​=argθmax​fX∣Θ​(x∣θ).

So MAP = MLE with the prior multiplied in. In log form:

θ^MAP​=argθmax​[lnL(θ)+lnfΘ​(θ)].

The lnfΘ​ term acts like a "penalty" pulling the estimate towards where the prior thinks θ is. Two consequences:

  • Uniform prior ⇒ MAP = MLE (the prior term is constant), as long as the MLE lies inside the prior's range. If it doesn't, the MAP gets clipped to the edge of the prior's support.
  • As n→∞, lnL is a sum of n terms while lnfΘ​ is fixed, so the likelihood dominates and MAP → MLE.

For a discrete Θ (finitely many candidate values), MAP is just "compute likelihood × prior for each candidate and pick the largest". That's how hypothesis-testing-style problems (HW 12 P2, P4, P5, Tutorial 12 Q1) work.

Slide 52: MAP for Example 2 (Gaussian)

The posterior is Gaussian, and a Gaussian's peak is at its mean, so

θ^MAP​=n+1∑i=1n​xi​+μ0​​

(again, n+1, not n as the slide says).

Slide HW: is it the same as the MLE? No. θ^ML​=xˉ=n∑xi​​, while the MAP mixes in μ0​. They're equal only in the special case μ0​=xˉ. The difference is

θ^MAP​−θ^ML​=n+1μ0​−xˉ​→0,

so they agree in the limit, exactly as argued above. The MAP is "shrunk" a little towards the prior mean.

Slide 53: the conditional expectation (CE) estimator

The other natural one-number summary is the mean of the posterior:

θ^CE​=E[Θ∣X=x]=∫θfΘ∣X​(θ∣x)dθ​

(It's also called the LMS or MMSE estimator, because of the next fact.)

Why it's special: it minimises the mean squared error (that's last year's HW 12 P9). For any guess g(x):

E[(Θ−g(x))2∣X=x]=Var(Θ∣X=x)+(E[Θ∣X=x]−g(x))2,

(same bias–variance identity as slide 8, applied to the posterior). The first term doesn't depend on g, and the second is ≥0 with equality iff g(x)=E[Θ∣X=x]. So the CE estimator is the best possible guess in mean-squared-error terms. The minimum achievable error is the posterior variance.

Slide 53 HW: θ^CE​ for all the previous examples (plus MAP and MLE)

Example Posterior θ^MLE​ θ^MAP​ (mode) θ^CE​ (mean)
Beta prior, k heads in n Beta(α+k, β+n−k) nk​ α+β+n−2α+k−1​ α+β+nα+k​
… with uniform prior Beta(k+1, n−k+1) nk​ nk​ n+2k+1​
Gaussian, prior N(μ0​,σ2) N(n+1∑xi​+μ0​​,n+1σ2​) xˉ n+1∑xi​+μ0​​ n+1∑xi​+μ0​​
Gamma prior, one Poisson count x Gamma(α+x, 1+β) x 1+βα+x−1​ 1+βα+x​
Gamma prior, n Poisson counts Gamma(α+∑xi​, β+n) xˉ β+nα+∑xi​−1​ β+nα+∑xi​​

(The Beta MAP needs α+k>1 and β+n−k>1 so the peak is interior; the Gamma MAP needs α+x≥1.)

Patterns to notice:

  • For the Gaussian, MAP = CE, because a Gaussian is symmetric, so its peak and its mean coincide.
  • For Beta and Gamma, the posterior is skewed, so MAP = CE. The mode always has that "−1" and the mean doesn't.
  • With a uniform prior, MAP = MLE, but CE is still different (Laplace's n+2k+1​).
  • In every case all three estimators converge to the same thing as n→∞.
