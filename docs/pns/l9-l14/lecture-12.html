Lecture 12

Conditioning on a random variable, E[X∣Y] and iterated expectations

Deck 2, slides 12 to 29 · 21 min read · 10 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

The big picture. L11 conditioned on a single event. Now we condition on the value of another random variable: for each possible y, the event {Y=y} gives a conditional PMF of X and a conditional mean E[X∣Y=y]. Treating that conditional mean as a function of the random Y gives the random variable E[X∣Y], and its average is E[X] (the law of iterated expectations). This is the most used result of the whole first half of the course: last year's midsem Q1 and Q2 and the endsem Q4 are all built on it.

Read alongside: Bertsekas §2.6, §3.5 and §4.3 (conditional expectation and variance revisited), §4.5 (sum of a random number of random variables); probabilitycourse.com §5.1.5 (conditional expectation, law of total expectation, law of total variance) and §5.2.3 (conditioning for continuous variables).

Part A: the slides

Slide 12/36: Today's class

On the slide. Conditioning X on an event A∈F. Conditional expectation E[X∣A]. Conditioning X with disjoint partitions {Ai​} of Ω. Conditioning X on an event {X∈A}∈F′. Conditioning X on another random variable Y. Conditional expectation E[X∣Y=y]. Law of iterated expectation E[X∣Y]. Bayes rule revisited. Sums of random variables.

The last two items are taught in L13.

Slide 13/36: Conditioning X on random variable Y

On the slide. Consider discrete random variables X and Y with joint PMF pXY​(x,y) and marginal PMFs pX​(x) and pY​(y). Suppose an event A:{Y=y} has happened and we are interested in the probability that X=x given Y=y. This conditional PMF is denoted pX∣Y​(x∣y). In fact pX∣Y​(x∣y):=P(Y=y)P(X=x,Y=y)​=pY​(y)pX,Y​(x,y)​.

pX,Y​(x,y)=pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y)​

This is essentially the same as P(A∩B)=P(A∣B)P(B). Is pX∣Y​(x∣y) consistent? ∑x​pX∣Y​(x∣y)=∑x​pY​(y)pX,Y​(x,y)​=1.

In the table picture. Fix the column Y=y. Its entries are pXY​(x,y) for the various x, and they add up to pY​(y) (the column sum). Divide the column by its sum: that normalised column is pX∣Y​(⋅∣y). One conditional PMF per column. (It is only defined for y with pY​(y)>0.)

Running example (pick 2 from {1,2,3}, L11 slide 3). Column Y=1 is (0,61​,61​) with sum 31​, so pX∣Y​(2∣1)=pX∣Y​(3∣1)=21​: given the second number is 1, the first is 2 or 3 equally likely. In general pX∣Y​(x∣y)=21​ for x=y.

Multiplication rule in both directions. pXY​(x,y)=pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y)=pY∣X​(y∣x)pX​(x). Models are often built this way: first draw Y, then draw X from a distribution that depends on Y (two-stage experiments). B1 and B2 below are examples.

Slide 14/36: What if X and Y are independent?

On the slide. When do we say X and Y are independent? When pX,Y​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y). We also know pX,Y​(x,y)=pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y). This implies pX∣Y​(x∣y)=pX​(x). NOTE: Independence implies E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. Independent random variables are uncorrelated (Cov(X,Y)=0). But uncorrelated random variables need not be independent!! (See Example 4.13 in Bertsekas.)

Meaning. Independent ⟺ every column of the table, once normalised, is the same distribution pX​. Learning Y does not change your beliefs about X.

Bertsekas Example 4.13 (uncorrelated but dependent). (X,Y) takes the four values (1,0), (0,1), (−1,0), (0,−1), each with probability 41​ (the four points of a diamond).

  • E[X]=E[Y]=0 by symmetry, and XY=0 at every point, so E[XY]=0 and Cov(X,Y)=0.
  • But pX∣Y​(0∣1)=1 (if Y=1 then X must be 0), while pX​(0)=21​. So they are dependent.

What goes wrong: covariance only detects linear tendencies. Here ∣X∣ and ∣Y∣ are perfectly (negatively) related, ∣X∣+∣Y∣=1, but the relation is symmetric, so there is no linear trend.

Slide 15/36: Conditioning X on random variable Y (total probability)

On the slide. From pX,Y​(x,y)=pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y), summing both sides over y:

pX​(x)=y∑​pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y)​

Similarly from pX,Y​(x,y)=pY∣X​(y∣x)pX​(x), summing over x: pY​(y)=x∑​pY∣X​(y∣x)pX​(x)​. Notice the similarity to the law of total probability P(A)=∑i​P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​).

This is L11 slide 8 with the partition Ai​={Y=yi​}.

Example (Bertsekas §2.6, the noisy channel; also last year's HW6 Q6 in spirit). N∼ Poisson(λ) messages arrive; each is independently lost with probability 1−q. Let X be the number received. Given N=n, X∼ Binomial(n,q). Then

pX​(k)=n≥k∑​(kn​)qk(1−q)n−ke−λn!λn​=e−λk!(λq)k​m≥0∑​m!(λ(1−q))m​=e−λqk!(λq)k​.

So X∼ Poisson(λq): thinning a Poisson gives a Poisson (Bertsekas Problem 2.37).

Slide 16/36: Picture of conditional PMFs

On the slide. A 3D bar plot of a joint PMF pX,Y​(x,y) over a grid with y=1,2,3, and next to it three "slice views": the conditional PMFs pX∣Y​(x∣3), pX∣Y​(x∣2), pX∣Y​(x∣1), each obtained from one row y of the joint and rescaled.

Reading it. Slicing the joint at Y=y gives a profile whose shape is the conditional PMF; dividing by the slice's total height pY​(y) just rescales it to sum to 1. Different slices can have completely different shapes; that is dependence.

Slide 17/36: Recap

On the slide. Boxes: pX∣A​(x):=P(A)pX​(x)​ if x∈A. E[X∣A]=∑x​xpX∣A​(x). pX​(x)=∑i=1n​P(Ai​)pX∣Ai​​(x). E[X]=∑i=1n​P(Ai​)E[X∣Ai​]. pX,Y​(x,y)=pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y). pX​(x)=∑y​pX∣Y​(x∣y)pY​(y).

Slide 18/36: Conditional expectation E[X∣Y=y]

On the slide. It is easy to guess that

E[X∣Y=y]:=x∑​xpX∣Y​(x∣y),E[Y∣X=x]:=y∑​ypY∣X​(y∣x)​

Can you write E[X] in terms of E[X∣Y=y]?

E[X]=y∑​pY​(y)E[X∣Y=y]​

Proof: ∑y​pY​(y)E[X∣Y=y]=∑y​pY​(y)∑x​xpX∣Y​(x∣y)=∑x​∑y​xpX,Y​(x,y)=∑x​xpX​(x)=E[X].

Proof, line by line. (1) Definition. (2) pY​(y)pX∣Y​(x∣y)=pXY​(x,y) (slide 13), and swap the order of summation. (3) ∑y​pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x) (marginal). (4) Definition of E[X].

Running example. Given Y=y, X is uniform on the other two numbers:

E[X∣Y=1]=22+3​=2.5,E[X∣Y=2]=21+3​=2,E[X∣Y=3]=21+2​=1.5.

Then E[X]=31​(2.5+2+1.5)=2. ✓ Note the pattern: E[X∣Y=y]=26−y​ (the sum of the other two is 6−y).

Slide 19/36: Summary (discrete)

On the slide. The four boxes of slide 17, plus E[X∣Y=y]:=∑x​xpX∣Y​(x∣y) and E[X]=∑y​pY​(y)E[X∣Y=y].

Slides 20/36 to 25/36: How about all this for continuous X and Y?

On the slides. The eight summary boxes are converted one at a time to the continuous case, ending with:

fX∣A​(x)=P(A)fX​(x)​ if x∈A,E[X∣A]=∫−∞∞​xfX∣A​(x)dx,
fX​(x)=i=1∑n​P(Ai​)fX∣Ai​​(x),E[X]=i=1∑n​P(Ai​)E[X∣Ai​],
fX,Y​(x,y)=fX∣Y​(x∣y)fY​(y),fX​(x)=∫y​fX∣Y​(x∣y)fY​(y)dy,
E[X∣Y=y]=∫x​xfX∣Y​(x∣y)dx,E[X]=∫y​E[X∣Y=y]fY​(y)dy.

The rule of thumb. Replace PMFs by PDFs and sums over the values of a continuous variable by integrals. Sums over a partition {Ai​} stay sums (there are still finitely many pieces).

The one subtle point: conditioning on Y=y when P(Y=y)=0. For continuous Y, the event {Y=y} has probability 0, so P(Y=y)P(⋅∩{Y=y})​ is 00​. The definition

fX∣Y​(x∣y):=fY​(y)fXY​(x,y)​(fY​(y)>0)​

is justified as a limit: condition on the thin strip {y≤Y≤y+δ}, which has positive probability ≈fY​(y)δ, and let δ→0:

P(X∈[x,x+ϵ]∣y≤Y≤y+δ)≈fY​(y)δfXY​(x,y)ϵδ​=fY​(y)fXY​(x,y)​ϵ.

Geometric picture. fX∣Y​(⋅∣y) is the slice of the joint density surface along the horizontal line Y=y, rescaled to integrate to 1. For a uniform joint density, every slice is flat, so X∣Y=y is uniform on the slice. This makes triangle and disc problems quick (B5 below).

Example (last year's HW5 Q6). fXY​(x,y)=cx(y−x)e−y for 0≤x≤y<∞. Find c and both conditional densities.

Solution. Integrate x first: ∫0y​x(y−x)dx=6y3​. So fY​(y)=c6y3​e−y and ∫0∞​6y3​e−ydy=63!​=1, giving c=1. Integrating y instead: fX​(x)=xe−x∫x∞​(y−x)e−(y−x)dy=xe−x. Then

fX∣Y​(x∣y)=y3e−y/6x(y−x)e−y​=y36x(y−x)​, 0≤x≤y;fY∣X​(y∣x)=xe−xx(y−x)e−y​=(y−x)e−(y−x), y≥x.​

(The second says: given X=x, Y−x∼ Gamma(2,1), with mean 2, so E[Y∣X=x]=x+2.)

Slide 26/36: Conditional expectation E[X∣Y]

On the slide. Recall E[X∣Y=y]:=∑x​xpX∣Y​(x∣y). E[X∣Y=y] is a constant given y. g(y):=E[X∣Y=y] is a function of y. Now consider the random variable E[X∣Y]. When Y takes the value y (this happens with probability pY​(y)), E[X∣Y] takes the value E[X∣Y=y]. E[X∣Y] is a function of Y, say g(Y). What is the expectation of E[X∣Y]?

This is the conceptual jump of the lecture.

  • E[X∣Y=2] is a number.
  • E[X∣Y] is a random variable: first compute the function g(y)=E[X∣Y=y], then plug in the random Y.

In the running example, g(y)=26−y​, so E[X∣Y]=26−Y​, a random variable taking values 2.5,2,1.5 with probability 31​ each. It is "the best guess of X once you have seen Y". Two extreme cases: if X and Y are independent, E[X∣Y]=E[X] (a constant: Y tells you nothing); if X=h(Y), then E[X∣Y]=h(Y) (Y tells you everything).

Slide 27/36: The law of iterated expectations

On the slide. g(Y)=E[X∣Y]. What is E[g(Y)]=E[E[X∣Y]]? E[g(Y)]=∑y​g(y)pY​(y)=∑y​E[X∣Y=y]pY​(y). This implies E[g(Y)]=E[E[X∣Y]]=E[X]. This is the law of iterated expectation.

E[E[X∣Y]]=E[X]​

Why it is just slide 18 again. LOTUS on the function g of Y gives E[g(Y)]=∑y​g(y)pY​(y), and slide 18 says that sum is E[X]. The inner expectation is over X (with Y frozen), the outer one over Y.

Two companion rules (Bertsekas §4.3, Problem 4.25).

  1. Taking out what is known: E[h(Y)X∣Y]=h(Y)E[X∣Y]. (Given Y, h(Y) is a constant.)
  2. Law of total variance:
Var(X)=E[Var(X∣Y)]+Var(E[X∣Y])​

"Total spread = average spread within each group + spread between the group means." Proof: E[Var(X∣Y)]=E[E[X2∣Y]]−E[(E[X∣Y])2]=E[X2]−E[(E[X∣Y])2] and Var(E[X∣Y])=E[(E[X∣Y])2]−(E[X])2. Add them: E[X2]−(E[X])2.

Running example check. Given Y=y, X is uniform on two numbers that differ by 1 or 2: Var(X∣Y=1)=41​ (values 2, 3), Var(X∣Y=2)=1 (values 1, 3), Var(X∣Y=3)=41​. So E[Var(X∣Y)]=31​(41​+1+41​)=21​. And Var(26−Y​)=41​⋅32​=61​. Total 21​+61​=32​=Var(X) for X uniform on {1,2,3}. ✓

Slide 28/36: Conditional expectation E[X∣Y], Example

On the slide. Consider Y=λ1​ with probability p and λ2​ with probability 1−p. Now consider an exponential random variable X with a random parameter Y. What is E[X]? E[X]=E[E[X∣Y]]=∑y​E[X∣Y=y]pY​(y). We have X∼ Exp(λ1​) with probability p when Y=λ1​, and X∼ Exp(λ2​) with probability 1−p. E[X∣Y=λi​]=λi​1​, so E[X]=λ1​p​+λ2​1−p​.

Going further (a typical exam extension).

  • Density (total probability, slide 20): fX​(x)=pλ1​e−λ1​x+(1−p)λ2​e−λ2​x, a mixture (hyperexponential). It is not exponential with the average rate.
  • Variance (total variance): Var(X∣Y=λi​)=λi2​1​, so
Var(X)=(λ12​p​+λ22​1−p​)+(λ12​p​+λ22​1−p​−(λ1​p​+λ2​1−p​)2).
  • Tail: P(X>t)=pe−λ1​t+(1−p)e−λ2​t.
  • Last year's endsem Q4 is the general version: if P(Z=i)=pi​ and X∣Z=i has the distribution of Yi​, then fX​=∑i​pi​fYi​​ and E[X]=∑i​pi​E[Yi​].

Slide 29/36: Conditional expectation E[X∣Y], Example 2 (random sums)

On the slide. Consider Y=X1​+X2​+⋯+XN​, where N is a positive integer valued random variable with PMF pN​(⋅) and the Xi​ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean E[X] (and independent of N). What is E[Y]? Use E[Y]=E[E[Y∣N]]. What is E[Y∣N=n]? E[Y∣N=n]=E[X1​+⋯+Xn​]=nE[X]. This implies E[Y∣N]=NE[X]. Now E[Y]=E[E[Y∣N]]=E[NE[X]]=E[X]E[N]. What is Var(Y)? (section 4.5)

Where independence of N is used. E[Y∣N=n]=E[X1​+⋯+Xn​∣N=n]=E[X1​+⋯+Xn​] only because knowing N=n does not change the distribution of the Xi​.

HW solved: Var(Y). Use the law of total variance with N as the conditioning variable. Given N=n, Y is a sum of n i.i.d. terms, so (L13) Var(Y∣N=n)=nVar(X), i.e. Var(Y∣N)=NVar(X). Also E[Y∣N]=NE[X]. Hence

Var(Y)=E[NVar(X)]+Var(NE[X])=E[N]Var(X)+(E[X])2Var(N)​

Two sources of randomness: the terms themselves (first part) and how many terms there are (second part). Sanity checks: if N=n is fixed, Var(N)=0 and we get nVar(X); if every Xi​=c is constant, Y=cN and we get c2Var(N).

Worked example. You roll a die to get N, then toss N fair coins; Y is the number of heads. E[N]=3.5, Var(N)=1235​, E[X]=21​, Var(X)=41​:

E[Y]=3.5⋅21​=1.75,Var(Y)=3.5⋅41​+41​⋅1235​=87​+4835​=4877​≈1.60.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's midsem Q1). A fair die is rolled until a 6 appears. Each roll pays its face value, except the final 6, which pays 0. Find the expected total reward.

Solution

Solution. Let N = number of rolls, Geometric(61​), E[N]=6. Given N=n, the first n−1 rolls are non-6 rolls, each uniform on {1,…,5} with mean 3 (conditioning on "not a 6", L11 slide 10), and the last pays 0. So E[Y∣N]=3(N−1) and

E[Y]=E[3(N−1)]=3(6−1)=15.

(Last year's solution also does it by summing the series ∑n​3(n−1)(65​)n−161​; the iterated-expectation route is the one to learn.)

B2 (last year's midsem Q2). X,Y i.i.d. U[0,1]. Prove P(X<Y)=21​ by conditioning.

Solution

Solution. Condition on Y (the continuous total probability theorem):

P(X<Y)=∫01​P(X<Y∣Y=y)fY​(y)dy=∫01​P(X<y)dy=∫01​ydy=21​.

The step P(X<Y∣Y=y)=P(X<y) uses independence. (For i.i.d. continuous variables, symmetry gives 21​ without any integral: P(X<Y)=P(Y<X) and P(X=Y)=0.)

B3 (last year's HW5 Q9). fXY​=4x2​+4y2​+6xy​ on 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤2. For 0≤y≤2 find fX∣Y​(x∣y) and P(X<21​∣Y=y).

Solution

Solution. fY​(y)=∫01​(4x2​+4y2​+6xy​)dx=121​+4y2​+12y​. Multiply top and bottom by 12:

fX∣Y​(x∣y)=1+y+3y23x2+3y2+2xy​,0≤x≤1;P(X<21​∣Y=y)=1+y+3y281​+23​y2+4y​​=8(1+y+3y2)1+2y+12y2​.​

(Check at y=0: fX∣Y​(x∣0)=3x2, and P(X<21​∣Y=0)=81​. ✓)

B4 (Bertsekas Problem 3.21, breaking a stick twice). Break a stick of length ℓ at a uniform point and keep the left piece Y; break that at a uniform point and keep the left piece X. Find fXY​, fX​ and E[X] two ways.

Solution

Solution. fY​(y)=ℓ1​ on [0,ℓ] and fX∣Y​(x∣y)=y1​ on [0,y]. Multiplication rule: fXY​(x,y)=ℓy1​ for 0≤x≤y≤ℓ. Marginal:

fX​(x)=∫xℓ​ℓydy​=ℓ1​lnxℓ​,0<x≤ℓ;E[X]=∫0ℓ​ℓx​lnxℓ​dx=4ℓ​.

Iterated expectations is much faster: E[X∣Y]=2Y​, so E[X]=2E[Y]​=4ℓ​.

B5 (Bertsekas Problem 3.23(c) to (e), the triangle again). (X,Y) uniform on the triangle (0,0), (1,0), (0,1) (L10 B4). Find fX∣Y​, E[X∣Y=y], and E[X] by total expectation and symmetry.

Solution

Solution. The slice at height y is [0,1−y] and the joint is flat, so X∣Y=y∼U[0,1−y]: fX∣Y​(x∣y)=1−y1​ and E[X∣Y=y]=21−y​. Total expectation: E[X]=21−E[Y]​. By symmetry E[X]=E[Y], so E[X]=21−E[X]​, i.e. E[X]=31​.

B6 (Bertsekas Problem 2.32, joint lives). m couples (2m people); each person is alive later with probability p, independently. A = number alive, S = number of couples with both alive. Find E[S∣A=a].

Solution

Solution. Given A=a, by symmetry the set of survivors is a uniformly random a-subset of the 2m people. Write S=∑j=1m​1j​, where 1j​ indicates that couple j survives. P(both in couple j survive∣A=a)=(a2m​)(a−22m−2​)​=2m(2m−1)a(a−1)​. Linearity of conditional expectation:

E[S∣A=a]=m⋅2m(2m−1)a(a−1)​=2(2m−1)a(a−1)​.

(Check: a=2m gives m.)

B7 (Bertsekas Problem 4.41, the elevator; a compound Poisson sum). N∼ Poisson(λ) people enter; person i's excess weight fraction Xi​∼U[0,1], independent. Y=∑i=1N​Xi​. Find E[Y] and Var(Y).

Solution

Solution. E[Y]=E[N]E[X]=2λ​. Var(Y)=E[N]Var(X)+(E[X])2Var(N)=12λ​+4λ​=3λ​. (For a Poisson number of terms this always simplifies to λE[X2], here λ⋅31​. ✓ The MGF part of this problem is in the L14 to L20 notes.)

B8 (Bertsekas Problem 2.41(c), traffic tickets). You drive 250 days a year and get a ticket on each day with probability 0.02, independently. Each ticket costs $10, $20 or $50 with probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. Find the mean and variance of the yearly amount.

Solution

Solution. N∼ Binomial(250,0.02): E[N]=5, Var(N)=4.9. One ticket: E[X]=5+6+10=21, E[X2]=50+120+500=670, Var(X)=670−441=229. So E[Y]=5⋅21=105 and

Var(Y)=5⋅229+212⋅4.9=1145+2160.9=3305.9.

B9 (a two-stage experiment with a continuous first stage). P∼U[0,1] is the (random) bias of a coin; given P=p, toss it n times and let K be the number of heads. Find E[K], Var(K) and P(K=k).

Solution

Solution. K∣P∼ Binomial(n,P), so E[K∣P]=nP and Var(K∣P)=nP(1−P).

E[K]=nE[P]=2n​Var(K)=nE[P−P2]+n2Var(P)=n(21​−31​)+12n2​=12n(n+2)​.​

And P(K=k)=∫01​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−kdp=(kn​)(n+1)!k!(n−k)!​=n+11​ for k=0,…,n: uniform! (The integral is the Beta integral ∫01​pa(1−p)bdp=(a+b+1)!a!b!​.) Compare: a fair coin gives Var(K)=4n​, much smaller than 12n(n+2)​ for large n; uncertainty about the bias adds the Var(E[K∣P]) term.

B10 (Bertsekas Problem 4.26, variance of a product). X, Y independent. Show Var(XY)=(E[X])2Var(Y)+(E[Y])2Var(X)+Var(X)Var(Y).

Solution

Solution. Condition on X. E[XY∣X]=XE[Y] and Var(XY∣X)=X2Var(Y). Total variance:

Var(XY)​=E[X2]Var(Y)+(E[Y])2Var(X)=(Var(X)+(E[X])2)Var(Y)+(E[Y])2Var(X).​
