Lecture 10

Independence, joint PDFs, functions of two variables, covariance

Deck 1, slides 12 to 26⏱ 25 min read✓ 15 practice problems
Solutions stay hidden until you click them.

The big picture. With the joint PMF in hand, this lecture asks four questions. When are X and Y independent? How do we do the same things for continuous pairs (joint CDF and joint PDF)? How do we compute E[g(X,Y)] and the distribution of Z=g(X,Y) (with max and min as the key example)? And how do we measure how two variables move together (covariance)?

Read alongside: Bertsekas §2.5 to 2.7, §3.4 and §4.2; probabilitycourse.com §5.1 (two discrete variables), §5.2 (two continuous variables) and §5.3.1 (covariance and correlation).

Part A: the slides

Slide 12/26: Independence

On the slide. Back with the running example of coin and die. Write down pXY​(x,y) and FXY​(x,y). Notice that pXY​(1,i)=pX​(1)pY​(i) and FXY​(1,i)=FX​(1)FY​(i). In general, if pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y) and FXY​(x,y)=FX​(x)FY​(y) we say X and Y are independent. Two random variables X and Y are independent if pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y) and FXY​(x,y)=FX​(x)FY​(y).

Writing them down. pXY​(x,y)=121​ for x∈{0,1}, y∈{1,…,6}, and 0 otherwise. For the CDF, count the cells to the lower-left of (x,y):

FXY​(x,y)=FX​(x)FY​(y),FX​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​021​1​x<00≤x<1x≥1​,FY​(y)=⎩⎨⎧​06⌊y⌋​1​y<11≤y<6y≥6​

For example FXY​(0,3.5)=P(X=0,Y≤3)=123​=21​⋅63​. ✓

"For all x,y". Independence needs the product rule at every pair (x,y). One failure is enough to make them dependent; one success proves nothing. (For discrete variables, the PMF condition and the CDF condition are equivalent, so checking one is enough.)

A fast test from the table. pXY​=pX​pY​ for all cells means every row of the joint table is proportional to every other row. If a cell is 0 while its row and column sums are both positive, the variables are dependent immediately (the product would be positive).

Slide 13/26: Independence and P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

On the slide. How does this relate to P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)? Take A={ω:X(ω)≤x} and B={ω:Y(ω)≤y}. Then FXY​(x,y):=P{ω:X(ω)≤x and Y(ω)≤y}=P(A∩B).

So FXY​(x,y)=FX​(x)FY​(y) says exactly that the events {X≤x} and {Y≤y} are independent events (L3), for every choice of x and y. Similarly pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y) says {X=x} and {Y=y} are independent events. Independence of random variables = independence of every event about X from every event about Y. A consequence (last year's HW5 Q11): if X and Y are independent then so are g(X) and h(Y) for any functions g,h, because {g(X)=a}={X∈g−1(a)} is an event about X.

Slide 14/26: E[XY]

On the slide. E[X]=∑x​xpX​(x) and E[Y]=∑y​ypY​(y). Also E[X]=∑x​∑y​xpXY​(x,y) and E[Y]=∑x​∑y​ypXY​(x,y). How do we define E[XY]? You want to search over all values X×Y can take ({1,2,…,6}) and weight it by the corresponding probabilities. E[XY]=∑x​∑y​xypXY​(x,y)=1.75=E[X]E[Y]. If X and Y are independent, E[XY]=E[X]E[Y].

Why E[X]=∑x​∑y​xpXY​(x,y). Do the inner sum first: ∑y​pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x) (marginal, L9). So it collapses to ∑x​xpX​(x).

The number 1.75. XY=0 when the coin is tails and XY=Y when it is heads, so E[XY]=∑i=16​i⋅121​=1221​=1.75. And E[X]E[Y]=21​⋅27​=1.75. ✓

Proof of the boxed fact. If pXY​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y), the double sum factorises:

E[XY]=x∑​y∑​xypX​(x)pY​(y)=(x∑​xpX​(x))(y∑​ypY​(y))=E[X]E[Y].

The same argument gives E[g(X)h(Y)]=E[g(X)]E[h(Y)] for independent X, Y. The converse is false: E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] does not imply independence (see L12 slide 14 and B8 below).

Slide 15/26: Example where X and Y are dependent

On the slide. Now consider rolling a die. X=1 if the outcome is odd, 0 otherwise; Y=1 if the outcome is even, 0 otherwise. What is pX​(x), pY​(y), pXY​(x,y) and FXY​(x,y)? What is E[XY]?

Solution. Y=1−X always. pX​(0)=pX​(1)=21​, and the same for Y. The joint table:

Y=0 Y=1
X=0 0 21​
X=1 21​ 0

Joint CDF: FXY​(x,y)=0 if x<0 or y<0; =pXY​(0,0)=0 if 0≤x<1, 0≤y<1; =21​ if exactly one of x,y is ≥1 and the other is in [0,1); =1 if x≥1 and y≥1.

E[XY]=0, because XY=0 always (a number cannot be both odd and even). But E[X]E[Y]=41​. So E[XY]=E[X]E[Y], which proves they are dependent. Also pXY​(0,0)=0=41​=pX​(0)pY​(0) and FXY​(0,0)=0=FX​(0)FY​(0)=41​. Covariance (slide 25): Cov(X,Y)=0−41​=−41​, negative because when one is up the other is down.

Slide 16/26: Consistency conditions

On the slide. ∑x,y​pXY​(x,y)=1. FXY​(∞,∞)=1. FXY​(−∞,−∞)=0. FXY​(−∞,∞)=0. FXY​(∞,−∞)=0. FXY​(x,∞)=FX​(x) (marginal CDF). FXY​(∞,y)=FY​(y) (marginal CDF).

Why. FXY​(x,∞)=P(X≤x,Y<∞)=P(X≤x), because "Y<∞" is always true. If either argument is −∞ the event is impossible, so the CDF is 0. All limits are justified by continuity of probability, as in L9.

One more property you need (not on the slide). The probability of a rectangle:

P(a<X≤b, c<Y≤d)=F(b,d)−F(a,d)−F(b,c)+F(a,c).

(Inclusion-exclusion: take the big lower-left quadrant at (b,d), remove the two quadrants at (a,d) and (b,c), add back the doubly-removed one at (a,c).) This is why a valid joint CDF must give a non-negative value here, which is stronger than being non-decreasing in each variable.

Slide 17/26: Multiple continuous random variables

On the slide. Pick a point uniformly at random from a unit square centred at (.5,.5). Random variables X and Y represent the x and y coordinates of the point chosen. FX,Y​(x,y) denotes the probability that the point chosen lies below and to the left of (x,y). In this example, FX,Y​(x,y)=xy. Now visualise FX,Y​(x+h,y)−FX,Y​(x,y). This is the probability that the point lies in the thin strip below y and between x and x+h.

Uniform means area. For a uniform point in a region of area 1, the probability of any subregion is its area. "Below and to the left of (x,y)", inside the square, is an x×y rectangle, so FXY​(x,y)=xy for 0≤x,y≤1. In full, FXY​(x,y)=x~y~​ with x~=min(max(x,0),1), and the same for y~​.

Slide 18/26: Multiple continuous random variables (rate in x)

On the slide. FX,Y​(x+h,y)−FX,Y​(x,y) is the probability that the point lies in the thin strip below y and between x and x+h. ∂x∂FXY​(x,y)​=limh→0​hFX,Y​(x+h,y)−FX,Y​(x,y)​. This is the rate of change of the joint CDF in the x direction.

Slide 19/26: Multiple continuous random variables (the joint PDF)

On the slide. ∂y∂FXY​(x,y)​=limh→0​hFX,Y​(x,y+h)−FX,Y​(x,y)​ denotes the rate of change in the y direction. fX,Y​(x,y):=∂x∂y∂2FXY​(x,y)​ is the joint probability density function. fX,Y​(x,y)dxdy denotes the probability that (X,Y) lies in a rectangle of area dxdy around (x,y). In this example fX,Y​(x,y)=1. FXY​(x,y):=∫−∞x​∫−∞y​fXY​(s,t)dsdt.

How to think of the joint PDF. It is probability per unit area. Probabilities of regions are double integrals:

P((X,Y)∈A)=∬A​fXY​(x,y)dxdy​,fXY​≥0,∬R2​fXY​=1.

In the square example, ∂x∂y∂2​(xy)=1 inside the square and 0 outside.

The 90% skill: drawing the region. Almost every joint-PDF problem is "integrate f over a region". Always sketch the support of f and the region A, then choose the order of integration so the limits are easy.

Example (last year's Tutorial 5 Q8). fXY​(x,y)=x+y on [0,1]2. Find P(X>Y∣X<21​).

P(X<21​)=∫01/2​∫01​(x+y)dydx=∫01/2​(x+21​)dx=81​+41​=83​,
P(Y<X<21​)=∫01/2​∫0x​(x+y)dydx=∫01/2​23​x2dx=161​P(X>Y∣X<21​)=3/81/16​=61​.​

Slide 20/26: Summary for continuous random variables

On the slide. fXY​(x,y) denotes the joint pdf for X and Y. FXY​(x,y):=∫−∞x​∫−∞y​fXY​(s,t)dsdt and fX,Y​(x,y):=∂x∂y∂2FXY​​. The marginal pdfs fX​ and fY​ can be obtained from the joint pdf: fX​(x)=∫−∞∞​fXY​(x,y)dy and fY​(y)=∫−∞∞​fXY​(x,y)dx. Two random variables X and Y are independent if fXY​(x,y)=fX​(x)fY​(y), FXY​(x,y)=FX​(x)FY​(y) and E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. Rules similar for more than 2 random variables.

Marginals: "integrate out" the other variable. Watch the limits: if the support is not a rectangle, the limits of the inner integral depend on the outer variable.

Careful with the independence box. The definition is fXY​=fX​fY​ for all (x,y) (equivalently, FXY​=FX​FY​). E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] is a consequence, not part of the definition, and on its own it does not imply independence.

Fast independence test for continuous pairs. X and Y are independent exactly when fXY​(x,y)=g(x)h(y) for some functions g,h and the support is a product set (a rectangle, possibly infinite). Two classic traps:

  • f=2 on the triangle 0≤y≤x≤1: the formula "2" factorises, but the support is a triangle, so they are dependent (knowing x restricts y to [0,x]).
  • f=x+y on [0,1]2: the support is a square, but x+y does not factorise, so they are dependent.

Example (last year's Tutorial 5 Q3). fXY​=6e−(2x+3y) for x,y≥0. This is 2e−2x⋅3e−3y on a product set, so X∼ Exp(2) and Y∼ Exp(3) are independent. Hence E[Y∣X>2]=E[Y]=31​ (information about X tells you nothing about Y).

Slide 21/26: Towards E[g(X,Y)]

On the slide. What about E[aX+bY+c]?

E[aX+bY+c]​=x,y∑​(ax+by+c)pXY​(x,y)=ax,y∑​xpXY​(x,y)+bx,y∑​ypXY​(x,y)+cx,y∑​pXY​(x,y)=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c.​

What is E[aX+bY+c] for the running example? Along similar lines, one would expect E[g(X,Y)]=∑x​∑y​g(x,y)pXY​(x,y).

Running example. E[X]=21​, E[Y]=27​, so E[aX+bY+c]=2a​+27b​+c.

Linearity needs no independence. The derivation never used independence. E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y] is true for any X, Y (last year's HW5 Q8). This is what makes indicator tricks work (B11 below).

Slide 22/26: Finding pZ​(⋅) where Z=g(X,Y)

On the slide. Suppose Z=g(X). Then what is pZ​(z)? pZ​(z)=∑{x:g(x)=z}​pX​(x). Now suppose Z=g(X,Y); then we have

pZ​(z)={x,y:g(x,y)=z}∑​pXY​(x,y).

Does this need a proof? No, if you are an unconscious statistician!

In words. To find P(Z=z), collect every cell of the joint table where g equals z and add them up. (The joke: LOTUS users happily do this without proof; the proof is just that {Z=z} is the disjoint union of the events {X=x,Y=y} over those cells.)

Example. Two fair dice, Z=max(X,Y). The cells with max equal to z form an "L" shape with 2z−1 cells, so pZ​(z)=362z−1​ for z=1,…,6.

Slide 23/26: E[g(X,Y)]

On the slide. How do we define E[g(X,Y)]? One way is to define Z=g(X,Y) and find E[Z]=∑z​zpZ​(z). Recall pZ​(z)=∑{x,y:g(x,y)=z}​pXY​(x,y). This gives E[Z]=∑z​∑{x,y:g(x,y)=z}​zpXY​(x,y). This is the same as E[g(X,Y)]=∑(x,y)​g(x,y)pXY​(x,y).

E[g(X,Y)]=x,y∑​g(x,y)pXY​(x,y)​E[g(X,Y)]=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​g(x,y)fXY​(x,y)dxdy (continuous)​​

The step "this is the same as". Inside the double sum, z=g(x,y) for every cell in the inner sum. So we are adding g(x,y)pXY​(x,y) over all cells, grouped by the value of g. Regrouping a sum does not change it. That is 2D LOTUS: you never need the distribution of Z to find its mean.

Example (Bertsekas Problem 4.7; last year's midsem Q2 in spirit). X,Y i.i.d. U[0,1]. Then

E∣X−Y∣=∫01​∫01​∣x−y∣dxdy=2∫01​∫0x​(x−y)dydx=2∫01​2x2​dx=31​.

(By symmetry, the regions x>y and x<y contribute equally.) Similarly P(X<Y)=∬x<y​1dxdy=21​, the area of a half square.

Slide 24/26: Example of a function of random variables

On the slide. Suppose X and Y are continuous independent random variables. Let Z=max(X,Y) and Z=min(X,Y). Find the CDF and pdf of Z for each case. HW: When X and Y are exponential with parameters λ1​ and λ2​, then Z (=min) is also exponential with parameter λ1​+λ2​.

Max: "everyone is below z".

{max(X,Y)≤z}={X≤z}∩{Y≤z} ⇒ Fmax​(z)=FX​(z)FY​(z)fmax​(z)=fX​(z)FY​(z)+FX​(z)fY​(z).​

Min: "everyone is above z". Work with the tail P(Z>z):

{min(X,Y)>z}={X>z}∩{Y>z} ⇒ Fmin​(z)=1−(1−FX​(z))(1−FY​(z)),
fmin​(z)=fX​(z)(1−FY​(z))+fY​(z)(1−FX​(z)).

Both use independence to split the probability of the intersection. For n i.i.d. variables with CDF F and PDF f: Fmax​=Fn, fmax​=nFn−1f, Fmin​=1−(1−F)n, fmin​=n(1−F)n−1f.

HW solved. X∼ Exp(λ1​), Y∼ Exp(λ2​) independent, Z=min(X,Y). For z≥0, P(X>z)=e−λ1​z and P(Y>z)=e−λ2​z, so

P(Z>z)=e−λ1​ze−λ2​z=e−(λ1​+λ2​)z⇒ FZ​(z)=1−e−(λ1​+λ2​)z,fZ​(z)=(λ1​+λ2​)e−(λ1​+λ2​)z, z≥0.​

That is the Exp(λ1​+λ2​) tail, so Z∼ Exp(λ1​+λ2​). For z<0 both CDFs are 0. (Last year's midsem Q3 is the three-variable version: min(X,Y,Z)∼ Exp(λ1​+λ2​+λ3​).) Interpretation: two independent alarm clocks ringing at rates λ1​ and λ2​; the first ring comes at the combined rate.

Slide 25/26: Covariance of X and Y

On the slide. Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−E[X])(Y−E[Y])]=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]. When the covariance is zero, they are said to be uncorrelated. (Section 4.2 Bertsekas.) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance

Proof of the shortcut. Write μX​=E[X], μY​=E[Y] and expand, using linearity:

E[(X−μX​)(Y−μY​)]=E[XY]−μY​E[X]−μX​E[Y]+μX​μY​=E[XY]−μX​μY​.

Correlation coefficient. Covariance depends on units (measure X in cm instead of m and it grows by 100). The unit-free version is

ρ(X,Y)=Var(X)Var(Y)​Cov(X,Y)​∈[−1,1],

with ρ=±1 exactly when Y−E[Y] is a positive (negative) multiple of X−E[X] (Bertsekas Problems 4.20 and 4.21, via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (E[XY])2≤E[X2]E[Y2]).

Independent ⇒ uncorrelated (from slide 14). The converse fails; see L12 slide 14.

Slide 26/26: Covariance of X and Y (pictures)

On the slide. Three scatter plots: a cloud tilted down (cov(X,Y)<0), a round cloud (cov(X,Y)≈0), a cloud tilted up (cov(X,Y)>0).

Reading the pictures. The product (X−μX​)(Y−μY​) is positive in the top-right and bottom-left quadrants (around the mean point) and negative in the other two. An upward-tilted cloud puts most of its mass in the positive quadrants, so the average product is positive. Covariance measures linear association only: points on a circle or a parabola symmetric about μX​ can have covariance 0 while being completely dependent.

B12 (last year's HW5 Q3). X1​,X2​,X3​ i.i.d. Bernoulli(p); Y1​=max(X1​,X2​), Y2​=max(X1​,X3​), Y3​=max(X2​,X3​), Y=Y1​+Y2​+Y3​. Find E[Y] and Var(Y).

Solution

Solution. Each Yi​ is Bernoulli with q:=P(Yi​=1)=1−(1−p)2=2p−p2. So E[Y]=3q=3p(2−p) (linearity; the Yi​ are dependent but that does not matter). For the variance, use the L13 formula Var(∑Yi​)=∑Var(Yi​)+2∑i<j​Cov(Yi​,Yj​). By symmetry all three covariances are equal:

P(Y1​=Y2​=1)=P(X1​=1)+P(X1​=0,X2​=1,X3​=1)=p+(1−p)p2,
Var(Y)=3q(1−q)+6[p+(1−p)p2−q2]=3p(1−p)2(4−3p).

(Sanity checks: p=0 or p=1 gives 0, as it must.)

B13 (last year's HW5 Q12, the multinomial; Bertsekas Problem 2.27). A die with r faces (face i has probability pi​) is rolled n times; Xi​ is the number of times face i comes up. Find the joint PMF, E[Xi​], Var(Xi​) and E[Xi​Xj​].

Solution

Solution. A particular sequence with k1​ ones, ..., kr​ r's has probability p1k1​​⋯prkr​​, and there are k1​!⋯kr​!n!​ such sequences:

pX1​…Xr​​(k1​,…,kr​)=k1​!⋯kr​!n!​p1k1​​⋯prkr​​,k1​+⋯+kr​=n.

Each Xi​ alone is Binomial(n,pi​): E[Xi​]=npi​, Var(Xi​)=npi​(1−pi​). For i=j write Xi​=∑m=1n​Am​, Xj​=∑l=1n​Bl​ with indicators Am​=1{roll m is i}, Bl​=1{roll l is j}. E[Am​Bm​]=0 (one roll cannot show both faces) and E[Am​Bl​]=pi​pj​ for m=l:

E[Xi​Xj​]=n(n−1)pi​pj​,Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=n(n−1)pi​pj​−n2pi​pj​=−npi​pj​.

Negative, as expected: more 1s leaves fewer rolls for 2s.

B14 (Bertsekas Problem 4.17). X and Y have the same variance. Show X−Y and X+Y are uncorrelated.

Solution

Solution. Expand bilinearly (L13): Cov(X−Y,X+Y)=Var(X)+Cov(X,Y)−Cov(Y,X)−Var(Y)=0.

B15 (last year's Tutorial 5 Q2). X,Y i.i.d. N(0,1), Z=1+X+XY2, W=1+X. Find Cov(Z,W).

Solution

Solution. Constants do not matter: Cov(Z,W)=Cov(X+XY2,X)=Var(X)+Cov(XY2,X). And Cov(XY2,X)=E[X2Y2]−E[XY2]E[X]=E[X2]E[Y2]−0=1 (independence). So Cov(Z,W)=2.
