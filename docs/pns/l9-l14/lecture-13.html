Lecture 13

Sums of random variables, Bayes' rule revisited, variance of sums

Deck 3, slides 2 to 13 · 22 min read · 13 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

The big picture. Three tools that combine everything from L9 to L12. (1) The convolution formula gives the distribution of X+Y. (2) Bayes' rule for random variables turns fY∣X​ (how measurements behave) into fX∣Y​ (what we learn from a measurement). (3) The variance of a sum expands like a bracket, with covariance as the cross term. The last one is the seed of the law of large numbers (L17 in the L14 to L20 notes).

Read alongside: Bertsekas §3.6 (continuous Bayes' rule), §4.1 (sums of independent variables, convolution) and §4.2 (covariance); probabilitycourse.com §5.2.4 (functions of two continuous random variables) and §5.3.1 (covariance and correlation). There is a shorter earlier version of this lecture in study/L13-notes.md; this section replaces it.

Part A: the slides

Slide 2/18: Topics

On the slide. We have seen: conditioning; law of iterated expectations. This class: sums of random variables and convolutions; Bayes rule revisited; variance of sums of random variables; moment generating functions.

The MGF slides (14 to 18) are not in the PDF; MGFs are taught in L14 (see the last section and the L14 to L20 notes).

Slide 3/18: Sums of independent random variables

On the slide. Consider Z=X+Y. What is the pdf of Z? What is pZ​(z) or fZ​(z)? pZ​(z)=∑{(x,y):x+y=z}​pX,Y​(x,y); fZ​(z)=∫{(x,y):x+y=z}​fX,Y​(x,y)dxdy. If X and Y are independent, pX,Y​(x,y)=pX​(x)pY​(y) and fX,Y​(x,y)=fX​(x)fY​(y). This gives us the Convolution formula

pZ​(z)=x∑​pX​(x)pY​(z−x),fZ​(z)=∫−∞∞​fX​(x)fY​(z−x)dx.

HW: What if X and Y are not independent?

Where it comes from. The discrete version is L10 slide 22 with g(x,y)=x+y: collect the cells on the anti-diagonal x+y=z. Once x is fixed, y must be z−x, so there is one cell per x:

pZ​(z)=x∑​pXY​(x,z−x).

For the continuous case, go through the CDF (the safe route of L8). For independent X, Y, condition on X=x:

FZ​(z)=P(X+Y≤z)=∫fX​(x)P(Y≤z−x)dx=∫fX​(x)FY​(z−x)dx,

and differentiating under the integral gives fZ​(z)=∫fX​(x)fY​(z−x)dx.

HW solved: not independent. Nothing changes except that the joint no longer factorises:

pZ​(z)=x∑​pXY​(x,z−x),fZ​(z)=∫−∞∞​fXY​(x,z−x)dx​

(The slide's ∫{x+y=z}​fdxdy is shorthand for this line integral.) Example: (X,Y) uniform on the triangle x,y≥0, x+y≤1 (f=2). Then fZ​(z)=∫0z​2dx=2z for 0≤z≤1. (Check: FZ​(z)=z2, the area of the smaller triangle {x+y≤z} times 2.)

The recipe (where most mistakes happen).

  1. Write fX​(x)fY​(z−x).
  2. Find the x for which both factors are non-zero. This gives limits that depend on z.
  3. Split into cases of z according to how the limits change.
  4. Integrate (or sum) in each case, and state that fZ​=0 elsewhere.

Slide 4/18: Examples

On the slide. EX1: Suppose X and Y are independent and U[0,1]. Find the pdf and CDF of Z=X+Y. (Link: the Wikipedia animation "Convolution of box signal with itself".) Ex2: Suppose X and Y are outcomes of independent rolls of dice. Find the pmf of Z=X+Y.

EX1. fX​(x)=1 needs 0≤x≤1; fY​(z−x)=1 needs 0≤z−x≤1, i.e. z−1≤x≤z. So x∈[max(0,z−1),min(1,z)]:

fZ​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​∫0z​1dx=z∫z−11​1dx=2−z0​0≤z≤11≤z≤2otherwise​FZ​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​02z2​1−2(2−z)2​1​z<00≤z≤11≤z≤2z>2​

A triangle on [0,2] peaking at z=1. The animation shows one box sliding across the other; fZ​(z) is the overlap length when the shift is z. (The CDF is also immediate geometrically: {X+Y≤z} is a corner triangle of area 2z2​.)

Ex2. pZ​(z)=∑x​61​⋅61​ over the x∈{1,…,6} with z−x∈{1,…,6}, i.e. max(1,z−6)≤x≤min(6,z−1). Counting:

pZ​(z)=366−∣z−7∣​,z=2,3,…,12.

The discrete version of the same triangle. Adding more uniforms makes the shape smoother and more bell-like: three uniforms give a piecewise quadratic (Bertsekas Problem 4.12), and the limit is the central limit theorem.

Slide 5/18: Inference problem

On the slide. A block diagram: X (with density fX​(x)) → Measurement → Y (with density fY∣X​(y∣x)) → Inference → fX∣Y​(x∣y). X is an unobservable random variable with a known distribution. We only observe measurements Y that take values according to fY∣X​(y∣x). The objective is to draw inference about X having seen a realisation of Y, i.e. obtain fX∣Y​(x∣y) using only fX​(x) and fY∣X​(y∣x), both of which are known.

Vocabulary. fX​ is the prior (what we believe before measuring). fY∣X​ is the likelihood or measurement model. fX∣Y​ is the posterior (what we believe after seeing Y=y). Examples: X = transmitted bit, Y = noisy received voltage; X = disease status, Y = test result; X = bulb quality, Y = observed lifetime.

Slide 6/18: Bayes' rule revisited

On the slide.

P(B∣A)=P(A)P(A∣B)P(B)​

For continuous random variables X and Y:

fX∣Y​(x∣y)=fY​(y)fY∣X​(y∣x)fX​(x)​=∫−∞∞​fY∣X​(y∣t)fX​(t)dtfY∣X​(y∣x)fX​(x)​

For discrete random variables X and Y:

pX∣Y​(x∣y)=pY​(y)pY∣X​(y∣x)pX​(x)​=∑i​pY∣X​(y∣i)pX​(i)pY∣X​(y∣x)pX​(x)​

Proof in one line. Write the joint both ways (L12 slide 13): fX∣Y​(x∣y)fY​(y)=fXY​(x,y)=fY∣X​(y∣x)fX​(x), and divide by fY​(y). The denominator is the total probability theorem (L12 slide 20).

Working rule. posterior ∝ likelihood × prior, as a function of x, with y fixed. The denominator does not depend on x; it is just whatever constant makes the posterior integrate (or sum) to 1. Often you can recognise the posterior's shape and skip computing it.

Slide 7/18: Example 3.19 (Bertsekas), bulb lifetime

On the slide. The lifetime of a Philips bulb is assumed to be an exponential random variable Y with parameter Λ. Λ itself is a uniform random variable over [1,1.5]. You test a bulb and see that it has a lifetime of y units. What can you say about the randomness of Λ having observed Y=y? What is fΛ​(λ)? What is fY∣Λ​(y∣λ)? What is fY​(y)? Answer: fΛ∣Y​(λ∣y)=∫11.5​2te−tydt2λe−λy​ for λ∈[1,1.5].

Step by step.

  • Prior: fΛ​(λ)=1.5−11​=2 on [1,1.5].
  • Likelihood: fY∣Λ​(y∣λ)=λe−λy, y≥0.
  • Evidence: fY​(y)=∫11.5​2te−tydt (integrate by parts if you need it in closed form: ∫te−tydt=−yt​e−ty−y21​e−ty).
  • Posterior: likelihood × prior over evidence, giving the slide's answer.

Interpretation. Compare the posterior at the two ends: f(1∣y)f(1.5∣y)​=1.5e−0.5y. For a short lifetime (y<2ln1.5≈0.81) the posterior favours large λ (a bad bulb); for a long lifetime it favours small λ (a good bulb). The data pull the belief in the sensible direction.

Slide 8/18: Bayes' rule revisited (mixed case)

On the slide. For discrete N and continuous random variable Y:

P(N=n∣Y=y)=fY​(y)fY∣N​(y∣n)pN​(n)​=∑i​fY∣N​(y∣i)pN​(i)fY∣N​(y∣n)pN​(n)​

Equivalently:

fY∣N​(y∣n)=pN​(n)P(N=n∣Y=y)fY​(y)​=∫−∞∞​P(N=n∣Y=t)fY​(t)dtP(N=n∣Y=y)fY​(y)​

Rule. Use a PMF for whichever variable is discrete and a PDF for whichever is continuous; sums over discrete values, integrals over continuous ones. The structure never changes. The first form is used for detection (which of finitely many hypotheses is true?), as in the next slide.

Slide 9/18: Example 3.20 (Bertsekas), signal in noise

On the slide. Suppose X=1 w.p. p and X=−1 w.p. 1−p. While transmitting this signal, it is corrupted by Gaussian noise N∼N(0,1). We observe Y=X+N. Suppose you observe Y=y; then show that

P(X=1∣Y=y)=pey+(1−p)e−ypey​.

Intuitively, this probability goes to zero as y decreases to −∞ and increases to 1 as y increases to ∞. P(X=1∣Y=y)=fY​(y)fY∣X​(y∣1)pX​(1)​. Here fY​(y)=fY∣X​(y∣1)pX​(1)+fY∣X​(y∣−1)pX​(−1). Substitute values to obtain the answer.

Solution. Given X=1, Y=1+N∼N(1,1); given X=−1, Y∼N(−1,1) (noise independent of the signal). So

P(X=1∣Y=y)=pe−(y−1)2/2+(1−p)e−(y+1)2/2pe−(y−1)2/2​

(the 2π​1​ cancels). Multiply top and bottom by e(y2+1)/2: −2(y−1)2​+2y2+1​=y and −2(y+1)2​+2y2+1​=−y. This gives the slide's formula.

Decision rule. Decide "X=1" when this posterior exceeds 21​, i.e. pey>(1−p)e−y, i.e.

y>21​lnp1−p​.

For p=21​ the threshold is 0 (decide by the sign of y). If +1 is a priori more likely (p>21​), the threshold moves below 0: you need stronger evidence to decide −1.

Slide 10/18: Variance of sums of random variables

On the slide. Let X1​,X2​,…,Xn​ be possibly dependent and non-identical random variables. Let's say you know the joint pdf/pmf for every pair of random variables from this collection. AIM: calculate Var(Z) where Z=∑i=1n​ai​Xi​ for some scalars ai​.

Why "every pair" is enough. The answer will need only Var(Xi​) and Cov(Xi​,Xj​), and each of these involves at most two variables at a time. You do not need the full n-dimensional joint distribution.

Slide 11/18: Variance of sums, properties of covariance

On the slide. Recall Var(X)=E[X−E[X]]2=E[X2]−E[X]2. Also recall Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]. The following properties of covariance follow (HW):

  1. Cov(X,X)=Var(X)
  2. If X,Y are independent, Cov(X,Y)=0
  3. Cov(X,Y)=Cov(Y,X)
  4. Cov(aX,Y)=aCov(X,Y)
  5. Cov(X+a,Y)=Cov(X,Y)
  6. Cov(X+Z,Y)=Cov(X,Y)+Cov(Z,Y)
  7. Cov(∑i=1m​ai​Xi​,∑j=1n​bj​Yj​)=∑i=1m​∑j=1n​ai​bj​Cov(Xi​,Yj​)

HW solved. Use Cov(U,V)=E[(U−EU)(V−EV)] and linearity of expectation.

  1. Cov(X,X)=E[(X−EX)2]=Var(X).
  2. Independence gives E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] (L10 slide 14), so Cov=0.
  3. The definition is symmetric in X and Y.
  4. aX−E[aX]=a(X−EX), so the factor a comes out of the expectation.
  5. (X+a)−E[X+a]=X−EX: shifting does not change deviations from the mean.
  6. (X+Z)−E[X+Z]=(X−EX)+(Z−EZ); multiply by (Y−EY) and use linearity.
  7. Apply 4 and 6 repeatedly in the first slot, then use 3 to do the same in the second slot.

How to remember 7: covariance expands like multiplying out brackets, with constants pulled out and additive constants dropped.

Slide 12/18: Variance of sums, the derivation

On the slide. AIM: calculate Var(Z) where Z=∑i=1n​ai​Xi​. Var(Z)=Cov(Z,Z) and therefore

Cov(i∑​ai​Xi​,j∑​aj​Xj​)​=i∑​j∑​ai​aj​Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=i=1∑n​ai2​Var(Xi​)+(i,j):i=j∑​ai​aj​Cov(Xi​,Xj​).​
Var(i=1∑n​ai​Xi​)=i=1∑n​ai2​Var(Xi​)+(i,j):i=j∑​ai​aj​Cov(Xi​,Xj​)​

Reading the double sum. Put the terms ai​aj​Cov(Xi​,Xj​) in an n×n grid. The diagonal (i=j) gives the variances (property 1). Off the diagonal, the grid is symmetric (property 3), so each unordered pair appears twice:

Var(i∑​ai​Xi​)=i∑​ai2​Var(Xi​)+2i<j∑​ai​aj​Cov(Xi​,Xj​).

(In matrix form, Var(aTX)=aTCX​a with the covariance matrix of L19.)

Slide 13/18: Variance of sums, consequences

On the slide. Show that Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y). Now if the Xi​ are independent, what is Var(Z)? Let {Xi​,i=1,2,…,n} be i.i.d. and consider Sn​=n∑i=1n​Xi​​. Show that Var(Sn​)=nVar(X)​.

Solutions.

  • n=2, a1​=a2​=1: the off-diagonal pair appears twice, giving Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y). (Last year's midsem Q4 asked exactly this; a direct proof expands E[((X−EX)+(Y−EY))2].) More generally,
Var(aX+bY+c)=a2Var(X)+b2Var(Y)+2abCov(X,Y).

In particular Var(X−Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)−2Cov(X,Y): for independent variables, variances add even when you subtract.

  • Independent Xi​: every covariance is 0, so Var(∑i​ai​Xi​)=∑i​ai2​Var(Xi​). (Pairwise uncorrelated is enough.)
  • Sample mean: ai​=n1​, so Var(Sn​)=n⋅n21​Var(X)=nVar(X)​. Averaging n samples shrinks the spread by a factor n (the standard deviation by n​). Together with E[Sn​]=E[X] and Chebyshev's inequality, this gives the weak law of large numbers: P(∣Sn​−E[X]∣≥ϵ)≤nϵ2Var(X)​→0.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

Convolution

B1 (last year's HW6 Q13; Bertsekas Problem 4.10). X uniform on {1,2,3}; P(Y=0)=21​, P(Y=1)=31​, P(Y=2)=61​; independent. Find the PMF of Z=X+Y.

Solution

Solution. pZ​(z)=∑x​31​pY​(z−x), i.e. 31​ times the sum of pY​ over the allowed values z−x:

pZ​(1)=31​⋅21​=61​,pZ​(2)=31​(31​+21​)=185​,pZ​(3)=31​⋅1=31​,pZ​(4)=31​(61​+31​)=61​,pZ​(5)=31​⋅61​=181​.​

(Sum: 183+5+6+3+1​=1. ✓)

B2 (last year's HW6 Q14; Bertsekas Problem 4.11). Show that the sum of independent Poisson(μ) and Poisson(λ) is Poisson(μ+λ).

Solution

Solution. For z=0,1,2,…:

pZ​(z)=x=0∑z​e−μx!μx​e−λ(z−x)!λz−x​=z!e−(μ+λ)​x=0∑z​(xz​)μxλz−x=e−(μ+λ)z!(μ+λ)z​,

by the binomial theorem. (The limits 0≤x≤z come from needing both x≥0 and z−x≥0.)

B3 (last year's endsem Q3; Bertsekas Problem 4.8). X1​,X2​ i.i.d. Exp(λ). Find the PDF of Z=X1​+X2​ and E[Z].

Solution

Solution. Both factors are non-zero when x≥0 and z−x≥0, so for z≥0:

fZ​(z)=∫0z​λe−λxλe−λ(z−x)dx=λ2e−λz∫0z​dx=λ2ze−λz,

and 0 for z<0. This is Gamma(2,λ) (Erlang). E[Z]=λ2​ (linearity; no integral needed). With different rates λ=μ the same steps give fZ​(z)=μ−λλμ​(e−λz−e−μz).

B4 (Bertsekas Problem 4.9 / Example 4.9 variant, a difference). X∼ Exp(λ), Y∼ Exp(μ) independent. Find the PDF of W=X−Y.

Solution

Solution. W=X+(−Y) with f−Y​(t)=μeμt for t≤0. For w≥0: need x≥0 and x−w≥0, so x≥w:

fW​(w)=∫w∞​λe−λxμe−μ(x−w)dx=λ+μλμ​e−λw.

For w<0: x≥0 is the binding constraint and the same computation gives λ+μλμ​eμw. Two exponential tails glued at 0; for λ=μ it is the Laplace density 2λ​e−λ∣w∣.

B5 (last year's Tutorial 6 Q1; Bertsekas Problem 4.6). (X,Y) is uniform on the triangle with vertices (0,1), (0,−1), (1,0). Find the CDF and PDF of Z=∣X−Y∣.

Solution

Solution. The triangle is {0≤x≤1,∣y∣≤1−x}, of area 1, so fXY​=1. First find the density of U=X−Y with the dependent-convolution formula from slide 3 (for differences: fU​(u)=∫fXY​(x,x−u)dx). The point (x,x−u) is in the triangle when x≥0 and ∣x−u∣≤1−x. The second condition means x−u≤1−x (i.e. x≤21+u​) and u−x≤1−x (i.e. u≤1). So for −1≤u≤1 the allowed range is 0≤x≤21+u​. So fU​(u)=21+u​ on [−1,1]. Then Z=∣U∣ has, for 0≤z≤1,

fZ​(z)=fU​(z)+fU​(−z)=21+z​+21−z​=1,FZ​(z)=z.

So Z∼U[0,1], a surprisingly clean answer. (A simulation with 2×106 points gives quartiles 0.251, 0.501, 0.751.)

B6 (Bertsekas Problem 3.35(b), conditioning on a sum). X,Y i.i.d. Exp(λ), Z=X+Y. Find fX∣Z​(x∣z).

Solution

Solution. Bayes with fZ∣X​(z∣x)=fY​(z−x) (given X=x, Z is Y shifted by x):

fX∣Z​(x∣z)=fZ​(z)fX​(x)fY​(z−x)​=λ2ze−λzλe−λxλe−λ(z−x)​=z1​,0≤x≤z.

Given the total, the split point is uniform: knowing X+Y=z tells you nothing about where in [0,z] the first part ends. (The joint density λ2e−λ(x+y) is constant along each line x+y=z, so every split is equally likely.)

Bayes' rule

B7 (last year's HW6 Q6, the Geiger counter). The rate is λ1​=0.1 (prior 0.8) or λ2​=0.5 (prior 0.2). The count in one second is K∣λ∼ Poisson(λ). You observe K=2. Find P(λ=0.1∣K=2).

Solution

Solution. Discrete Bayes (slide 6). The likelihoods are e−0.120.12​ and e−0.520.52​; the 21​ cancels:

P(λ1​∣K=2)=0.8⋅0.01e−0.1+0.2⋅0.25e−0.50.8⋅0.01e−0.1​=0.007239+0.0303270.007239​≈0.193.

Two counts in a second is far more typical of the high rate, so the belief flips from 80% "low" to about 19% "low".

B8 (Bertsekas Problem 3.34, the defective coin minting machine). The bias P of a coin has PDF fP​(p)=pep on [0,1]. (a) Find P(H) for one toss. (b) Given heads, find the posterior of P. (c) Given heads on the first toss, find P(heads on the second).

Solution

Solution. (a) Total probability: P(H)=∫01​p⋅pepdp=[ep(p2−2p+2)]01​=e−2≈0.718. (b) Mixed Bayes (slide 8): fP∣H​(p)=e−2p⋅pep​=e−2p2ep​ on [0,1]. (c) Given P, tosses are independent, so P(H2​∣H1​)=E[P∣H1​]=e−2∫01​p3epdp​=e−26−2e​≈0.784. Seeing a head raised the probability of the next head from 0.718 to 0.784: the tosses are not independent unconditionally, only given P.

B9 (a medical test with a continuous reading). A patient is ill with probability 0.01. A blood marker Y is N(5,1) for ill patients and N(2,1) for healthy ones. The reading is y=4. Find P(ill∣Y=4).

Solution

Solution. Mixed Bayes; the normal constants cancel:

P(ill∣Y​=4)=0.01e−1/2+0.99e−(4−2)2/20.01e−(4−5)2/2​=0.006065+0.99⋅0.13530.01⋅0.6065​=0.006065+0.13400.006065​≈0.043.​

Even though y=4 is closer to the ill mean, the tiny prior keeps the posterior at about 4%. (Base-rate effect, the continuous version of the L3 disease-test example.)

Variance of sums and covariance

B10 (last year's HW6 Q9). Var(X)=4, Var(Y)=9, Cov(X,Y)=−2. Find Var(3X−2Y+5), and the value if X and Y were independent.

Solution

Solution. 9⋅4+4⋅9+2⋅3⋅(−2)⋅(−2)=36+36+24=96. Independent: 72. The "+5" never matters, and the two minus signs make the cross term positive.

B11 (Bertsekas Problem 4.18). W,X,Y,Z have mean 0, variance 1, and are pairwise uncorrelated. Let R=W+X, S=X+Y, T=Y+Z. Find ρ(R,S) and ρ(R,T).

Solution

Solution. Var(R)=Var(S)=Var(T)=2. Expand bilinearly: Cov(R,S)=Cov(W+X,X+Y)=Var(X)=1 (the only shared term), and Cov(R,T)=0 (nothing shared). So ρ(R,S)=21​ and ρ(R,T)=0.

B12 (the hat problem, Bertsekas §4.2). n people throw their hats in a box and each picks one at random. Let X be the number who get their own hat. Find E[X] and Var(X).

Solution

Solution. X=∑i=1n​Xi​ with Xi​=1{person i gets own hat}. E[Xi​]=n1​, so E[X]=1. Var(Xi​)=n1​(1−n1​). For i=j: E[Xi​Xj​]=P(both)=n1​⋅n−11​, so Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=n(n−1)1​−n21​=n2(n−1)1​. Then

Var(X)=n⋅n1​(1−n1​)+n(n−1)⋅n2(n−1)1​=1−n1​+n1​=1.

Mean 1 and variance 1 for every n≥2, the same as a Poisson(1). The ordered-pair count n(n−1) is the "∑i=j​" of slide 12.

B13 (the binomial variance in one line). X∼ Binomial(n,p) is a sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) indicators, so Var(X)=np(1−p) with no algebra. Contrast with drawing without replacement (hypergeometric: n draws from N balls of which K are red): the indicators are now negatively correlated, Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=N(N−1)K(K−1)​−N2K2​, and the variance shrinks by the factor N−1N−n​.

B14 (choosing a portfolio: minimum variance). Two assets have returns X, Y with Var(X)=σX2​, Var(Y)=σY2​, Cov(X,Y)=c. You invest a fraction a in X and 1−a in Y. Which a minimises the variance?

Solution

Solution. V(a)=a2σX2​+(1−a)2σY2​+2a(1−a)c. Setting V′(a)=0:

a∗=σX2​+σY2​−2cσY2​−c​.

For independent assets with equal variance σ2, a∗=21​ and V=2σ2​: diversification halves the risk, the two-asset version of Var(Sn​)=nVar(X)​.
