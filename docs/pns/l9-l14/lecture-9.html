Lecture 9

Right-continuity of the CDF, mixed random variables, joint PMFs

Deck 1, slides 2 to 11 · 18 min read · 9 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

The big picture. So far every random variable lived alone. This lecture finishes the story of a single random variable (why the CDF is right-continuous, and what to do when a variable is neither discrete nor continuous) and then starts the main theme of L9 to L13: several random variables defined on the same experiment. The key objects are the joint PMF and the marginals.

Read alongside: Bertsekas §2.5 (joint PMFs) and Problem 3.9 (mixed random variables); probabilitycourse.com §4.3.1 (mixed random variables) and §5.1.1 (joint PMFs).

Part A: the slides

Slide 2/26: This lecture

On the slide. Right-continuity of CDF. Mixed random variables. Multiple random variables.

Slide 3/26: For a random variable X, its CDF satisfies the following

On the slide.

  • FX​(∞)=1 and FX​(−∞)=0 when P(−∞<X<∞)=1.
  • FX​:R→[0,1] is non-decreasing and right continuous.
  • At a point of discontinuity x we have (1) the right-hand limit FX​(x+):=limϵ↓0​FX​(x+ϵ); (2) the left-hand limit FX​(x−):=limϵ↑0​FX​(x−ϵ); (3) FX​(x−)=FX​(x+); (4) FX​(x) could be set to either of the two. Which one?
  • Right continuity mandates that at a point of discontinuity we have FX​(x)=FX​(x+).
  • By default, FX​(x)=FX​(x+)=FX​(x−) if FX​ is continuous at x.

What "FX​(±∞)" means. FX​(∞) is shorthand for limx→∞​FX​(x). The condition P(−∞<X<∞)=1 just says X is a genuine real number (it never "escapes to infinity").

Answer to "which one?". Because FX​(x)=P(X≤x) uses "≤", the point x itself is included. So the value at a jump is the top of the jump, the right-hand limit:

FX​(x)=FX​(x+),FX​(x−)=P(X<x),FX​(x)−FX​(x−)=P(X=x)​.

The size of a jump is exactly the probability sitting at that point. A continuous CDF has no jumps, which is why P(X=a)=0 for continuous variables.

Example. A fair die: FX​(3)=P(X≤3)=63​, while FX​(3−)=P(X<3)=P(X≤2)=62​. The jump at 3 is 61​=P(X=3). When you draw a staircase CDF, the filled dot goes on the upper step at each jump.

Slide 4/26: Right-continuity, proof that FX​ is non-decreasing

On the slide. Consider a<b. We want to show FX​(a)≤FX​(b). Define A:={ω:X(ω)≤a} and B:={ω:X(ω)≤b}. Easy to see that A⊆B, hence P(A)≤P(B). So FX​(a)=PX​((−∞,a])=P(A)≤P(B)=FX​(b).

Why A⊆B. If X(ω)≤a and a<b then X(ω)≤b. Why P(A)≤P(B). Write B=A∪(B∖A) as a disjoint union, so P(B)=P(A)+P(B∖A)≥P(A) (monotonicity of probability, L2).

Slide 5/26: Right-continuity, the proof

On the slide. We want to prove FX​(x)=FX​(x+). Consider a sequence {xn​} decreasing to x, so FX​(x+)=limxn​↓x​FX​(xn​). Define An​:={ω:X(ω)≤xn​} and A:={ω:X(ω)≤x}. Is An​↑A or An​↓A? Clearly An​↓A. From continuity of probability, limn​P(An​)=P(A). This implies limxn​↓x​FX​(xn​)=FX​(x). You cannot prove the other way by considering xn​↑x because ⋃n​(−∞,xn​]=(−∞,x) and PX​(−∞,x)=FX​(x).

Why An​↓A. Since xn​ decreases, A1​⊇A2​⊇⋯. Their intersection is {X≤xn​ for every n}. A number that is ≤xn​ for all n is ≤limxn​=x, and conversely. So ⋂n​An​={X≤x}=A.

Why the left side fails. Take xn​↑x (strictly below x). Then An​={X≤xn​} increases, and the union is {X<x}, not {X≤x}: the point x itself is never reached. Continuity of probability gives FX​(x−)=P(X<x), which differs from FX​(x) by P(X=x). That is the whole asymmetry: "≤" in the definition of the CDF makes it right-continuous, not left-continuous.

Exam tip. A favourite short question is "prove that a CDF is right-continuous" or "show that P(X<x)=FX​(x−)". Both are this slide: pick the right monotone sequence of events and apply continuity of probability.

Slide 5/26 (section title): Mixed random variables

Slide 6/26: Mixed random variables

On the slide. Random variables that are neither continuous nor discrete are called mixed random variables. Their CDF is partly continuous and partly piecewise continuous (has jumps). Example: X is a U[0,1] random variable and Y=X if X≤0.5 and Y=0.5 if X>0.5. What is the CDF and PDF of Y?

Solution. Y=min(X,0.5). All the probability of {X>0.5}, which is 21​, gets piled onto the single point 0.5.

  • y<0: FY​(y)=0.
  • 0≤y<0.5: Y≤y happens only when X≤y (since Y=X there), so FY​(y)=P(X≤y)=y.
  • y≥0.5: Y is always ≤0.5≤y, so FY​(y)=1.
FY​(y)=⎩⎨⎧​0y1​y<00≤y<0.5y≥0.5​

The CDF climbs continuously from 0 to 0.5, then jumps from 0.5 to 1 at y=0.5. The jump size is P(Y=0.5)=P(X>0.5)=0.5, and the value at the jump is the top, as slide 3 requires.

The "PDF". There is no ordinary PDF, because of the jump. Using a Dirac delta to represent the point mass:

fY​(y)=continuous part, total mass 0.51⋅1{0≤y<0.5}​​+point mass0.5δ(y−0.5)​​.

Mean. E[Y]=∫00.5​y⋅1dy+0.5×0.5=0.125+0.25=0.375. (Check by LOTUS on X: E[min(X,0.5)]=∫00.5​xdx+∫0.51​0.5dx=0.125+0.25. ✓)

Slide 7/26: Mixed random variables, expectation

On the slide. Let FY​(y)=C(y)+D(y), where C(y) corresponds to the continuous part and D(y) to the discontinuous part. Then

E[Y]=∫−∞∞​yc(y)dy+yk​∑​yk​P(Y=yk​),

where {y1​,y2​,…} are the jump points of D(y), with P(Y=yk​)>0, and c(y)=C′(y). See section 4.3.1 of probabilitycourse.com. Example: the amount of pending workload on a server. A server on a cluster may be idle with a finite probability; if busy, the pending work is a continuous random variable.

How to split a mixed CDF. D(y) is the staircase made of the jumps: D(y)=∑yk​≤y​P(Y=yk​). Whatever is left, C(y)=FY​(y)−D(y), is continuous and non-decreasing; its derivative c(y) is the "continuous density". Note that C(∞)+D(∞)=1, so neither part integrates to 1 on its own. In the slide 6 example, D(y)=0.5⋅1{y≥0.5}, C(y)=min(max(y,0),0.5), c(y)=1 on [0,0.5).

Recipe for any mixed question.

  1. Find the point masses P(Y=yk​) (usually from a "with probability ..." clause or from a min/max that clips).
  2. Find the continuous density c(y) on the rest.
  3. Check: ∑k​P(Y=yk​)+∫c(y)dy=1.
  4. E[g(Y)]=∫g(y)c(y)dy+∑k​g(yk​)P(Y=yk​).

The server example, made concrete. Suppose the server is idle with probability 0.3 (pending work W=0), and when busy the pending work is Exponential(2). Then P(W=0)=0.3, c(w)=0.7⋅2e−2w for w>0, FW​(w)=0.3+0.7(1−e−2w) for w≥0, and E[W]=0⋅0.3+0.7⋅21​=0.35.

Slide 7/26 (section title): Multiple random variables

Slide 8/26: A running example

On the slide. Consider an experiment of tossing a coin and a die together. Ω={0,1}×{1,2,3,4,5,6}, F=2Ω, P(ω)=121​. Let X and Y denote the random variables depicting the outcome of the coin and the die respectively. For ω=(1,5) we have X(ω)=1 and Y(ω)=5. We are now interested in the joint PMF pXY​(x,y) and joint CDF FXY​(x,y) of X and Y together.

The point. X and Y are two functions on the same Ω. Each ω produces a pair (X(ω),Y(ω)). Knowing pX​ and pY​ separately does not tell you how they behave together (for example whether a head tends to come with a 6). The joint distribution does.

Slide 9/26: An example

On the slide. pXY​(x,y):=P{ω∈Ω:X(ω)=x and Y(ω)=y}. FXY​(x,y):=P{ω:X(ω)≤x and Y(ω)≤y}. We can use the PMF to calculate P((X,Y)∈A): P((X,Y)∈A)=P{ω:(X(ω),Y(ω))∈A}, therefore P((X,Y)∈A)=∑(x,y)∈A​pXY​(x,y). Suppose A is the event that you get a head and the roll is even. What is P((X,Y)∈A)?

Answer. A={(1,2),(1,4),(1,6)}, each with joint probability 121​, so P((X,Y)∈A)=123​=41​.

Why the sum formula holds. The events {X=x,Y=y} for different pairs (x,y) are disjoint (each ω gives one pair), and {(X,Y)∈A} is their union over (x,y)∈A. Countable additivity does the rest.

The joint PMF as a table. For two discrete variables, always draw the table: rows for x, columns for y, entries pXY​(x,y). Here it is a 2×6 table with every entry 121​. Probabilities of events are sums of cells; marginals are row and column sums (next slide).

Slide 10/26: Marginals

On the slide. What is pXY​(1,i)? (=121​.) ∑i​pXY​(1,i)=P{ω:X(ω)=1}=21​=pX​(1). Similarly pXY​(1,i)+pXY​(0,i)=61​=pY​(i). The marginal PMFs pX​ and pY​ can be obtained from the joint PMF as follows: pX​(x)=∑y​pXY​(x,y) and pY​(y)=∑x​pXY​(x,y). This is true in general, and requires a proof.

In the table picture: pX​ is the row sums, pY​ the column sums, written in the margins of the table, hence the name.

Warning. The marginals do not determine the joint. Example: X and Y both Bernoulli(21​). Joint 1: all four cells 41​ (independent coins). Joint 2: p(0,0)=p(1,1)=21​ (so Y=X). Joint 3: p(0,1)=p(1,0)=21​ (so Y=1−X). Same marginals, three completely different joints. Going joint → marginal is always possible; marginal → joint needs extra information (such as independence, L10).

Slide 11/26: Marginals, the proof

On the slide.

pX​(x)​=P{ω:X(ω)=x}=P{y⋃​{ω:X(ω)=x,Y(ω)=y}}=y∑​P{ω:X(ω)=x,Y(ω)=y}.​

Line by line. (1) Definition of the PMF. (2) Y takes some value, so {X=x} splits according to which value Y took. (3) The pieces {X=x,Y=y} for different y are disjoint, and Y takes countably many values, so countable additivity applies. This is just the total probability theorem with the partition {Y=y}y​.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (Bertsekas Problem 3.9 idea; probabilitycourse.com §4.3.1). X∼U[−1,1] and Y=max(X,0). Find FY​, the point masses, and E[Y], E[Y2].

Solution

Solution. Y=0 whenever X≤0, so P(Y=0)=21​. For 0≤y≤1: FY​(y)=P(X≤y)=2y+1​. So FY​(y)=0 for y<0, 21+y​ for 0≤y<1, 1 for y≥1; a jump of 21​ at 0, then slope 21​. Continuous density c(y)=21​ on (0,1).

E[Y]=0⋅21​+∫01​y⋅21​dy=41​,E[Y2]=∫01​y2⋅21​dy=61​,Var(Y)=61​−161​=485​.

B2 (last year's HW5 Q1). With probability 0.4, Y=2; with probability 0.6, Y∼ Exp(1). Find P(Y≥1) and the CDF of Y.

Solution

Solution. Condition on which case happens (total probability).

P(Y≥1)=0.4⋅1+0.6⋅e−1≈0.4+0.2207=0.6207.

CDF: P(Y≤y)=0.41{y≥2}+0.6(1−e−y) for y≥0, i.e.

FY​(y)=⎩⎨⎧​00.6(1−e−y)0.4+0.6(1−e−y)​y<00≤y<2y≥2​

with a jump of 0.4 at y=2. (E[Y]=0.4⋅2+0.6⋅1=1.4.)

B3 (last year's HW5 Q7; also probabilitycourse.com §4.3.1). fX​(x)=2x on [0,1], and Y=X if X≤21​, Y=21​ if X>21​. Find the CDF of Y.

Solution

Solution. Same shape as slide 6. For 0≤y<21​: FY​(y)=P(X≤y)=y2. For y≥21​: FY​(y)=1. So

FY​(y)=⎩⎨⎧​0y21​y<00≤y<21​y≥21​​

with a jump of 1−41​=43​=P(X>21​) at y=21​. (E[Y]=∫01/2​y⋅2ydy+21​⋅43​=121​+83​=2411​.)

B4 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q7, the taxi and the bus). With probability 21​ a taxi is waiting (wait X=0). Otherwise the next taxi comes at T∼U[0,8] minutes and the bus comes at exactly 5 minutes; you take whichever comes first. Find the CDF of X and E[X].

Solution

Solution. With no taxi waiting, X=min(T,5). Point masses: P(X=0)=21​ and P(X=5)=21​P(T>5)=21​⋅83​=163​. Continuous part on (0,5): density 21​⋅81​=161​. Check: 21​+163​+5⋅161​=1. ✓

FX​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​021​+16x​1​x<00≤x<5x≥5​E[X]=∫05​16x​dx+5⋅163​=3225​+3230​=3255​≈1.72 min.

B5 (last year's Tutorial 5 Q4, producer and consumer). A producer makes b items with probability p and nothing otherwise; the consumer tries to take T∼ Exp(λ) items. Items consumed: C=0 if nothing is produced, else min(T,b). Find the distribution of C and E[C].

Solution

Solution. Point masses: P(C=0)=1−p (the event T=0 has probability 0), and P(C=b)=pP(T≥b)=pe−λb. Continuous part on (0,b): c(t)=pλe−λt. Check: (1−p)+pe−λb+p(1−e−λb)=1. ✓

E[C]=pE[min(T,b)]=p(∫0b​tλe−λtdt+be−λb)=p∫0b​P(T>t)dt=λp(1−e−λb)​.

(The middle step uses the tail formula E[W]=∫0∞​P(W>t)dt for W≥0, last year's Tutorial 4 Q3, with P(min(T,b)>t)=e−λt for t<b and 0 after.)

B6 (last year's HW4 Q8(b), a mixed variable in disguise). A dart lands uniformly in a disc of radius r; X is its distance from the centre, so FX​(x)=r2x2​ on [0,r]. Score: S=X1​ if X≤t (with t<r), else S=0. Find FS​. Is S continuous?

Solution

Solution. P(S=0)=P(X>t)=1−r2t2​, a point mass, so S is not continuous: it is mixed. When X≤t, S=X1​≥t1​. So FS​(s)=0 for s<0, FS​(s)=1−r2t2​ for 0≤s<t1​, and for s≥t1​:

FS​(s)=P(S=0)+P(s1​≤X≤t)=1−r2t2​+r2t2−1/s2​=1−s2r21​.

B7 (Bertsekas Problem 2.24, the stock trader). (X,Y) is uniform over the integer pairs with −2≤x≤4 and −1≤y−x≤1. (a) Find the marginal PMFs and the means. (b) The trader holds 100 shares of A and 200 of B; find the mean profit 100X+200Y.

Solution

Solution. For each of the 7 values of x there are 3 values of y (x−1,x,x+1): 21 equally likely points, each 211​. (a) pX​(x)=213​=71​ for x=−2,…,4, so E[X]=1. Y ranges over −3,…,5; count the points in each column: y=−3 and 5: 1 point each; y=−2 and 4: 2 each; y=−1,…,3: 3 each. So pY​=211​(1,2,3,3,3,3,3,2,1), symmetric about 1, and E[Y]=1. (b) By linearity (L10 slide 21), E[100X+200Y]=100+200=300.

B8 (last year's Tutorial 5 Q1). pXY​(x,y)=2x+y1​ for x,y=1,2,3,… Find the marginals. Are X, Y independent? Find P(X2+Y≤10).

Solution

Solution. pX​(x)=∑y≥1​2−x2−y=2−x⋅1=(21​)x: Geometric(21​). Same for Y. Since pXY​=pX​pY​ for every pair, they are independent (L10). For the event, list x (only x≤3 works, since x=4 needs Y≤−6):

P=21​P(Y≤9)+41​P(Y≤6)+81​P(Y≤1)=21​(1−2−9)+41​(1−2−6)+161​=1024827​≈0.808.

B9 (last year's HW5 Q4, two coins). A box has a fair coin and a coin with P(H)=32​. Pick one at random and toss it (X), then toss the other one (Y). Find pXY​. Are X and Y independent?

Solution

Solution. Condition on which coin goes first (each with probability 21​):

pXY​(1,1)=21​(21​⋅32​)+21​(32​⋅21​)=31​,pXY​(1,0)=21​⋅21​⋅31​+21​⋅32​⋅21​=41​,
pXY​(0,1)=41​ (by symmetry),pXY​(0,0)=21​⋅21​⋅31​+21​⋅31​⋅21​=61​.

Marginals: pX​(1)=31​+41​=127​=pY​(1). Then pX​(1)pY​(1)=14449​=14448​=pXY​(1,1): not independent (only slightly). Intuition: a head on the first toss makes it a bit more likely that the biased coin went first, so the second coin is more likely to be the fair one.
