Lecture 11

Conditioning a random variable on an event

Deck 2, slides 2 to 11 · 14 min read · 6 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

The big picture. Conditioning is the most powerful tool in this course: if a problem is hard, condition on something that would make it easy, then average. This lecture does it for events: given that event A happened, what is the new PMF of X, and what is its new mean? Then it splits Ω into pieces and puts the pieces back together (the total probability and total expectation theorems for random variables).

Read alongside: Bertsekas §2.6 (conditioning) and §3.5 (conditioning for continuous variables); probabilitycourse.com §5.1.3 (conditioning and independence) and §5.1.5 (conditional expectation).

Part A: the slides

Slide 2/36: Today's class

On the slide. Conditioning X on an event A∈F. Conditional expectation E[X∣A]. Conditioning X with disjoint partitions {Ai​} of Ω. Conditioning X on an event {X∈A}∈F′. Conditioning X on another random variable Y. Conditional expectation E[X∣Y=y].

The last two items are L12. F′ is the σ-algebra on the range of X (for real-valued X, the Borel sets), so "{X∈A} with A∈F′" is an event described through the value of X, such as {X≥3}.

Slide 3/36: A new running example

On the slide. Pick 2 integers from {1,2,3} without replacement. Ω={(1,2),(1,3),(2,1),(2,3),(3,1),(3,2)}, P{ω}=61​ for all ω. Denote them by random variables X and Y. For ω=(1,3), X(ω)=1 and Y(ω)=3. Write down their joint PMF pX,Y​(x,y). Write down their marginal PMFs pX​ and pY​. What is E[X], E[Y] and E[XY]?

Solution. The joint table has 61​ off the diagonal and 0 on it:

Y=1 Y=2 Y=3 pX​
X=1 0 61​ 61​ 31​
X=2 61​ 0 61​ 31​
X=3 61​ 61​ 0 31​
pY​ 31​ 31​ 31​

Both marginals are uniform on {1,2,3}, so E[X]=E[Y]=2. For E[XY], add xy over the six off-diagonal cells: 61​(2+3+2+6+3+6)=622​=311​. So Cov(X,Y)=311​−4=−31​: dependent, and negatively related (a big first number leaves smaller numbers for the second). The zeros on the diagonal already showed dependence.

Slide 4/36: Conditioning on an event A

On the slide. Consider a discrete random variable X with PMF pX​(x). Suppose an event A∈F has happened. Consider the event {ω:X(ω)=x}, shorthand {X=x}. What is P(X=x∣A)? P(X=x∣A)=P(A)P({X=x}∩A)​.

pX∣A​(x):=P(A)P({X=x}∩A)​​

pX∣A​ denotes the conditional PMF of X under event A. In the running example say A is the event that the first number is odd and the second is even: A={(1,2),(3,2)}. Compute pX∣A​(⋅). How do we know that it is consistent, i.e., ∑x​pX∣A​(x)=1?

Solution. P(A)=62​=31​. {X=1}∩A={(1,2)} and {X=3}∩A={(3,2)}, each of probability 61​. So

pX∣A​(1)=1/31/6​=21​,pX∣A​(3)=21​,pX∣A​(2)=0.

Picture. Restrict attention to the outcomes in A, throw the rest away, and rescale the survivors so they add up to 1. Conditioning never creates new possibilities; it only removes some and rescales the rest.

Slide 5/36: Consistency of the conditional PMF

On the slide. ∑x​pX∣A​(x)=1. Proof: ∑x​pX∣A​(x)=∑x​P(A)P({X=x}∩A)​. {ω:X(ω)=x} are disjoint sets for different x. From the theorem of total probability, this implies that {X=x}∩A are disjoint sets for all x. ∑x​pX∣A​(x)=P(A)P(⋃x​{{X=x}∩A})​=P(A)P(A)​=1.

The key step. The sets {X=x} partition Ω (X takes exactly one value), so the pieces {X=x}∩A partition A. Their probabilities add up to P(A). In fact pX∣A​ is just the PMF of X under the new probability measure P(⋅∣A), which is a legitimate probability measure (L3). So everything you know about PMFs carries over to conditional PMFs: expectations, LOTUS, variances.

Slide 6/36: Another example

On the slide. Let X denote the outcome of a die. Let A denote the event that the roll is odd. What is pX∣A​(x)? Given that event A has happened, what is the average value of the die, i.e., E[X∣A]?

E[X∣A]=x∑​xpX∣A​(x)​Using LOTUS,E[g(X)∣A]=x∑​g(x)pX∣A​(x)​

Solution. P(A)=21​, so pX∣A​(x)=1/21/6​=31​ for x∈{1,3,5} and 0 otherwise. Then E[X∣A]=31+3+5​=3. With LOTUS, E[X2∣A]=31+9+25​=335​, so the conditional variance is Var(X∣A)=335​−9=38​.

Slide 7/36: Today's class (agenda, item 3 highlighted)

Slide 8/36: Conditioning with disjoint partitions

On the slide. Now let {Ai​,i=1,…,n} be a disjoint partition of Ω. Prove the following using the law of total probability:

pX​(x)=i=1∑n​P(Ai​)pX∣Ai​​(x)​

Proof: ∑i=1n​P(Ai​)P(Ai​)P({X=x}∩Ai​)​=∑i=1n​P({X=x}∩Ai​)=P({X=x}). The last equality follows from the law of total probability. An important consequence is

E[X]=i=1∑n​P(Ai​)E[X∣Ai​]​

Proof of the consequence (total expectation theorem). Multiply the first box by x and sum over x, then swap the two finite/absolutely convergent sums:

E[X]=x∑​xi∑​P(Ai​)pX∣Ai​​(x)=i∑​P(Ai​)x∑​xpX∣Ai​​(x)=i∑​P(Ai​)E[X∣Ai​].

In words: the overall mean is the probability-weighted average of the conditional means. Check with the die: A1​ = odd, A2​ = even. E[X∣A1​]=3, E[X∣A2​]=4, so E[X]=21​⋅3+21​⋅4=3.5. ✓

The divide-and-conquer recipe. When E[X] is hard to compute directly, find a partition (the outcome of the first step, which coin was picked, which case of a mixed variable happens) such that each E[X∣Ai​] is easy. Then average. B1 to B4 below and the geometric example on slide 11 all work this way.

Slide 9/36: Today's class (agenda, item 4 highlighted)

Slide 10/36: Conditioning on the event X∈A

On the slide. Consider a discrete random variable X with PMF pX​(x). Suppose an event X∈A has happened, where A∈F′. X∈A={ω:X(ω)∈A} and P{X∈A}=∑x∈A​pX​(x). We use the same notation pX∣A​(x):=P(X∈A)P({X=x}∩{X∈A})​. If x∈/A, we have pX∣A​(x)=0. Otherwise (when x∈A) we have pX∣A​(x)=P(X∈A)pX​(x)​. Running example: suppose we are given X∈A where A={2,3}. What is pX∣A​(x)?

Why it simplifies. When the event is about X itself, {X=x}∩{X∈A} is either {X=x} (if x∈A) or empty (if not). So you just keep the PMF on A and divide by its total mass:

pX∣A​(x)=⎩⎨⎧​P(X∈A)pX​(x)​0​x∈Ax∈/A​

Running example. pX​ is uniform on {1,2,3}, P(X∈{2,3})=32​, so pX∣A​(2)=pX∣A​(3)=2/31/3​=21​. (A conditioned uniform is uniform on what is left.)

Continuous version (L12 slide 21 and Bertsekas §3.5). fX∣A​(x)=P(X∈A)fX​(x)​ for x∈A, and 0 otherwise. Example: X∼ Exp(λ) and A={X>t}. Then fX∣A​(x)=e−λtλe−λx​=λe−λ(x−t) for x>t: a fresh exponential started at t. This is the memoryless property, and it gives E[X∣X>t]=t+λ1​.

Slide 11/36: Revisiting the geometric random variable

On the slide. Let N be a geometric random variable with parameter p. Its PMF is pN​(k)=(1−p)k−1p. Suppose we are given the event A:={N>n}; P(A)=(1−p)n. What is pN∣A​(k)? For k>n, pN∣A​(k)=P(N>n)P{(N>n)∩(N=k)}​=(1−p)k−1−np. For k≤n, pN∣A​(k)=0.

Why P(N>n)=(1−p)n. N>n means the first n trials all failed.

What the answer says: memorylessness. Put k=n+m. Then

P(N=n+m∣N>n)=(1−p)m−1p=P(N=m).

Given that the first n trials failed, the number of extra trials needed, N−n, is again Geometric(p). The coin has no memory of past failures. The geometric is the only memoryless distribution on {1,2,…}, just as the exponential is the only memoryless continuous one, and the geometric is the discrete version of the exponential (last year's HW4 Q2 makes this precise).

Consequence: the mean of the geometric in two lines (Bertsekas Example 2.17). Partition on the first trial: A1​={N=1} (probability p) and A2​={N>1} (probability 1−p). Then E[N∣A1​]=1 and, by memorylessness, E[N∣A2​]=1+E[N]. Total expectation:

E[N]=p⋅1+(1−p)(1+E[N]) ⇒ pE[N]=1 ⇒ E[N]=p1​​.

The same trick with E[N2∣A2​]=E[(1+N)2]=1+2E[N]+E[N2] gives E[N2]=p22−p​ and Var(N)=p21−p​.

Part B: extra worked examples and problems

B1 (last year's HW5 Q10). Two fair dice, S=X+Y. A1​={S≤7}, A2​={S≥8}. (a) Find pS∣A1​​ and pS∣A2​​. (b) Find P(S=6) from them.

Solution

Solution. pS​(s)=366−∣s−7∣​. P(A1​)=361+2+3+4+5+6​=3621​ and P(A2​)=3615​. Conditioning on an event about S (slide 10):

pS∣A1​​(s)=21/36(s−1)/36​=21s−1​, s=2,…,7;pS∣A2​​(s)=1513−s​, s=8,…,12.

(b) Slide 8: P(S=6)=P(A1​)pS∣A1​​(6)+P(A2​)pS∣A2​​(6)=3621​⋅215​+0=365​. ✓

B2 (Bertsekas Problem 3.18). fX​(x)=4x​ for 1<x≤3. Let A={X≥2}. Find E[X], P(A), fX∣A​ and E[X∣A].

Solution

Solution. E[X]=∫13​4x2​dx=1227−1​=613​. P(A)=∫23​4x​dx=89−4​=85​. So fX∣A​(x)=5/8x/4​=52x​ for 2≤x≤3, and

E[X∣A]=∫23​52x2​dx=152(27−8)​=1538​≈2.53.

(Check with slide 8: P(Ac)=83​, fX∣Ac​=32x​ on (1,2), E[X∣Ac]=92(8−1)​=914​, and 85​⋅1538​+83​⋅914​=1219​+127​=613​. ✓)

B3 (memorylessness used twice; Bertsekas Problem 3.20, the absent-minded professor). Two appointments are scheduled for the same time. Durations are independent Exp with mean 30 minutes. Student 1 arrives on time; student 2 arrives 5 minutes late. Find the expected time from student 1's arrival until student 2 leaves.

Solution

Solution. Let T1​ be student 1's duration. Partition on A={T1​≤5} (student 1 is done before student 2 arrives) and Ac.

  • On A (probability 1−e−5/30): student 2 starts at minute 5, so the total is 5+T2​, mean 5+30=35.
  • On Ac (probability e−1/6): student 2 waits until T1​ ends. By memorylessness, E[T1​∣T1​>5]=5+30. Then student 2 takes another T2​, so the mean total is 35+30=65.
E=35(1−e−1/6)+65e−1/6=35+30e−1/6≈35+25.4=60.4 minutes.

B4 (expected number of tosses to see two heads in a row). A fair coin is tossed until HH appears. Find the expected number of tosses N.

Solution

Solution. Partition on the first toss, and then on the second (each time the problem restarts, the mean is E[N] again):

  • First toss T (probability 21​): wasted 1 toss, restart: mean 1+E[N].
  • First H, second T (probability 41​): wasted 2 tosses, restart: mean 2+E[N].
  • First H, second H (probability 41​): done in 2.
E[N]=21​(1+E[N])+41​(2+E[N])+41​⋅2 ⇒ 41​E[N]=23​ ⇒ E[N]=6.

B5 (last year's Tutorial 4 Q2, rounding up an exponential). X∼ Exp(1) and N=⌈X⌉. Find the PMF of N and E[N]. Then find E[X∣N=n].

Solution

Solution. P(N=n)=P(n−1<X≤n)=e−(n−1)−e−n=(e−1)n−1(1−e−1): Geometric with p=1−e−1, so E[N]=1−e−11​≈1.582. Given N=n, X is the exponential restricted to (n−1,n]; by memorylessness it is n−1 plus an Exp(1) conditioned to be ≤1, so

E[X∣N=n]=n−1+1−e−1∫01​xe−xdx​=n−1+1−e−11−2e−1​≈n−1+0.418=n−0.582.

(Total expectation check: E[X]=E[N]−0.582=1.582−0.582=1. ✓)

B6 (truncated normal; a conditional expectation used in Black–Scholes, last year's HW4 Q12). Z∼N(0,1) and c≥0. Find E[Z∣Z>c] and E[max(Z−c,0)].

Solution

Solution. ∫c∞​zφ(z)dz=[−φ(z)]c∞​=φ(c), since φ′(z)=−zφ(z). So

E[Z∣Z>c]=1−Φ(c)φ(c)​E[max(Z−c,0)]=P(Z>c)(E[Z∣Z>c]−c)=φ(c)−c(1−Φ(c)).​

(The second is total expectation over {Z>c} and {Z≤c}; on the second piece the payoff is 0.)
