The Lecture 15 exercise set, solved

21 min read · 14 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

Lecture 15 (repeated as Lecture 17) ends with a long exercise set. The reductions in it (vertex capacities, Menger, project selection, circulations, matrix rounding) are in the previous chapter. This chapter solves the rest: the numerical warm-ups, the worst case for Ford–Fulkerson, both fixes with full proofs, the eight "packing versus covering" duality exercises, and the coal-and-steel case study.

Numerical warm-ups

LF1 (Lecture 15, valid flows). The candidate flow (labels f/c) is s→a 5/6, s→b 4/4, a→b 2/3, a→c 3/5, b→c 6/7, c→t 8/10. (1) Check every capacity constraint. (2) Check conservation at a, b, c. Is it a feasible flow? (3) If not, find the defective node, the discrepancy, and a minimal fix that keeps the capacities. (4) What is val(f) after the fix?

Solution

Solution. (1) 5≤6, 4≤4, 2≤3, 3≤5, 6≤7, 8≤10: every edge satisfies 0≤f(e)≤c(e).

(2) Conservation, in = out:

Node In Out Verdict
a f(s,a)=5 f(a,b)+f(a,c)=2+3=5 ✓
b f(s,b)+f(a,b)=4+2=6 f(b,c)=6 ✓
c f(a,c)+f(b,c)=3+6=9 f(c,t)=8 ✗

Not feasible: conservation fails at c.

(3) c accumulates 1 unit (9 in, 8 out). The smallest fix is to raise f(c,t) from 8 to 9, which is allowed since 9≤10. (Lowering an inflow instead, say f(b,c) to 5, would break conservation at b and need a second change.)

(4) val(f)=f(s,a)+f(s,b)=5+4=9, which also equals the inflow to t, f(c,t)=9, as it must.

LF2 (Lecture 15, cut capacities with opposing and internal edges). Edges and capacities: s→u 8, s→v 5, u→w 4, u→v 3, v→w 2, w→v 7, w→t 6, v→t 1. Find C(P,Q) for P1​={s}, P2​={s,u,v}, P3​={s,u,w}, P4​={s,u,v,w}, listing forward, backward and internal edges.

Solution

Solution. Only forward edges (P→Q) count toward capacity.

P Forward (P→Q) Backward (Q→P) Internal C(P,Q)
{s} s→u 8, s→v 5 none none 13
{s,u,v} u→w 4, v→w 2, v→t 1 w→v (7, not counted) s→u, s→v, u→v 7
{s,u,w} s→v 5, u→v 3, w→v 7, w→t 6 v→w (2, not counted) s→u, u→w 21
{s,u,v,w} w→t 6, v→t 1 none all other edges 7

The opposing pair v→w / w→v shows the point: whichever of the two points from P to Q counts, the other is ignored. The min cut has capacity 7. A flow of 7 exists (s→u→w→t 4, s→v→w→t 2, s→v→t 1), so by weak duality both are optimal.

LF3 (Lecture 15, cut invariance with back-flow). A flow of value 10: f(s,a)=7, f(s,b)=3, f(a,b)=2, f(a,t)=5, f(b,t)=5. For P={s,b}, Q={a,t}: compute the flow out of P, the flow back into P, the net flow, and explain why the back-flow term matters.

Solution

Solution. Out of P: f(s,a)+f(b,t)=7+5=12. Back from Q into P: f(a,b)=2. Net: f(P,Q)=12−2=10=val(f) ✓. Without the back-flow term we would report 12, more than the 10 units that leave s: the 2 units on a→b cross the cut forwards (on s→a) and then backwards (on a→b), and are counted twice unless the backward crossing is subtracted.

LF4 (Lecture 15, many sources and sinks, in full). Factories s1​..sk​ each produce at most Si​; hubs t1​..tℓ​ each absorb at most Tj​; intermediate nodes conserve flow. Reduce to single-source max flow, prove it, and interpret the min cut.

Solution

Solution. Construction. V′=V∪{s∗,t∗}; add s∗→si​ with capacity Si​ and tj​→t∗ with capacity Tj​; keep every original edge and capacity.

Correctness. Given a feasible multi-source flow f (each si​ sends net ≤Si​, each tj​ receives net ≤Tj​), set f′(s∗,si​) = net output of si​ and f′(tj​,t∗) = net intake of tj​. Capacities hold by the supply and demand limits, conservation now holds at si​ and tj​ too, and val(f′)=∑i​(output of si​)=val(f). Conversely, restricting a flow f′ in G′ to E gives a flow in which si​ sends f′(s∗,si​)≤Si​ and tj​ receives f′(tj​,t∗)≤Tj​, of the same value. Values match both ways, so max flows correspond.

Min cut. If the min cut consists of the edges out of s∗, production is the bottleneck; if it consists of original edges, the transmission lines are.

Ford–Fulkerson can be slow, and two fixes

LF5 (Lecture 15, worst case for Ford–Fulkerson). (1) Give a family GM​ with at most 6 vertices and 10 edges where Ford–Fulkerson (with an unlucky path choice) needs exactly 2M augmentations to reach the max flow 2M. (2) Trace the first 4 augmentations. (3) For M=260, how long does this take at 109 operations per second?

Solution

Solution. (1) Vertices s,u,v,t; edges s→u, s→v, u→t, v→t of capacity M, and a middle edge u→v of capacity 1. The max flow is 2M (the cut {s} has capacity 2M, and the two outer paths carry M each).

(2) Alternate between the two paths that use the middle edge:

Step Path in Gf​ Bottleneck Effect on u→v Value
1 s→u→v→t 1 f(u,v)=1; residual now has only the back edge v→u 1
2 s→v→u→t (back edge v→u) 1 cancels: f(u,v)=0 2
3 s→u→v→t 1 f(u,v)=1 again 3
4 s→v→u→t 1 cancelled again 4

Each step pushes 1 unit and then undoes the middle edge, so the algorithm keeps "pushing and cancelling" the same bottleneck edge, and needs 2M augmentations. A sensible choice (s→u→t, then s→v→t) finishes in 2.

(3) 2M=261≈2.3×1018 augmentations, each scanning about 10 edges: about 2.3×1019 operations, so 2.3×1010 seconds, roughly 700 years. That is (just) within the age of the universe, but useless. The O(∣E∣∣f∗∣) bound is pseudo-polynomial: it depends on the numerical value M, which takes only logM bits to write.

LF6 (Lecture 15, capacity scaling). Let U=maxe​c(e) and Δ the largest power of 2 with Δ≤U. In each phase augment only along paths in Gf​(Δ) (residual edges with cf​(e)≥Δ); when none is left set Δ←⌊Δ/2⌋; stop when Δ<1. (1) How many phases? (2) Prove each phase makes at most 2∣E∣ augmentations. (3) Conclude the running time.

Solution

Solution. (1) Δ runs through 2⌊log2​U⌋,…,2,1: 1+⌊log2​U⌋=O(logU) phases. The last phase has Δ=1, which is plain Ford–Fulkerson, so the algorithm ends with a max flow (integer capacities).

(2) End of a Δ-phase. Let A be the set of vertices reachable from s in Gf​(Δ); t∈/A. Every residual edge leaving A has cf​(e)<Δ, so the residual capacity of the cut (A,V∖A) is less than ∣E∣Δ. (An original edge (x,y) gives residual edges x→y and y→x, and at most one of them goes from A to V∖A, so at most ∣E∣ residual edges leave A, each with capacity below Δ.) Since any further flow must cross this cut in Gf​, val(f∗)−val(f)≤∣E∣Δ.

The next phase uses Δ/2: each augmentation pushes at least Δ/2, so it makes at most ∣E∣Δ/(Δ/2)=2∣E∣ augmentations. The first phase also obeys this: val(f∗)≤C({s},V∖{s})≤∣E∣U<2∣E∣Δ, and each augmentation pushes ≥Δ.

(3) O(logU) phases × O(∣E∣) augmentations × O(∣E∣) per BFS/DFS =O(∣E∣2logU)​. This is weakly polynomial: polynomial in the input length, but it still depends (logarithmically) on the numbers.

LF7 (Lecture 15, Edmonds–Karp in full). Ford–Fulkerson that always augments along a shortest path (fewest edges, by BFS). Let δf​(s,v) be the BFS distance in Gf​. Prove (1) δf​(s,v) never decreases; (2) each edge is critical at most ∣V∣/2 times; (3) the total time is O(∣V∣∣E∣2).

Solution

Solution. (1) Distances never decrease. Let one augmentation turn f into f′, and suppose some vertex got closer: δ′(v)<δ(v). Among such vertices choose v with the smallest δ′(v); v=s. Let u be the vertex before v on a shortest s–v path in Gf′​, so δ′(u)=δ′(v)−1, and by the choice of v, δ′(u)≥δ(u).

  • If (u,v) was already an edge of Gf​: δ(v)≤δ(u)+1≤δ′(u)+1=δ′(v), contradicting δ′(v)<δ(v).
  • If (u,v) is new in Gf′​: it appeared because the augmentation pushed flow along (v,u), which lies on a shortest path in Gf​, so δ(u)=δ(v)+1. Then δ(v)=δ(u)−1≤δ′(u)−1=δ′(v)−2<δ′(v), again a contradiction.

(2) Critical edges. (u,v) is critical if it is the bottleneck, cf​(u,v)=Δ; it then vanishes from the residual graph. At that moment it is on a shortest path, so δ(v)=δ(u)+1. To become critical again it must first reappear, which needs flow pushed along (v,u) on a later shortest path, at a time f′′ with δ′′(u)=δ′′(v)+1≥δ(v)+1=δ(u)+2 (using (1)). So between two consecutive times (u,v) is critical, δ(u) grows by at least 2. Distances are at most ∣V∣−1 (or ∞ once unreachable), so (u,v) is critical at most (∣V∣−1)/2<∣V∣/2 times.

(3) Every augmentation has at least one critical edge, there are at most 2∣E∣ residual edges, so there are at most 2∣E∣⋅∣V∣/2=∣V∣∣E∣ augmentations, each O(∣E∣) by BFS: O(∣V∣∣E∣2)​. This is strongly polynomial: no dependence on the capacities at all, so it also terminates with irrational capacities.

Packing versus covering: weak duality everywhere

Max flow ≤ min cut is one instance of a general pattern: the size of any packing (things that cannot share) is at most the size of any covering (things that must touch every packed item), because each packed item uses up a different covering element. The lecture's exercises:

LF8 (Exercise 3, independent sets versus clique partitions). If V is partitioned into cliques K1​..Kk​, show every independent set has at most one vertex from each clique, so α(G)≤k; conclude α(G)≤θ(G), the fewest cliques that partition V.

Solution

Solution. Two vertices of the same clique are adjacent, and an independent set contains no adjacent pair, so it meets each Ki​ in at most one vertex. The Ki​ cover V, so ∣I∣=∑i​∣I∩Ki​∣≤k. This holds for every independent set I and every clique partition, so the maximum of the left side is at most the minimum of the right: α(G)≤θ(G). (Equality can fail: on C5​, α=2 but θ=3.)

LF9 (Exercise 4, intervals). Call a set of intervals compatible if no two overlap, and a clique if they share a common point. (1) If the intervals are partitioned into k cliques, show a compatible set has at most one interval from each, so max compatible ≤k. (2) Relate this to d, the largest number of intervals overlapping at one point.

Solution

Solution. (1) Two intervals in the same clique share a point, so they overlap; a compatible set takes at most one from each clique, hence at most k in total. This is LF8 for the interval graph.

(2) Read literally, "max compatible ≤d" is false (two disjoint intervals have d=1 and a compatible set of size 2). The correct duality pairs with (1): the greedy earliest-finish algorithm picks compatible intervals J1​,J2​,…,Jr​, and the finish points of the Ji​ "stab" every interval (any interval not chosen overlaps some chosen Ji​ that ends no later than it, so it contains that finish point). The intervals through each finish point form a clique, so r cliques cover everything and max compatible =r= min cliques: equality for intervals. The number d belongs to the other duality: a point covered by d intervals needs d different rooms, so d≤ min rooms (a partition into compatible sets), and interval partitioning shows min rooms =d (greedy chapter).

LF10 (Exercises 5 and 6, chains, antichains and Dilworth). In a poset, a chain has every pair comparable and an antichain has no pair comparable. Let a(P) = largest antichain, c(P) = fewest chains partitioning P. (1) Show a(P)≤c(P). (2) Give a 4-element poset with equality. (3) Prove a(P)=c(P) (Dilworth).

Solution

Solution. (1) Two elements of one chain are comparable, so an antichain has at most one element in each chain of a partition: ∣A∣≤k for every antichain and every chain partition, so a(P)≤c(P).

(2) P={a,b,c,d} with a≺b and c≺d only. Chains {a,b}, {c,d} give c(P)≤2; {a,c} is an antichain, so a(P)≥2; with (1), a(P)=c(P)=2.

(3) Dilworth via König. Build a bipartite graph with left copies x− and right copies x+ of each element, and an edge x−y+ whenever x≺y.

  • Matching → chains. A matching M says "y follows x" for each edge x−y+; each element has at most one successor and one predecessor, and by transitivity following the edges gives chains. Each matched edge merges two chains, so there are n−∣M∣ chains: c(P)≤n−ν, with ν the maximum matching.
  • Cover → antichain. By König there is a vertex cover C with ∣C∣=ν. Let A be the elements x with neither x− nor x+ in C; then ∣A∣≥n−∣C∣=n−ν. If x≺y were both in A, the edge x−y+ would be uncovered. So A is an antichain: a(P)≥n−ν.

So c(P)≤n−ν≤a(P)≤c(P), and all are equal. (MF3 above is the same argument for path covers of a DAG.)

LF11 (Exercises 7 and 8, set packing versus hitting sets; paths versus cuts). (1) A packing is a family of pairwise disjoint sets from S; a hitting set H meets every set of S. Show ∣P∣≤∣H∣. (2) If P1​..Pk​ are edge-disjoint s–t paths and C is an s–t cut (a set of edges whose removal disconnects t from s), show k≤∣C∣, and compare with matchings and vertex covers.

Solution

Solution. (1) For each set S∈P pick an element hS​∈S∩H (exists since H hits S). The sets in P are disjoint, so the hS​ are distinct: S↦hS​ is an injection from P into H, so ∣P∣≤∣H∣. Weak duality: every packing is at most every cover, hence max packing ≤ min hitting set.

(2) Each path Pi​ goes from s to t, so removing C breaks it: Pi​ contains an edge ei​∈C. The paths share no edges, so e1​..ek​ are distinct and k≤∣C∣. Same shape as matching ≤ vertex cover: each matching edge needs its own cover vertex (two matching edges share no endpoint). In both cases max flow–min cut (Menger, König) upgrades the inequality to an equality.

Two commodities: why the flow toolkit stops working

LF12 (Lecture 15 case study, coal and steel). Coal goes from s1​ to t1​, steel from s2​ to t2​, 3 trains a day each, on shared tracks: s1​→j1​ 3, s2​→j1​ 1, s2​→j2​ 3, j1​→j2​ 2, j1​→t1​ 3, j2​→t2​ 3, j2​→t1​ 1. Coal and steel together may not exceed a track's capacity. (1) Why not join s1​,s2​ to a super-source and t1​,t2​ to a super-sink? (2) Does "route as much coal as possible, then steel in what is left" work? (3) Can steel simply push coal backwards, as residual edges do?

Solution

Solution. (1) Flow has no identity: a single max flow could send coal-origin units into t2​ and steel-origin units into t1​ (the super-sink only counts totals). Keeping them apart needs conservation per commodity at every vertex: ∑u​fi​(u,v)=∑w​fi​(v,w) for i=1,2 separately, with the shared constraint f1​(e)+f2​(e)≤c(e). That is a multicommodity flow, not a max flow.

In this network the demands can be met: coal on s1​→j1​→t1​ (3) and steel on s2​→j2​→t2​ (3) use disjoint tracks.

(2) Not in general. The first phase is free to choose any max coal flow, and a bad choice can block steel. A small example: tracks s1​→u, u→v, v→t1​, s1​→t1​, s2​→u, v→t2​, all capacity 1, demands 1 each. If phase 1 routes its coal unit along s1​→u→v→t1​, the shared track u→v is full and steel cannot move; routing coal on s1​→t1​ instead lets steel use s2​→u→v→t2​ and meets both demands. Maximising coal greedily also takes both coal routes and leaves steel nothing.

(3) No. A residual back edge cancels flow of the same commodity, and conservation repairs itself because any unit is as good as any other. If steel "cancels" coal on u→v, the cancelled coal unit is stranded at u; coal must simultaneously be rerouted along a completely different s1​–t1​ path (s1​→t1​ above). One local undo becomes a global, coordinated change to another commodity's routing, which augmenting paths cannot express. That is why multicommodity flow is fundamentally harder: it is solved by linear programming, there is no max-flow min-cut equality in general, and integral multicommodity flow is NP-hard.

Textbook exercises the lectures assigned (KT 7)

LF13 (KT 7.4). True or false: if f is a maximum s–t flow (positive integer capacities), then f saturates every edge out of s.

Solution

Solution. False. s→a capacity 10, a→t capacity 1: the max flow is 1 and s→a carries 1 of 10.

LF14 (KT 7.5). True or false: if (A,B) is a minimum s–t cut, it remains a minimum cut after adding 1 to every capacity.

Solution

Solution. False. A cut's capacity grows by its number of forward edges, so a min cut with many edges can be overtaken by one with few. Edges s→u (4), u→a, u→b, u→c (1 each), and a→t, b→t, c→t (5 each). The min cut is ({s,u},rest) with capacity 1+1+1=3; the cut ({s},rest) has 4. After adding 1 everywhere they become 2+2+2=6 and 5, so ({s,u},⋅) is no longer minimum. (Multiplying every capacity by λ>0, by contrast, keeps every min cut minimum: MF2.)
