Free energy and phases

20 min read · 29 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

29 questions with worked answers (tap a solution to open it). Points in brackets. Items tagged with an Atkins code are from your HW list (sometimes with data filled in so they work without the tables). R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; RT at 298 K ≈ 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹.

A. State and explain

Problem 1 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

State the third law of thermodynamics and explain it in terms of S = k ln W.

Solution

Solution. Third law. The entropy of every perfect crystalline substance is zero at T=0:

S(0)=0

Why, from S=klnW. At absolute zero a perfect crystal is in its single lowest-energy arrangement, so the number of microstates is W=1:

S=kln1=0

Residual entropy. If there is any disorder frozen in (defects, random molecular orientations such as CO or ice), then W>1 and S(0)>0.

Use. It sets the zero of the entropy scale, so absolute entropies Sm∘​ can be tabulated.

Problem 2 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why are the Helmholtz and Gibbs energies more convenient than the total entropy for deciding spontaneity?

Solution

Solution. Spontaneity criterion. The second law says ΔStotal​=ΔSsys​+ΔSsurr​≥0, which seems to need information about the surroundings.

How A and G help. At constant T and p the surroundings receive qsurr​=−ΔH reversibly, so

ΔSsurr​=−TΔH​⇒ΔStotal​=−TΔH−TΔS​=−TΔG​

The criterion ΔStotal​≥0 becomes

ΔGT,p​≤0(or ΔAT,V​≤0)

which uses only system properties.

Problem 3 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 3 pts]

What is the physical meaning of ΔA and ΔG in terms of work?

Solution

Solution. Helmholtz energy A=U−TS. At constant T,

−ΔA=wmax​  (maximum work of any kind)

Gibbs energy G=H−TS. At constant T and p,

−ΔG=wnon-exp,max​  (maximum non-expansion work)

for example the electrical work of a fuel cell.

Why less than ΔU or ΔH. The −TΔS term is the heat that must be exchanged with the surroundings for the process to be allowed. If ΔS>0, the system can absorb heat and turn it into extra work.

Problem 4 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

A reaction has ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0. At what temperatures is it spontaneous? Give the general condition.

Solution

Solution. Condition. A reaction is spontaneous at constant T,p when

ΔG=ΔH−TΔS<0

Here ΔH>0 (it costs energy) and ΔS>0 (it increases disorder):

ΔG<0⟺T>ΔSΔH​

The reaction is entropy-driven: spontaneous only at high temperature.

The four cases.

ΔH ΔS spontaneous?
− + always
+ − never
− − low T
+ + high T

Problem 5 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is the chemical potential of a pure substance, and how does it decide which phase is stable?

Solution

Solution. Chemical potential. For a pure substance,

μ=Gm​=(∂n∂G​)T,p​

Stability rule. At given T and p:

  • The phase with the lowest μ is the stable one.
  • Matter flows spontaneously from high μ to low μ.
  • Two phases coexist where μα​=μβ​ (a phase boundary).

Slopes. (∂μ/∂T)p​=−Sm​ and (∂μ/∂p)T​=Vm​. These explain which phase is stable where, and the shape of a phase diagram.

Problem 6 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 3 pts]

Sketch the phase diagram of water and label the areas, lines, triple point and critical point. Why does the solid–liquid line slope backwards?

Solution

Solution. Phase diagram of water (schematic; see the figure in the Learn page).

  • Areas: solid (low T, high p), liquid, vapour (low p, high T).
  • Lines: melting (solid–liquid), boiling (liquid–vapour), sublimation (solid–vapour).
  • Triple point: 273.16 K and 611 Pa, where all three phases coexist.
  • Critical point: 647 K and 218 atm, where the boiling line ends and liquid and vapour become indistinguishable.

Why the melting line slopes backwards. From the Clapeyron equation

dTdp​=TΔfus​VΔfus​H​

Δfus​H>0 always, but for water Δfus​V<0 (ice is less dense than liquid water). So dp/dT<0: raising the pressure lowers the melting point.

Problem 7 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why does solid CO₂ sublime at 1 atm rather than melt?

Solution

Solution. Reason. The triple point of CO2​ is at 5.11 atm (and 216.6 K). The liquid is stable only above that pressure.

At 1 atm, which is below the triple-point pressure, the solid–vapour line is crossed on heating, so the solid goes directly to vapour (sublimes). That is why it is called dry ice.

To melt CO2​ you need a pressure above 5.11 atm.

Contrast. Water's triple point is at 0.006 atm, well below 1 atm, so ice melts at normal pressure.

Problem 8 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why does the chemical potential of a gas fall more steeply with temperature than that of the liquid?

Solution

Solution. Slope of μ against T. From dμ=−Sm​dT+Vm​dp:

(∂T∂μ​)p​=−Sm​

Entropies. Sm​(gas)≫Sm​(liquid)>Sm​(solid), so the gas's μ(T) falls most steeply.

Consequence. The curves cross: at low T the liquid has the lowest μ, but because the gas line falls faster, it crosses below the liquid line at the boiling point. Above it the gas is the stable phase.

Also this explains why every transition goes in the direction of increasing entropy as T rises: solid → liquid → gas.

B. Show or derive

Problem 9 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Starting from the Clausius inequality, show that at constant temperature and pressure a spontaneous change has dG ≤ 0.

Solution

Solution. Clausius inequality. For any process dS≥dq/T.

Constant pressure, expansion work only: dq=dH, so

dS≥TdH​ ⇒ dH−TdS≤0

Constant temperature: dG=dH−TdS−SdT=dH−TdS, so

dGT,p​≤0​

Spontaneous processes lower G; dG=0 at equilibrium.

Common mistake. The condition needs constant T and p; at constant T,V the quantity to use is A.

Problem 10 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

From dU = TdS − pdV and the definitions of H, A and G, write dH, dA and dG. Derive the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂p/∂T)_V.

Solution

Solution. Differentials. Start from dU=TdS−pdV.

H=U+pV:dH=TdS+Vdp
A=U−TS:dA=−SdT−pdV
G=H−TS:dG=−SdT+Vdp

Maxwell relation. From dA: (∂T∂A​)V​=−S and (∂V∂A​)T​=−p. A is a state function, so mixed second derivatives are equal:

−(∂V∂S​)T​=−(∂T∂p​)V​ ⇒ (∂V∂S​)T​=(∂T∂p​)V​​

Problem 11 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that π_T = T(∂p/∂T)_V − p, and evaluate it for a perfect gas and for a van der Waals gas.

Solution

Solution. Divide dU=TdS−pdV by dV at constant T:

πT​=(∂V∂U​)T​=T(∂V∂S​)T​−p

Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​:

πT​=T(∂T∂p​)V​−p​

Perfect gas: p=nRT/V, so T(nR/V)−p=0: the energy does not depend on volume.

van der Waals gas: p=Vm​−bRT​−Vm2​a​, so (∂p/∂T)V​=R/(Vm​−b):

πT​=Vm​−bRT​−p=Vm2​a​​

The attraction a gives the internal energy a volume dependence.

Problem 12 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts · P3E.4]

Use (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂p/∂T)_V to find (∂S/∂V)_T for a perfect gas, a van der Waals gas p = RT/(V_m − b) − a/V_m², and a Dieterici gas p = RTe^(−a/RTV_m)/(V_m − b). For an isothermal expansion, compare ΔS for the perfect and van der Waals gases.

Solution

Solution. Strategy. (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​; differentiate p at fixed V.

  • Perfect gas p=RT/Vm​: (∂p/∂T)V​=R/Vm​.
  • van der Waals p=Vm​−bRT​−Vm2​a​: (∂p/∂T)V​=Vm​−bR​.
  • Dieterici p=Vm​−bRTe−a/RTVm​​:
(∂T∂p​)V​=Vm​−bR​e−a/RTVm​(1+RTVm​a​)

Isothermal expansion Vi​→Vf​ (ΔS=∫(∂S/∂V)T​dV):

ΔSperfect​=RlnVi​Vf​​,ΔSvdW​=RlnVi​−bVf​−b​

Since Vi​−bVf​−b​>Vi​Vf​​, the van der Waals gas gains more entropy: the molecules have less free volume at the start, so the same expansion releases more.

Problem 13 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Derive the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation (∂(G/T)/∂T)_p = −H/T².

Solution

Solution. Differentiate the quotient:

(∂T∂(G/T)​)p​=T1​(∂T∂G​)p​−T2G​

Use (∂G/∂T)p​=−S (from dG=−SdT+Vdp):

=−TS​−T2G​=−T2TS+G​

Use G=H−TS, so G+TS=H:

(∂T∂(G/T)​)p​=−T2H​​

Use. Applied to ΔG it gives (∂(ΔG/T)/∂T)p​=−ΔH/T2, which tells how an equilibrium constant changes with temperature.

Problem 14 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Derive the Clapeyron equation, and from it the Clausius–Clapeyron equation. State the approximations.

Solution

Solution. Phase equilibrium. Along the boundary between phases α and β, μα​=μβ​, and this holds as we move along the line, so dμα​=dμβ​. With dμ=−Sm​dT+Vm​dp:

−Sα​dT+Vα​dp=−Sβ​dT+Vβ​dp
dTdp​=Δtrs​VΔtrs​S​=TΔtrs​VΔtrs​H​​(Clapeyron)

Clausius–Clapeyron (liquid to vapour). Approximations:

  1. Vliquid​≪Vvapour​, so ΔV≈Vvapour​.
  2. The vapour is a perfect gas: Vm​=RT/p.
dTdp​=RT2Δvap​Hp​ ⇒ dTdlnp​=RT2Δvap​H​​
  1. For integration, Δvap​H is taken constant over the range:
lnp1​p2​​=−RΔvap​H​(T2​1​−T1​1​)

Problem 15 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 2 pts]

Using the phase rule, show that four phases of a pure substance cannot coexist.

Solution

Solution. Phase rule. F=C−P+2.

For a pure substance C=1 and P=4:

F=1−4+2=−1<0

A negative number of degrees of freedom is impossible: the system would be over-determined. Three phases (F=0, the triple point) is the most that can coexist.

Check. Three equal-μ conditions are enough to fix T and p; a fourth would be an extra equation with nothing left to adjust.

C. Numericals

Problem 16 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts · Ex 3C.1]

The molar heat capacity of a solid at 10 K is 0.43 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find its molar entropy at that temperature.

Solution

Solution. Idea. At very low temperature Cp​=aT3 (Debye), and the third law sets S(0)=0.

S(T)=∫0T​T′Cp​​dT′=∫0T​aT′2dT′=3aT3​=3Cp​(T)​
S(10 K)=30.43​=0.14 J K−1mol−1​

Problem 17 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

Calculate Δ_rS° at 298 K for ½N₂(g) + 3/2 H₂(g) → NH₃(g). S°_m: NH₃ 192.45, N₂ 191.61, H₂ 130.68 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (E3C.3(a))

Solution

Solution. Idea. Δr​S∘=∑νSm∘​(products)−∑νSm∘​(reactants), with ν the stoichiometric coefficients.

Δr​S∘=192.45−21​(191.61)−23​(130.68)=192.45−95.81−196.02=−99.4 J K−1mol−1​

Check. Negative, as expected: two moles of gas (21​ N2​ + 23​ H2​) become one mole of gas, so the disorder falls.

Problem 18 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

Find Δ_rG° at 298 K for 4HI(g) + O₂(g) → 2I₂(s) + 2H₂O(l). Δ_fG°: HI(g) +1.70, H₂O(l) −237.13 kJ mol⁻¹. (E3D.2(a))

Solution

Solution. Idea. Δr​G∘=∑νΔf​G∘(products)−∑νΔf​G∘(reactants). Elements in their reference states (I2​(s), O2​(g)) have Δf​G∘=0.

Δr​G∘=2(−237.13)−4(+1.70)=−474.26−6.80=−481.1 kJ mol−1​

Check. Δr​G∘<0, so the reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions.

Problem 19 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · like Ex 3D.2]

For the combustion of glucose at 298 K, Δ_cH° = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹ and Δ_cS° = +182 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. How much non-expansion work can 1 mol supply at most? Why is it more than |ΔH|?

Solution

Solution. Idea. The maximum non-expansion work is ∣ΔG∣, and ΔG=ΔH−TΔS.

ΔG=−2808 kJ−(298 K)(0.182 kJ K−1)=−2808−54=−2862 kJ
wnon-exp,max​=2862 kJ per mole​

Why more than ∣ΔH∣? ΔS>0, so the system can absorb heat TΔS from the surroundings without making the total entropy fall. That extra energy is available to be turned into work.

Problem 20 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ has ΔH° = +178 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Above what temperature is it spontaneous?

Solution

Solution. Idea. A reaction is spontaneous when ΔG=ΔH−TΔS<0. Here ΔH>0 and ΔS>0, so entropy wins only at high T.

ΔG<0⟺T>ΔSΔH​=161 J K−1mol−1178000 J mol−1​=1100 K (≈830 ∘C)​

Problem 21 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · like Ex 3E.3]

Find the change in molar Gibbs energy at 298 K when (a) a perfect gas and (b) liquid water (V_m = 18 cm³ mol⁻¹) are compressed isothermally from 1 bar to 2 bar. Comment.

Solution

Solution. Idea. At constant T, dGm​=Vm​dp.

(a) Perfect gas: Vm​=RT/p, so

ΔGm​=RTlnp1​p2​​=(2.48 kJ mol−1)ln2=1.72 kJ mol−1​

(b) Liquid water: Vm​ is essentially constant, so

ΔGm​=Vm​Δp=(18×10−6 m3)(105 Pa)=1.8 J mol−1​

Comment. The gas is about 1000 times more sensitive to pressure, because its molar volume (about 25 L) is about 1000 times larger than that of the liquid (18 mL).

Problem 22 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

The chemical potential of a substance differs by −8.3 kJ mol⁻¹ between two regions. By how much does G change when 0.15 mmol is transferred? (E4A.2(b))

Solution

Solution. Idea. μ=(∂G/∂n)T,p​, so moving n moles across a difference Δμ changes G by nΔμ.

ΔG=nΔμ=(0.15×10−3 mol)(−8.3×103 J mol−1)=−1.2 J​

Problem 23 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

What is the maximum number of phases in equilibrium in a four-component system? In a one-component system, is P = 1 an area, a line or a point? (E4A.3(b), E4A.4(a))

Solution

Solution. Idea. The phase rule is F=C−P+2.

Maximum phases: the most phases coexist when F=0, so Pmax​=C+2=4+2=6​.

One component, P=1: F=1−1+2=2. Both T and p can be varied independently, so one phase occupies an area of the phase diagram.

Problem 24 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

The vapour pressure of a liquid fits ln(p/Torr) = 18.361 − (3036.8 K)/T. Find Δ_vapH. (E4B.7(b))

Solution

Solution. Idea. Integrating Clausius–Clapeyron gives lnp=const−RΔvap​H​T1​, so the slope of lnp against 1/T is −Δvap​H/R.

Here the slope is −3036.8 K, so

Δvap​H=(3036.8 K)(8.314 J K−1mol−1)=25.2 kJ mol−1​

Problem 25 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts]

The vapour pressure of a liquid fits log₁₀(p/Torr) = 8.750 − (1625 K)/T. Find Δ_vapH and the normal boiling point (log₁₀ 760 = 2.881). (E4B.8(b))

Solution

Δvap​H=2.303R(1625 K)=2.303×8.314×1625=31.1 kJ mol−1​

Normal boiling point (p=760 Torr, log10​760=2.881):

Tb​1625​=8.750−2.881=5.869 ⇒ Tb​=277 K​

Problem 26 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · P4B.5]

The entropies of fusion and vaporisation of water are 22.0 and 109.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find the difference in slope of μ against T on either side of (a) the freezing point and (b) the boiling point. (c) By how much does μ of water supercooled to −5 °C exceed that of ice?

Solution

Solution. Idea. (∂μ/∂T)p​=−Sm​, so on crossing a transition the slope of μ against T jumps by −Δtrs​S.

(a) At the freezing point (liquid minus solid): −Δfus​S=−22.0 J K−1mol−1​.

(b) At the boiling point (vapour minus liquid): −Δvap​S=−109.9 J K−1mol−1​.

(c) At the freezing point μliq​=μice​. Cooling by ΔT=5 K, the liquid's μ rises relative to ice's by the slope difference times ΔT:

μliq​−μice​≈Δfus​SΔT=22.0×5=110 J mol−1​

Supercooled water has the higher μ, so it tends to freeze.

Problem 27 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts]

Estimate the melting point of ice at 50 bar. Densities: ice 0.92, water 1.00 g cm⁻³; Δ_fusH = 6.01 kJ mol⁻¹. (E4B.14(a))

Solution

Solution. Idea. Clapeyron for melting: dTdp​=TΔfus​VΔfus​H​, so for a small pressure change ΔT≈Δfus​HTΔfus​VΔp​.

Volume change (18.0 g mol−1): Vm​(ice)=18.0/0.92=19.6 cm3, Vm​(water)=18.0 cm3, so Δfus​V=−1.6×10−6 m3 mol−1.

ΔT≈6010(273)(−1.6×10−6)(49×105)​=−0.35 K

Ice melts at about −0.35 ∘C​. The melting point falls because water is denser than ice.

Use Trouton's rule to estimate Δ_vapH of benzene (T_b = 353 K).
