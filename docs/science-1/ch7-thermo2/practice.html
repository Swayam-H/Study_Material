Free energy and phases

12 min read · 29 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

29 questions with worked answers (tap a solution to open it). Points in brackets. Items tagged with an Atkins code are from your HW list (sometimes with data filled in so they work without the tables). R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; RT at 298 K ≈ 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹.

A. State and explain

Problem 1 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

State the third law of thermodynamics and explain it in terms of S = k ln W.

Solution

Solution. The entropy of every perfect crystalline substance is zero at T = 0. A perfect crystal at absolute zero has exactly one arrangement, W = 1, so S = k ln 1 = 0. Any disorder (defects, random orientations) leaves W > 1 and a residual entropy.

Problem 2 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why are the Helmholtz and Gibbs energies more convenient than the total entropy for deciding spontaneity?

Solution

Solution. Spontaneity requires ΔS_total ≥ 0, which needs the entropy of the surroundings too. At constant T and p (or T and V) the surroundings' entropy change is −ΔH/T (or −ΔU/T), and it is built into G (or A), so the criterion uses only system properties.

Problem 3 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 3 pts]

What is the physical meaning of ΔA and ΔG in terms of work?

Solution

Solution. −ΔA is the maximum work (of any kind) a process at constant T can do. −ΔG is the maximum non-expansion work at constant T and p, such as the electrical work of a cell.

Problem 4 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

A reaction has ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0. At what temperatures is it spontaneous? Give the general condition.

Solution

Solution. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS < 0 when T > ΔH/ΔS. Entropy drives it at high temperature.

Problem 5 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is the chemical potential of a pure substance, and how does it decide which phase is stable?

Solution

Solution. μ = G_m. At given T and p the phase with the lowest μ is stable; matter flows from high to low μ; phases coexist where their μ are equal.

Problem 6 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 3 pts]

Sketch the phase diagram of water and label the areas, lines, triple point and critical point. Why does the solid–liquid line slope backwards?

Solution

Solution. Areas solid, liquid, vapour; boundaries for melting, boiling and sublimation; triple point 273.16 K, 611 Pa; liquid–vapour line ends at the critical point (647 K, 218 atm). The solid–liquid line has negative slope because Δ_fusV < 0 (ice is less dense), so in dp/dT = ΔH/(TΔV) the slope is negative: pressure lowers the melting point.

Problem 7 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why does solid CO₂ sublime at 1 atm rather than melt?

Solution

Solution. Its triple point is at 5.11 atm. Below that pressure the liquid is never the stable phase, so at 1 atm the solid goes straight to vapour.

Problem 8 [Thermo 5 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why does the chemical potential of a gas fall more steeply with temperature than that of the liquid?

Solution

Solution. (∂μ/∂T)_p = −S_m, and S_m(gas) > S_m(liquid), so the gas's μ falls faster. That is why the gas becomes the stable phase at high temperature.

B. Show or derive

Problem 9 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Starting from the Clausius inequality, show that at constant temperature and pressure a spontaneous change has dG ≤ 0.

Solution

Solution. Clausius: dS ≥ dq/T. At constant p with only expansion work, dq = dH, so dS ≥ dH/T, i.e. dH − TdS ≤ 0. At constant T, dG = dH − TdS − SdT = dH − TdS, so dG ≤ 0. Equality holds at equilibrium.

Problem 10 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

From dU = TdS − pdV and the definitions of H, A and G, write dH, dA and dG. Derive the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂p/∂T)_V.

Solution

Solution. dH = dU + pdV + Vdp = TdS + Vdp. dA = dU − TdS − SdT = −SdT − pdV. dG = dH − TdS − SdT = −SdT + Vdp. From dA: (∂A/∂T)_V = −S and (∂A/∂V)_T = −p. Equal mixed derivatives give (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂p/∂T)_V.

Problem 11 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that π_T = T(∂p/∂T)_V − p, and evaluate it for a perfect gas and for a van der Waals gas.

Solution

Solution. Divide dU = TdS − pdV by dV at constant T: π_T = T(∂S/∂V)_T − p = T(∂p/∂T)_V − p (Maxwell). Perfect gas: T(nR/V) − p = 0. Van der Waals: T·R/(V_m − b) − p = a/V_m².

Problem 12 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts · P3E.4]

Use (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂p/∂T)_V to find (∂S/∂V)_T for a perfect gas, a van der Waals gas p = RT/(V_m − b) − a/V_m², and a Dieterici gas p = RTe^(−a/RTV_m)/(V_m − b). For an isothermal expansion, compare ΔS for the perfect and van der Waals gases.

Solution

Solution. Perfect: R/V_m, so ΔS = R ln(V_f/V_i). Van der Waals: R/(V_m − b), so ΔS = R ln((V_f − b)/(V_i − b)), larger than the perfect-gas value because the molecules have less free volume to start with. Dieterici: (∂p/∂T)_V = [R/(V_m − b)] e^(−a/RTV_m)(1 + a/RTV_m).

Problem 13 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Derive the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation (∂(G/T)/∂T)_p = −H/T².

Solution

Solution. (∂(G/T)/∂T)_p = (1/T)(∂G/∂T)_p − G/T² = −S/T − G/T² = −(TS + G)/T² = −H/T², using G = H − TS.

Problem 14 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Derive the Clapeyron equation, and from it the Clausius–Clapeyron equation. State the approximations.

Solution

Solution. Along the boundary μα = μβ, so dμα = dμβ: −SαdT + Vαdp = −SβdT + Vβdp. So dp/dT = Δ_trsS/Δ_trsV = Δ_trsH/(TΔ_trsV). For vaporisation, neglect V_liquid and treat the vapour as perfect: ΔV ≈ RT/p. Then dp/dT = pΔ_vapH/RT², i.e. d ln p/dT = Δ_vapH/RT². Also assume Δ_vapH is constant over the range when integrating.

Problem 15 [Thermo 5 · Derivation · 2 pts]

Using the phase rule, show that four phases of a pure substance cannot coexist.

Solution

Solution. F = C − P + 2 = 1 − 4 + 2 = −1 < 0, which is impossible, so four phases cannot coexist.

C. Numericals

Problem 16 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts · Ex 3C.1]

The molar heat capacity of a solid at 10 K is 0.43 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find its molar entropy at that temperature.

Solution

Solution. S = C_p/3 = 0.14 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Problem 17 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

Calculate Δ_rS° at 298 K for ½N₂(g) + 3/2 H₂(g) → NH₃(g). S°_m: NH₃ 192.45, N₂ 191.61, H₂ 130.68 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (E3C.3(a))

Solution

Solution. 192.45 − ½(191.61) − 3/2(130.68) = 192.45 − 95.81 − 196.02 = −99.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Negative, since 2 mol of gas become 1.

Problem 18 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

Find Δ_rG° at 298 K for 4HI(g) + O₂(g) → 2I₂(s) + 2H₂O(l). Δ_fG°: HI(g) +1.70, H₂O(l) −237.13 kJ mol⁻¹. (E3D.2(a))

Solution

Solution. 2(−237.13) − 4(1.70) = −474.26 − 6.80 = −481.1 kJ mol⁻¹. Spontaneous.

Problem 19 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · like Ex 3D.2]

For the combustion of glucose at 298 K, Δ_cH° = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹ and Δ_cS° = +182 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. How much non-expansion work can 1 mol supply at most? Why is it more than |ΔH|?

Solution

Solution. ΔG = −2808 − 298 × 0.182 = −2808 − 54 = −2862 kJ, so up to 2862 kJ of non-expansion work. Since ΔS > 0, some heat can flow in from the surroundings and still keep the total entropy from falling, adding to the work available.

Problem 20 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ has ΔH° = +178 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Above what temperature is it spontaneous?

Solution

Solution. T > 178000/161 ≈ 1100 K (about 830 °C).

Problem 21 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · like Ex 3E.3]

Find the change in molar Gibbs energy at 298 K when (a) a perfect gas and (b) liquid water (V_m = 18 cm³ mol⁻¹) are compressed isothermally from 1 bar to 2 bar. Comment.

Solution

Solution. (a) RT ln 2 = 2.48 × 0.693 ≈ 1.72 kJ mol⁻¹. (b) V_mΔp = 18 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁵ = 1.8 J mol⁻¹. A gas's Gibbs energy is about a thousand times more sensitive to pressure, because its molar volume is so much larger.

Problem 22 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

The chemical potential of a substance differs by −8.3 kJ mol⁻¹ between two regions. By how much does G change when 0.15 mmol is transferred? (E4A.2(b))

Solution

Solution. ΔG = nΔμ = 0.15 × 10⁻³ × (−8.3 × 10³) ≈ −1.2 J.

Problem 23 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

What is the maximum number of phases in equilibrium in a four-component system? In a one-component system, is P = 1 an area, a line or a point? (E4A.3(b), E4A.4(a))

Solution

Solution. F = 0 gives P_max = C + 2 = 6. For C = 1, P = 1 gives F = 2: an area, in which T and p can both be varied.

Problem 24 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

The vapour pressure of a liquid fits ln(p/Torr) = 18.361 − (3036.8 K)/T. Find Δ_vapH. (E4B.7(b))

Solution

Solution. Slope of ln p vs 1/T is −Δ_vapH/R, so Δ_vapH = 3036.8 × 8.314 ≈ 25.2 kJ mol⁻¹.

Problem 25 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts]

The vapour pressure of a liquid fits log₁₀(p/Torr) = 8.750 − (1625 K)/T. Find Δ_vapH and the normal boiling point (log₁₀ 760 = 2.881). (E4B.8(b))

Solution

Solution. Δ_vapH = 2.303 × 8.314 × 1625 ≈ 31.1 kJ mol⁻¹. At p = 760 Torr: 1625/T = 8.750 − 2.881 = 5.869, so T_b ≈ 277 K.

Problem 26 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts · P4B.5]

The entropies of fusion and vaporisation of water are 22.0 and 109.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find the difference in slope of μ against T on either side of (a) the freezing point and (b) the boiling point. (c) By how much does μ of water supercooled to −5 °C exceed that of ice?

Solution

Solution. The slope is −S_m, so the difference (liquid minus solid, or vapour minus liquid) is −Δ_trsS. (a) −22.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (b) −109.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (c) Δμ ≈ Δ_fusS × ΔT = 22.0 × 5 = 110 J mol⁻¹ above ice, so supercooled water tends to freeze.

Problem 27 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts]

Estimate the melting point of ice at 50 bar. Densities: ice 0.92, water 1.00 g cm⁻³; Δ_fusH = 6.01 kJ mol⁻¹. (E4B.14(a))

Solution

Solution. V_m(ice) = 18.0/0.92 ≈ 19.6 cm³, V_m(water) = 18.0 cm³, so Δ_fusV ≈ −1.6 cm³ mol⁻¹. ΔT ≈ TΔVΔp/ΔH = 273 × (−1.6 × 10⁻⁶) × (49 × 10⁵)/6010 ≈ −0.35 K. It melts at about −0.35 °C.

D. Estimate

Problem 28 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 2 pts]

Use Trouton's rule to estimate Δ_vapH of benzene (T_b = 353 K).

Solution

Solution. Δ_vapH ≈ 85 × 353 ≈ 30 kJ mol⁻¹ (measured: 30.8).

Problem 29 [Thermo 5 · Numerical · 3 pts]

At the top of a high mountain the pressure is about 1/3 atm. Estimate the boiling point of water (Δ_vapH = 40.7 kJ mol⁻¹, ln 3 ≈ 1.1).

Solution

Solution. 1/T = 1/373 + (R/Δ_vapH) ln 3 = 0.00268 + (8.314/40700)(1.1) ≈ 0.00268 + 0.000225 = 0.00291, so T ≈ 344 K, about 70 °C.
