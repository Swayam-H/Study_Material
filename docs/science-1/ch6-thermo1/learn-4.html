How to use these notes

These notes teach Atkins 11e Topics 2C (thermochemistry) and 2D (state functions, internal pressure and the Joule–Thomson effect) from scratch, in about 60 minutes of reading. Both topics are in the Quiz-2 range (Thermo-1 to Thermo-4).

Sign convention (same as the slides and Atkins). w is the work done on the system, and the first law is

ΔU=q+w,dU=dq+dw,dw=−pex​dV

So an expanding gas has w < 0, and heat flowing in has q > 0. Enthalpy is H = U + pV, and at constant pressure ΔH = q_p.

Part A. Topic 2C: Thermochemistry

There are no lecture slides for 2C. The 10-Oct lecture log lists it as reading, and the homework sets seven items on it, so everything in Part A is taught from Atkins 11e p.51–58 in my own words.

The one idea behind the whole topic. Enthalpy is a state function. So ΔH for going from reactants to products does not care which route you take. Every result in 2C (Hess's law, formation enthalpies, Kirchhoff's law) is this one fact used in a different way.

Why chemists care. At constant pressure, the heat a reaction gives out is −ΔH (Topic 2B: ΔH = q_p). So tabulated enthalpies tell you the heat from burning a fuel or digesting food without doing the experiment.

Words you need.

  • Thermochemistry: the study of the heat released or absorbed by reactions and physical changes.
  • Exothermic: releases heat to the surroundings. At constant pressure, ΔH < 0 (Atkins also says exenthalpic).
  • Endothermic: absorbs heat. At constant pressure, ΔH > 0 (endenthalpic).
  • Calorimetry: measuring that heat. At constant volume the heat equals ΔU (Topic 2A); at constant pressure it equals ΔH (Topic 2B).

2C.1 Standard enthalpy changes

Intuition

The enthalpy change of a process depends on the conditions: the pressure, the temperature, and the phase of each substance. A table of ΔH values is only useful if everyone measures under the same agreed conditions. Those agreed conditions are the standard state.

Standard state

The standard state of a substance at a stated temperature is its pure form at exactly 1 bar.

  • Standard quantities carry a superscript °: ΔH°, H°_m.
  • Liquid ethanol at 298 K: its standard state is pure liquid ethanol at 1 bar and 298 K. Solid iron at 500 K: pure iron at 1 bar and 500 K.
  • Temperature is not part of the definition. A standard state exists at every temperature. Tables use 298.15 K by convention, so a ΔH° with no temperature attached means 298.15 K.
  • Solutions need a more careful definition (Topic 5E, not in this quiz).

A standard enthalpy change ΔH° is the enthalpy of the products in their standard states minus that of the reactants in their standard states, all at the same temperature.

(a) Enthalpies of physical change

The enthalpy change per mole for a change of phase is the standard enthalpy of transition, Δ_trsH°. Examples for water:

H2​O(l)→H2​O(g)Δvap​H∘(373 K)=+40.66 kJ mol−1
H2​O(s)→H2​O(l)Δfus​H∘(273 K)=+6.01 kJ mol−1

At 298 K, Δ_vapH°(H₂O) = +44.0 kJ mol⁻¹, larger than at 373 K. Section 2C.3 explains why.

Notation. The process goes on the Δ as a subscript (Δ_vapH), not on the H. You will still see the older H_vap in many books.

The common transitions and their symbols (all molar quantities):

Process What happens Symbol
Transition phase α → phase β Δ_trsH
Fusion s → l Δ_fusH
Vaporization l → g Δ_vapH
Sublimation s → g Δ_subH
Mixing pure components → mixture Δ_mixH
Solution solute → solution Δ_solH
Hydration X±(g) → X±(aq) Δ_hydH
Atomization species → atoms(g) Δ_atH
Ionization X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻(g) Δ_ionH
Electron gain X(g) + e⁻(g) → X⁻(g) Δ_egH

Activation reactants → activated complex Δ^‡H

Derivation: two consequences of H being a state function

1. Sublimation = fusion + vaporization. Turn ice into vapour in two ways at the same temperature T:

  • directly: s → g, enthalpy change Δ_subH°;
  • in two steps: s → l, then l → g, enthalpy change Δ_fusH° + Δ_vapH°.

Both routes start in the same state and end in the same state. H is a state function, so ΔH is the same on both:

Δsub​H∘=Δfus​H∘+Δvap​H∘(all at the same T)

Every Δ_fusH° is positive (melting always absorbs heat), so Δ_subH° > Δ_vapH° at a given temperature.

2. Reversing a process flips the sign. Go A → B and then B → A. You are back where you started, so the total ΔH is zero:

ΔH∘(A→B)=−ΔH∘(B→A)

So the enthalpy of condensation of water at 298 K is −44 kJ mol⁻¹.

Worked example. Estimate Δ_subH° of ice at 0 °C. Use Δ_fusH° = 6.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and Δ_vapH° ≈ 45 kJ mol⁻¹ at 0 °C (a little above the 44 kJ mol⁻¹ at 25 °C). Then Δ_subH° ≈ 6.0 + 45 = 51 kJ mol⁻¹. The two steps must be at the same temperature; mixing 6.01 kJ (at 273 K) with 40.66 kJ (at 373 K) would be wrong.

(b) Enthalpies of chemical change

A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation plus its standard enthalpy change:

CH4​(g)+2O2​(g)→CO2​(g)+2H2​O(l)ΔH∘=−890 kJ

It means: pure, separate reactants in their standard states turn into pure, separate products in their standard states. The small enthalpy of mixing and separating is ignored (except for ions in solution).

The standard reaction enthalpy Δ_rH° is defined from the standard molar enthalpies H°_m(J) of each species J:

Δr​H∘=products∑​νHm∘​−reactants∑​νHm∘​

Here ν are the stoichiometric coefficients (positive numbers). Its unit is kJ mol⁻¹, where "per mole" means per mole of reaction as written. For 2A + B → 3C + D, it is per 2 mol A, or per 1 mol B, or per 3 mol C. This formula cannot be used directly, because absolute enthalpies H°_m are unknown. Section 2C.2 fixes that.

The standard enthalpy of combustion Δ_cH° is Δ_rH° for complete oxidation of an organic compound to CO₂(g) and H₂O(l), plus N₂(g) if it contains nitrogen. For glucose, Δ_cH° = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹: burning 1 mol releases 2808 kJ of heat at constant pressure.

Worked example (per mole of reaction). For 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l), Δ_rH° = −572 kJ mol⁻¹. Written as H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l), it is −286 kJ mol⁻¹. Scaling the equation scales Δ_rH°. Burning 4.0 g of H₂ (2.0 mol) at constant pressure releases 2 × 286 = 572 kJ.

(c) Hess's law

The standard enthalpy of an overall reaction is the sum of the standard enthalpies of the reactions it can be split into.

  • Why it is true: H is a state function. Splitting a reaction into steps just picks a different route between the same start and end.
  • The steps need not happen in real life. They only have to balance.
  • Rules: reversing an equation flips the sign of its ΔH; multiplying an equation by a number multiplies its ΔH by that number.
  • Use: you can get Δ_rH° for a reaction that is hard to measure (too slow, or with side reactions) from reactions that are easy to measure, such as combustions.

Recipe. (1) Write the target equation. (2) For each species that appears in only one of the given equations, take that equation, reverse it if the species is on the wrong side, and scale it to the coefficient you need. (3) Add the equations and check that everything else cancels. (4) Add the ΔH values in the same way.

Worked example: Atkins Example 2C.1 (Hess)

Data. Hydrogenation of propene, C₃H₆(g) + H₂(g) → C₃H₈(g): −124 kJ mol⁻¹. Combustion of propane, C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l): −2220 kJ mol⁻¹. Formation of water, H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l): −286 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Δ_cH° of propene.

Target. C₃H₆(g) + (9/2)O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).

Assemble. C₃H₆ appears only in the hydrogenation, so keep it as written. The propane it makes has to be burned, so add the combustion. That leaves one H₂ too many on the left and one H₂O too many on the right, so add water formation reversed:

Step Equation Δ_rH° / kJ mol⁻¹
1 C₃H₆ + H₂ → C₃H₈ −124
2 C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O(l) −2220
3 H₂O(l) → H₂ + ½O₂ +286
Sum C₃H₆ + (9/2)O₂ → 3CO₂ + 3H₂O(l) −2058

Check the cancellation. C₃H₈ is made in step 1 and used in step 2. H₂ is used in step 1 and made in step 3. O₂: 5 used, ½ made back, net 9/2. H₂O: 4 made, 1 used, net 3.

Exam-style example: hydrogenation of benzene (Atkins Self-test 2C.1)

Q. Δ_cH° of liquid benzene is −3268 kJ mol⁻¹ and of liquid cyclohexane is −3920 kJ mol⁻¹. Take Δ_cH°(H₂) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Δ_rH° for C₆H₆(l) + 3H₂(g) → C₆H₁₂(l).

A. Burn the reactants, then "un-burn" the product:

Δr​H∘=Δc​H∘(C6​H6​)+3Δc​H∘(H2​)−Δc​H∘(C6​H12​)
=−3268+3(−286)−(−3920)=−3268−858+3920=−206 kJ mol−1

General rule from this example: from combustion data, Δ_rH° = Σ ν Δ_cH°(reactants) − Σ ν Δ_cH°(products). This is the opposite order to the formation-enthalpy formula in 2C.2. Burning the reactants takes you down to CO₂ and H₂O; un-burning the products brings you back up.

[beyond the quiz] Hydrogenating one C=C bond in cyclohexene releases about 120 kJ mol⁻¹. Three isolated double bonds would give about −360 kJ mol⁻¹, but benzene gives only −206. The missing ~150 kJ mol⁻¹ is the extra stability of benzene's delocalized ring.

2C.2 Standard enthalpies of formation

Intuition

We can never know the absolute enthalpy of a substance, just as there is no absolute height. Mountains are measured from sea level instead. Thermochemistry picks its own "sea level": the elements in their most stable forms have enthalpy zero. Every compound then gets an enthalpy relative to its elements, and one table of these numbers gives Δ_rH° for any reaction.

Definitions

The standard enthalpy of formation Δ_fH° of a substance is the standard reaction enthalpy for making 1 mol of it from its elements in their reference states.

The reference state of an element is its most stable state at the stated temperature and 1 bar.

  • At 298 K: nitrogen is N₂(g), mercury is Hg(l), carbon is graphite, tin is white (metallic) tin.
  • Exception: for phosphorus, white phosphorus is used because it is the most reproducible form, even though it is not the most stable.
  • Example: Δ_fH°(C₆H₆, l) refers to 6C(s, graphite) + 3H₂(g) → C₆H₆(l), and equals +49.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • An element in its reference state has Δ_fH° = 0 at every temperature, because its formation reaction is a "null" reaction such as N₂(g) → N₂(g).

Standard state versus reference state (a favourite short question). Every substance, in any phase, has a standard state: pure, at 1 bar. A reference state applies only to elements and picks out one phase or allotrope, the most stable one. Diamond has a standard state, but it is not carbon's reference state, so Δ_fH°(C, diamond) = +1.9 kJ mol⁻¹, not zero.

Ions in solution. You cannot make a solution of cations alone. So by convention the hydrogen ion is given zero at every temperature:

Δf​H∘(H+,aq)=0

Then if Δ_fH°(HBr, aq) = −122 kJ mol⁻¹, all of it is assigned to the anion: Δ_fH°(Br⁻, aq) = −122 kJ mol⁻¹. Other ions follow step by step.

Derivation: Δ_rH° from formation enthalpies

Picture the reaction as a two-step route through the elements:

  1. "Unform" the reactants into their elements. Unforming is formation reversed, so this step contributes −Σ ν Δ_fH°(reactants).
  2. Form the products from those same elements. This contributes +Σ ν Δ_fH°(products).

The atoms are conserved, so the elements at the halfway point are exactly the ones needed. H is a state function, so the direct route has the same ΔH as this detour:

Δr​H∘=products∑​νΔf​H∘−reactants∑​νΔf​H∘

A compact form uses stoichiometric numbers ν_J, which carry a sign: positive for products, negative for reactants.

Δr​H∘=J∑​νJ​Δf​H∘(J)

For CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O: ν(CH₄) = −1, ν(O₂) = −2, ν(CO₂) = +1, ν(H₂O) = +2. Don't mix up ν (always positive) with ν_J (signed).

Worked example: combustion of methane

Data at 298 K, in kJ mol⁻¹: Δ_fH°(CH₄, g) = −74.8; Δ_fH°(CO₂, g) = −393.5; Δ_fH°(H₂O, l) = −285.8; O₂(g) is an element in its reference state, so 0.

Δc​H∘=[−393.5+2(−285.8)]−[−74.8+2(0)]=−965.1+74.8=−890.3 kJ mol−1

This matches the −890 kJ mol⁻¹ in the thermochemical equation of 2C.1.

Exam-style example: running it backwards

Q. Δ_cH° of liquid benzene is −3268 kJ mol⁻¹. Find Δ_fH°(C₆H₆, l), using the CO₂ and H₂O(l) values above.

A. The combustion is C₆H₆(l) + (15/2)O₂ → 6CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l). Apply the formula, with the benzene value x as the only unknown:

−3268=[6(−393.5)+3(−285.8)]−x=−3218.4−x
x=Δf​H∘(C6​H6​,l)=−3218.4+3268≈+50 kJ mol−1

The tabulated value is +49.0; the small gap comes from rounding the data. A positive Δ_fH° means benzene sits above its elements on the enthalpy scale.

Exam-style example: a consistency check

Q. Δ_fH°(H₂O, l) = −285.83 and Δ_fH°(H₂O, g) = −241.82 kJ mol⁻¹. What is Δ_vapH° of water at 298 K?

A. H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) is a "reaction" like any other: Δ_vapH° = (−241.82) − (−285.83) = +44.01 kJ mol⁻¹, the value quoted in 2C.1. Watch the phase of water in every problem: using H₂O(g) instead of H₂O(l) changes Δ_rH° by 44 kJ mol⁻¹ per mole of water.

2C.3 Temperature dependence of reaction enthalpies (Kirchhoff's law)

Intuition

Tables give Δ_rH° at 298 K, but reactions often run hot. Heat the reactants and the products separately. Each one's enthalpy rises at a rate equal to its heat capacity C_p (because dH = C_p dT at constant pressure). If the products have the larger total heat capacity, their enthalpy climbs faster, and Δ_rH° = H(products) − H(reactants) becomes more positive as T rises. If the reactants have the larger heat capacity, Δ_rH° becomes more negative. Only the difference of heat capacities matters.

Derivation (show every step in an exam)

Step 1. One substance. At constant pressure, dH = C_p dT (Topic 2B). Integrate from T₁ to T₂ for the standard molar enthalpy of species J, assuming no phase change in between:

Hm∘​(J,T2​)=Hm∘​(J,T1​)+∫T1​T2​​Cp,m∘​(J)dT

Step 2. Combine with signed coefficients. Multiply by ν_J (positive for products, negative for reactants) and sum over all species. The left side becomes Δ_rH°(T₂) and the first term on the right becomes Δ_rH°(T₁):

Δr​H∘(T2​)=Δr​H∘(T1​)+∫T1​T2​​Δr​Cp∘​dT(Kirchhoff’s law)

Step 3. Define the reaction heat capacity:

Δr​Cp∘​=J∑​νJ​Cp,m∘​(J)=products∑​νCp,m∘​−reactants∑​νCp,m∘​

In differential form the law says d(Δ_rH°)/dT = Δ_rC_p°: the slope of Δ_rH° against T is the reaction heat capacity.

Step 4. Constant Δ_rC_p°. Individual heat capacities change with T, but their difference changes much less. Over a modest range, take Δ_rC_p° as constant and pull it out of the integral:

Δr​H∘(T2​)=Δr​H∘(T1​)+Δr​Cp∘​(T2​−T1​)

Same result as a Hess cycle (a good alternative derivation). To react at T₂: cool the reactants from T₂ to T₁, react at T₁, then heat the products from T₁ back to T₂. The three steps start and end in the same states as the direct reaction at T₂, so

Δr​H∘(T2​)=−∫T1​T2​​Cp​(reactants)dT+Δr​H∘(T1​)+∫T1​T2​​Cp​(products)dT

Combining the two integrals gives Kirchhoff's law again.

Caveat: phase changes. If a species melts or boils between T₁ and T₂, the simple formula fails. Split the range at the transition, add that species' Δ_trsH°, and switch to the heat capacity of the new phase.

Worked example: Atkins Example 2C.2 (Kirchhoff)

Q. Δ_fH°(H₂O, g) = −241.82 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Estimate it at 100 °C. Molar C_p in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: H₂O(g) 33.58; H₂(g) 28.84; O₂(g) 29.37. Assume they are constant.

Step 1. Equation. H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(g).

Δvap​H∘(373 K)≈44.0+(75)(−41.7×10−3)=44.0−3.1=40.9 kJ mol−1

The measured value is 40.66 kJ mol⁻¹. The liquid has a much larger heat capacity than the vapour, so as both are heated, the liquid's enthalpy catches up with the vapour's and the gap narrows.

For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: Δ_rC_p° ≈ 2(4R) − 4 × (7/2)R = −6R ≈ −50 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (tabulated values give −45). It is negative, so Δ_rH° becomes more negative as T rises, by about 5 kJ mol⁻¹ per 100 K.

2C.4 Experimental techniques

Three instruments, and one conversion you must know: a bomb calorimeter measures ΔU, tables want ΔH, and the bridge is ΔH = ΔU + Δn_g RT. (The bomb calorimeter and the Δn_g RT bridge are introduced in Atkins 2A and 2B; 2C.4 adds DSC and ITC.)

(a) Bomb calorimetry: measuring Δ_rU

Intuition. Burn the sample inside a sealed steel vessel (the "bomb") filled with oxygen at high pressure. The bomb sits in a water bath, and the whole assembly is insulated. The volume cannot change, so no expansion work is done, w = 0, and the heat released equals −ΔU of the reaction.

The calorimeter constant C is the heat capacity of the whole assembly: q = CΔT. Find it by calibration, either

  • electrically, q = IΔφt (current × potential difference × time), or
  • by burning a standard such as benzoic acid, whose Δ_cU is known accurately.

Bookkeeping. The heat released by n mol of reaction is absorbed by the calorimeter:

CΔT=−nΔc​U⟹Δc​U=−nCΔT​

Derivation: converting Δ_rU to Δ_rH

Step 1. From H = U + pV, for the reaction: Δ_rH = Δ_rU + Δ(pV).

Step 2. Solids and liquids have tiny molar volumes. For example, 1 bar × 0.1 L mol⁻¹ = 10 J mol⁻¹, negligible next to kJ mol⁻¹. So only the gases contribute to Δ(pV).

Step 3. Treat the gases as perfect: pV = n_g RT. At a fixed temperature, Δ(pV) = Δn_g RT, so

Δr​H=Δr​U+Δng​RT

Here Δn_g = (moles of gaseous products) − (moles of gaseous reactants) per mole of reaction as written. It is a pure number in this formula, and RT ≈ 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K.

Physical reading. If the gas amount shrinks (Δn_g < 0), then at constant pressure the surroundings push in and do work on the system. That extra energy also leaves as heat, so more heat comes out at constant pressure than at constant volume: Δ_rH is more negative than Δ_rU.

Worked example: Δn_g RT for methane

Q. For CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), Δ_rH° = −890.3 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. What does a bomb calorimeter measure?

A. Gases: 1 mol CO₂ on the right; 1 + 2 = 3 mol on the left (the water is liquid). So Δn_g = 1 − 3 = −2.

Δr​U=Δr​H−Δng​RT=−890.3−(−2)(2.48)=−890.3+5.0=−885.3 kJ mol−1

The correction is only about 0.6%, but exams test the sign. Counting the liquid water as a gas is the classic error.

Worked example: calibrate, then measure

Q. Supplying 10.0 kJ electrically raises a bomb calorimeter's temperature by 2.00 K. Then 0.90 g of glucose (M = 180 g mol⁻¹) is burned in it. Given Δ_cH° = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹, predict ΔT.

A.

  1. C = 10.0 kJ / 2.00 K = 5.00 kJ K⁻¹.
  2. C₆H₁₂O₆(s) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l): Δn_g = 6 − 6 = 0, so Δ_cU = Δ_cH = −2808 kJ mol⁻¹.
  3. n = 0.90/180 = 0.0050 mol, so q = 0.0050 × 2808 = 14.0 kJ.
  4. ΔT = q/C = 14.0/5.00 = 2.8 K.

Run backwards, the same steps turn a measured ΔT into Δ_cU, and then Δn_g RT turns it into Δ_cH.

(b) Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Intuition. Two small, identical pans are heated side by side: one holds the sample and one holds an inert reference that does nothing interesting. A computer heats both at the same steady rate and adjusts the power so their temperatures stay equal. While nothing happens, the sample needs only its ordinary heating power. When the sample melts or a protein unfolds (an endothermic process), it soaks up extra heat, and the computer must feed it extra power to keep up. The instrument records that excess power.

  • "Differential": sample compared with reference.
  • "Scanning": the temperature is swept linearly, T = T₀ + αt, where α is the scan rate (K s⁻¹).

Derivation of the excess heat capacity. With no process, heating by ΔT takes q_p = C_pΔT. During a process the sample needs q_p + q_p,ex. Treat the extra as an apparent rise in heat capacity, from C_p to C_p + C_p,ex. Since ΔT = αt and the excess power is P_ex = q_p,ex/t,

Cp,ex​=ΔTqp,ex​​=αtqp,ex​​=αPex​​

From the curve to ΔH. A plot of C_p,ex against T is a thermogram. In a small step dT, the extra heat absorbed is C_p,ex dT, and at constant pressure heat equals enthalpy change. Add up the steps from where the process starts (T₁) to where it ends (T₂):

ΔH=∫T1​T2​​Cp,ex​dT(the area under the peak)

How to read a thermogram.

  • Flat baseline: no process, so no excess heat capacity.
  • Peak: the process is happening. For protein unfolding, the peak position is the melting temperature T_m. Atkins' example (ubiquitin) keeps its native shape up to about 45 °C, then unfolds endothermically.
  • Peak area = ΔH of the process. A peak pointing up means endothermic (extra power needed); an exothermic process needs less power and shows a dip.
  • Baseline after ≠ baseline before: the product state has a different heat capacity. The height of that step is ΔC_p of the transition. Unfolded proteins usually have a larger C_p than folded ones.

Worked example: from power to C_p,ex

Q. At a scan rate of 1.0 K min⁻¹, a DSC needs 0.10 mW of excess power. What is C_p,ex?

A. α = 1.0 K min⁻¹ = (1/60) K s⁻¹. C_p,ex = P_ex/α = (1.0 × 10⁻⁴ W) × (60 s K⁻¹) = 6.0 × 10⁻³ J K⁻¹ = 6.0 mJ K⁻¹.

Worked example: integrating a peak

Q. A protein's thermogram has a roughly triangular peak 8.0 mJ K⁻¹ high with a base 20 K wide, on a flat baseline. The sample holds 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol of protein. Find the molar enthalpy of unfolding.

A. Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 20 K × 8.0 mJ K⁻¹ = 80 mJ = 0.080 J. Divide by the amount: ΔH = 0.080 J / 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol = 4.0 × 10⁵ J mol⁻¹ = +400 kJ mol⁻¹. It is positive because unfolding breaks many weak interactions. On a real curve, approximate the shape with triangles or trapezoids, or count grid squares (each square = its width in K × its height in mJ K⁻¹).

(c) Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), briefly

ITC is also differential. A reference cell and a sample cell, each with a small heater, are held at a fixed temperature difference. Small, precise volumes of a second reagent are injected into the sample cell, and the instrument records the power needed to hold the temperatures. An exothermic reaction needs less power, an endothermic one more. For injection i, the heat is q_i = P_i Δt, and it equals V Δ_rH Δc_i, where V is the solution volume and Δc_i the change in concentration of unreacted reagent. Summing over all injections gives Δ_rH.

The big idea. U and H are state functions, so their small changes dU and dH can be written in terms of small changes of two variables, with partial derivatives as coefficients. 2D.1 Exact and inexact differentials

Intuition (the slide's hiking analogy)

On a hike, your change in altitude depends only on where you start and where you finish. That makes altitude like a state function. The distance you walk depends on the trail you take, like a path function. Internal energy U is like altitude. Heat q and work w are like distance walked: they describe the journey, not the place. A state has no "heat content", so there is no such thing as q_A.

Definitions

  • State function: depends only on the current state, not on its history. Examples: U, H, p, V, T (and later S).
  • Path function: depends on the route between states. Examples: q and w.
  • Exact differential: a small change whose integral depends only on the end points. dU is exact:
∫AB​dU=UB​−UA​,∮dU=0
  • Inexact differential: a small quantity whose integral depends on the path, so the path must be specified. The slides write it with a barred d, đq and đw:
∫AB​đq=q(not qB​−qA​),∮đq=0 in general

Notice that the integral of đq is just q, the heat for that path, never a "Δq".

The first law in differential form adds two inexact pieces to give an exact one:

dU=đq+đw=đq−pdV(quasi-static)

The test for exactness [beyond the slides; from Atkins Toolkit 9]

Suppose a differential is written as

df=M(x,y)dx+N(x,y)dy

Test: df is exact if and only if

(∂y∂M​)x​=(∂x∂N​)y​

Why. If df is exact, a function f exists with M = (∂f/∂x)_y and N = (∂f/∂y)_x. Then ∂M/∂y and ∂N/∂x are both the mixed second derivative ∂²f/∂x∂y, and mixed derivatives don't depend on the order. If the cross-derivatives differ, no such f can exist, so the differential is inexact.

Worked example: a maths warm-up

Q. Is df = (2xy + 3) dx + (x² + 4y) dy exact? If so, find f.

A. M = 2xy + 3, so ∂M/∂y = 2x. N = x² + 4y, so ∂N/∂x = 2x. They are equal, so df is exact.

To find f, integrate M with respect to x, holding y fixed: f = x²y + 3x + g(y). Differentiate with respect to y and match to N: x² + g′(y) = x² + 4y, so g = 2y². Hence f = x²y + 3x + 2y² (+ a constant).

By contrast, y dx − x dy has ∂M/∂y = 1 but ∂N/∂x = −1, so it is inexact.

Exam-style example: q and w are inexact, and q/T is exact

Q. For a reversible change of a perfect gas with constant C_V, use T and V as variables. Test dU, đw and đq for exactness.

A. Write each one as M dT + N dV and compare ∂M/∂V with ∂N/∂T.

  • dU = C_V dT + 0 dV. ∂C_V/∂V = 0 (C_V is constant) and ∂(0)/∂T = 0. Equal, so exact.
  • đw = −p dV = 0 dT − (nRT/V) dV. ∂(0)/∂V = 0, but ∂(−nRT/V)/∂T = −nR/V. Unequal, so inexact.
  • đq = dU − đw = C_V dT + (nRT/V) dV. ∂C_V/∂V = 0, but ∂(nRT/V)/∂T = nR/V. Unequal, so inexact.

Now divide đq by T:

Tđqrev​​=TCV​​dT+VnR​dV

Now ∂(C_V/T)/∂V = 0 and ∂(nR/V)/∂T = 0. Equal, so it is exact. Dividing by T turns heat into the differential of a state function. That state function is the entropy, dS = đq_rev/T, the centre of Topic 3A. Mathematicians call 1/T an integrating factor.

2D.2 Changes in internal energy

(a) dU = C_V dT + π_T dV

Setting. Take a closed system of fixed composition. An equation of state links p, V and T, so any two of them fix the state. Choose T and V; this gives the simplest formulas for U. The total differential of U(T, V) is

dU=(∂T∂U​)V​dT+(∂V∂U​)T​dV

Both coefficients have names:

CV​=(∂T∂U​)V​πT​≡(∂V∂U​)T​(internal pressure)
dU=CV​dT+πT​dV
  • π_T has units of pressure: J m⁻³ = N m⁻² = Pa. That is why it is called the internal pressure.
  • It measures how U changes when the molecules are moved further apart at constant temperature.
  • "U of a perfect gas depends only on T" (Lecture 2) is exactly the statement π_T = 0.

Molecular interpretation of π_T and its sign

At constant T, the average kinetic energy of the molecules is fixed. So any change in U on expansion must come from the potential energy of intermolecular interactions. Think of the intermolecular potential curve: a steep repulsive wall at short range and an attractive well beyond it.

  • Perfect gas: no interactions, so moving molecules apart changes nothing: π_T = 0.
  • Attractions dominate (most gases at ordinary densities, and liquids): pulling molecules apart lifts them up out of the well. The potential energy rises, so U rises with V: π_T > 0.
  • Repulsions dominate (very high density, molecules jammed together): letting them move apart lets them slide down the repulsive wall. The potential energy falls: π_T < 0.

π_T from an equation of state

A general result, derived in Atkins Topic 3E (after the quiz range), lets you calculate π_T from any equation of state. Use it; you don't need to derive it.

πT​=T(∂T∂p​)V​−p(thermodynamic equation of state)

Perfect gas. p = nRT/V, so (∂p/∂T)_V = nR/V. Then

πT​=T⋅VnR​−p=p−p=0✓

van der Waals gas. Molar form: p = RT/(V_m − b) − a/V_m². At constant V_m, only the first term depends on T, so (∂p/∂T)_V = R/(V_m − b). Then

πT​=Vm​−bRT​−p=Vm​−bRT​−(Vm​−bRT​−Vm2​a​)=Vm2​a​

Only the attraction parameter a survives, so a van der Waals gas always has π_T > 0. The repulsion parameter b cancels out, so this model can never give the negative π_T of a repulsion-dominated fluid.

Joule's experiment (1843): why he saw no temperature change

Set-up. Two metal vessels sat in a water bath, joined by a tap. One held air at about 22 atm; the other was evacuated. Joule opened the tap and watched the bath thermometer.

Argument.

  1. The gas expands into vacuum (free expansion), so w = 0.
  2. The bath temperature did not change, so no heat flowed: q = 0.
  3. So ΔU = q + w = 0, even though V changed. Joule concluded (∂U/∂V)_T = π_T = 0.

The catch. The water bath had a huge heat capacity compared with the gas, so any real temperature change was far too small to see. Joule had in effect found a property of the perfect gas, not of real air.

Worked example: how big is the real effect?

Q. 1.00 mol of N₂ (a van der Waals gas) at 298 K expands freely from 24.8 L to 49.6 L in an insulated container. Estimate ΔT of the gas. Use a = 1.370 L² bar mol⁻² and C_V,m = (5/2)R = 20.8 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

A. Free and insulated, so q = w = 0 and ΔU = 0. Integrate dU = C_V dT + π_T dV with π_T = a/V_m². The temperature change is tiny, so treat C_V as constant:

0=CV,m​ΔT+∫Vi​Vf​​Vm2​a​dVm​=CV,m​ΔT+a(Vm,i​1​−Vm,f​1​)
ΔT=−CV,m​a​(Vm,i​1​−Vm,f​1​)

Numbers: 1/24.8 − 1/49.6 = 1/49.6 ≈ 0.0202 L⁻¹. Then a × 0.0202 = 0.0276 L bar mol⁻¹ = 2.76 J mol⁻¹ (since 1 L bar = 100 J). So ΔT ≈ −2.76/20.8 ≈ −0.13 K in the gas alone.

The gas cools: molecules climbing out of each other's attractive wells pay for it with kinetic energy. Now spread those 2.76 J over even 1 kg of water (heat capacity 4.2 kJ K⁻¹): the bath would change by less than a thousandth of a kelvin. That is why Joule saw nothing.

2D.3 Changes in enthalpy

Intuition

H = U + pV is built from state functions, so it is a state function too, and dH is exact. U was easiest as a function of T and V. H is naturally a function of T and p, because H is the quantity that matters when pressure is controlled (ΔH = q_p). So we repeat the 2D.2 game with H(p, T) and ask: which physical quantity is (∂H/∂p)_T?

Derivation 1: hold H constant (Atkins' route)

Step 1. Total differential of H(p, T), with (∂H/∂T)_p = C_p:

dH=(∂p∂H​)T​dp+Cp​dT

Step 2. Now consider a change at constant H, so dH = 0:

0=(∂p∂H​)T​dp+Cp​dT(constant H)

Step 3. Divide by dp. Because H is constant, dT/dp becomes (∂T/∂p)_H:

(∂p∂H​)T​=−Cp​(∂p∂T​)H​=−Cp​μ

The new derivative is the Joule–Thomson coefficient, the rate at which temperature changes with pressure when H is held fixed:

μ=(∂p∂T​)H​

Step 4. Substitute back into Step 1:

dH=−μCp​dp+Cp​dT

Derivation 2: the Euler chain relation (the slides' route)

H, p and T are three variables linked by the state of the system. The Euler chain relation for them reads

(∂p∂H​)T​(∂T∂p​)H​(∂H∂T​)p​=−1

Solve for the first factor and use the reciprocal rule on the other two, (∂p/∂T)_H = 1/(∂T/∂p)_H and (∂T/∂H)_p = 1/(∂H/∂T)_p:

(∂p∂H​)T​=−(∂T∂H​)p​(∂p∂T​)H​=−Cp​μ

Both routes give the same answer. In an exam, the chain-rule version is quicker to write.

The isothermal Joule–Thomson coefficient μ_T

μT​≡(∂p∂H​)T​=−Cp​μ
  • μ_T is the enthalpy analogue of π_T. π_T says how U changes with V at constant T; μ_T says how H changes with p at constant T.
  • Both are zero for a perfect gas. So U and H of a perfect gas depend only on T. (Consistent with H = U + nRT.)
  • With C_p,m in place of C_p you get the molar μ_T, in J mol⁻¹ Pa⁻¹ (or J mol⁻¹ bar⁻¹).

How it is measured. Measuring μ directly means keeping H fixed, which is awkward. It is easier to keep T fixed. Let the gas flow steadily through a porous plug from p_i to p_f, with a small heater beside the plug that holds the temperature constant. By the same flow-work argument used for the Joule–Thomson expansion in 2D.4, the heat q_m supplied per mole equals ΔH_m. So

μT​≈ΔpΔHm​​=pf​−pi​qm​​

The slide writes this as μ_T ≈ −Q/Δp, with Δp taken as the size of the pressure drop. It is the same thing. Then μ = −μ_T/C_p.

Worked example: the heater power for nitrogen

Q. For N₂ at 298 K, μ = +0.27 K bar⁻¹ and C_p,m = 29.1 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (a) Find μ_T. (b) How much heat per mole must the heater supply to keep the gas at 298 K as it flows from 10 bar to 1 bar?

A. (a) μ_T = −C_p,m μ = −29.1 × 0.27 ≈ −7.9 J mol⁻¹ bar⁻¹.

(b) Δp = 1 − 10 = −9 bar, so q_m = ΔH_m ≈ μ_T Δp = (−7.9)(−9) ≈ +71 J mol⁻¹. It is positive: heat must be put in, because nitrogen would otherwise cool on expansion (μ > 0).

2D.4 The Joule–Thomson effect

Intuition

Open the valve on a compressed-gas cylinder and the valve gets cold; a CO₂ fire extinguisher even frosts up. A gas forced from high pressure to low pressure through a narrow opening, with no heat exchanged, usually changes temperature. That change is the Joule–Thomson effect, and it is how air, nitrogen and other gases are liquefied. Joule and Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) found a way to measure it under a clean condition: constant enthalpy.

The set-up

Gas is pushed steadily through a porous plug (a throttle) inside an insulated tube. Upstream it is at a constant high pressure p_i and temperature T_i; downstream at a constant low pressure p_f and temperature T_f. Thermometers on both sides measure T_i and T_f once the flow is steady. The tube is insulated, so q = 0.

Derivation: why the expansion is isenthalpic

Follow one fixed amount of gas as it passes through the plug. Picture the gas behind it and the gas in front of it as two pistons that keep the pressures constant.

Step 0. Adiabatic. q = 0, so ΔU = w (w = work done on the gas).

Step 1. Upstream work. Before passing, our gas occupies V_i at p_i. The gas behind pushes it through at constant pressure p_i, and its upstream volume goes from V_i to 0:

w1​=−pi​(0−Vi​)=+pi​Vi​

This is positive: the gas is pushed on.

Step 2. Downstream work. On the far side, our gas must push the gas in front out of the way at constant pressure p_f, and its downstream volume goes from 0 to V_f:

w2​=−pf​(Vf​−0)=−pf​Vf​

This is negative: the gas does work on its surroundings.

Step 3. First law.

Uf​−Ui​=w1​+w2​=pi​Vi​−pf​Vf​

Step 4. Rearrange into the definition of H:

Uf​+pf​Vf​=Ui​+pi​Vi​⟹Hf​=Hi​

The expansion is isenthalpic. The measured ratio ΔT/Δp is therefore a temperature change at constant H. In the limit of a small pressure drop, it is the Joule–Thomson coefficient

μ=(∂p∂T​)H​,ΔT≈μΔp
  • μ > 0: expansion (Δp < 0) gives ΔT < 0, so the gas cools.
  • μ < 0: the gas warms.
  • Perfect gas: μ = 0. Its H depends only on T, so constant H means constant T. Any Joule–Thomson effect is therefore a sign of intermolecular forces.

A. ΔT ≈ μΔp = (+0.27)(−10) = −2.7 K. (μ is intensive, so the amount of gas doesn't matter. And 1 atm ≈ 1 bar at this precision.) Throttling from about 200 bar to 1 bar gives roughly 0.25 × (−200) ≈ −50 K: tens of kelvin, enough to matter. This is only an estimate, because μ itself changes with p and T along the way.

Molecular interpretation: attractions cool, repulsions heat

The gas is insulated, so any energy it needs must come from within. As it expands, its molecules move farther apart.

  • Attractions dominate. The molecules have to climb out of each other's potential wells. Like a ball thrown upward slowing against gravity, they pay with kinetic energy, so they slow down: T falls, μ > 0. (Atkins: this happens when Z < 1.)
  • Repulsions dominate. Moving apart lets the molecules slide down the repulsive wall, turning potential energy into kinetic energy: T rises, μ < 0 (Z > 1).
  • Perfect gas. No interactions, no change: μ = 0.
  • Temperature decides the winner. At high T, molecules move fast, collide hard and spend little time in the attractive region, so repulsions win. At low T, attractions win.

Quantitative: μ for a van der Waals gas

Step 1. Low-pressure van der Waals molar volume. From Topic 1C, the second virial coefficient of a van der Waals gas is B = b − a/RT, so V_m ≈ RT/p + B:

Vm​≈pRT​+b−RTa​

Step 2. A general formula for μ, derived in Topic 3E (use it):

μ=Cp,m​T(∂Vm​/∂T)p​−Vm​​

Step 3. Differentiate Step 1 at constant p:

(∂T∂Vm​​)p​=pR​+RT2a​⟹T(∂T∂Vm​​)p​=pRT​+RTa​

Step 4. Subtract V_m. The RT/p terms cancel:

T(∂T∂Vm​​)p​−Vm​=pRT​+RTa​−pRT​−b+RTa​=RT2a​−b
μ≈Cp,m​1​(RT2a​−b)
  • 2a/RT comes from attractions and drives cooling. It shrinks as T rises.
  • −b comes from molecular size (repulsions) and drives heating. It does not depend on T.
  • μ changes sign when the two terms balance. That is the inversion temperature:
RTI​2a​=b⟹TI​=Rb2a​

Below T_I the gas cools on throttling; above T_I it warms.

Note. This low-pressure μ does not vanish as p → 0, even though the equation of state then approaches the perfect-gas law. μ depends on derivatives of the equation of state (through B(T)), and those don't vanish.

[beyond the quiz] Where the Step 2 formula comes from. In Topic 3, dH = T dS + V dp. At constant T, (∂H/∂p)_T = T(∂S/∂p)_T + V. A Maxwell relation (from the Gibbs energy) gives (∂S/∂p)_T = −(∂V/∂T)_p. So μ_T = V − T(∂V/∂T)_p, and μ = −μ_T/C_p gives the Step 2 formula.

The inversion curve

T_I actually depends on pressure. In the (p, T) plane, a boundary curve, the inversion curve, separates a cooling region (μ > 0, inside) from a heating region (μ < 0, outside). At a given pressure, a gas typically has two inversion temperatures, an upper and a lower one, and it cools on throttling only between them. Throttling moves the gas along a curve of constant H (an isenthalp). The inversion curve passes through the point on each isenthalp where its slope changes sign.

Liquefaction: the Linde refrigerator

  1. Compress the gas, and remove the heat produced by compression.
  2. Throttle it through a valve. It cools, provided it is inside the inversion curve.
  3. Send the cold gas back past the incoming high-pressure gas in a counter-current heat exchanger, pre-cooling it.
  4. Repeat. Each pass starts colder, so the temperature falls cycle by cycle until some of the gas condenses and liquid drips from the throttle.

[embedded content: Linde refrigerator: compress, cool, pre-cool, throttle, recycle]

The loop is the trick: gas cooled at the throttle flows back through the heat exchanger and pre-cools the next batch, so every pass reaches the throttle colder than the one before.

H₂ and He must first be pre-cooled below their inversion temperatures: H₂ with liquid N₂ (77 K), He with liquid H₂ (20 K). Otherwise throttling would warm them.

Answers to the check-your-understanding questions

C1. Liquid Br₂ is bromine's reference state at 298 K (its most stable form at 1 bar). Its formation reaction is the null reaction Br₂(l) → Br₂(l), so Δ_fH° = 0. Br₂(g) is not the reference state. Its "formation" from the element is Br₂(l) → Br₂(g), which is just vaporization, so Δ_fH°(Br₂, g) = Δ_vapH°(Br₂) = +30.9 kJ mol⁻¹.

C2. Δ_rH = Δ_rU + Δn_g RT = −100.0 + (1)(2.48) = −97.5 kJ mol⁻¹. More heat is released in the bomb: 100.0 kJ against 97.5 kJ per mole. In an open beaker at constant pressure, the gas produced pushes back the atmosphere, so 2.5 kJ of the energy leaves as work instead of heat.

C3. (a) Δ_rH°(398 K) = −50.0 + (20 × 10⁻³)(100) = −48.0 kJ mol⁻¹. The products have the larger heat capacity, so Δ_rH° rises with T. (b) The unfolded state has a larger heat capacity than the native state. The height of the step is ΔC_p for unfolding, and it is positive.

D1. M = 3x²y, so ∂M/∂y = 3x². N = x³ + 2y, so ∂N/∂x = 3x². They are equal, so dz is exact. Integrating M over x gives z = x³y + g(y). Then ∂z/∂y = x³ + g′(y) = x³ + 2y, so g = y², and z = x³y + y² (+ a constant).

D2. (a) A perfect gas has μ = 0 at every temperature and pressure, because its H depends only on T. μ never changes sign, so there is nothing to "invert". In van der Waals terms a = b = 0, so neither the cooling term 2a/RT nor the heating term b exists. (b) Joule free expansion (q = 0, w = 0) keeps U constant. Joule–Thomson throttling keeps H constant. For a perfect gas, both U and H depend only on T (π_T = 0 and μ_T = 0), so constant U or constant H means constant T.

D3. RT = 24.8 L bar mol⁻¹. 2a/RT = 7.280/24.8 = 0.294 L mol⁻¹, and subtracting b gives 0.251 L mol⁻¹. So μ ≈ 100 × 0.251/37.1 ≈ 0.68 K bar⁻¹: right sign and size, but below the measured 1.1. T_I = 2a/(Rb) = 7.280/(0.08314 × 0.0427) = 7.280/0.00355 ≈ 2050 K, above the measured ~1500 K. As with N₂, the van der Waals model overestimates T_I.

Common mistakes

Thermochemistry (2C)

  1. Giving Δ_fH° = 0 to the wrong thing. Only elements in their reference state get zero. Br₂(g), C(diamond), O₃(g) and H(g) all have non-zero Δ_fH°.
  2. Order of subtraction. With formation data it is products − reactants. With combustion data it is reactants − products.
  3. Forgetting the coefficients, or forgetting that "kJ mol⁻¹" means per mole of reaction as written. Doubling the equation doubles Δ_rH°.
  4. The wrong phase of water. H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) differ by 44 kJ mol⁻¹ per mole of water. Combustion enthalpies assume liquid water unless told otherwise.
  5. Miscounting Δn_g. Count gases only, per mole of reaction. Use Δ_rH = Δ_rU + Δn_g RT with RT ≈ 2.48 kJ mol⁻¹, not 2480.
  6. Adding transition enthalpies at different temperatures, for example 6.01 kJ (273 K) + 40.66 kJ (373 K) for sublimation.
  7. Kirchhoff slips: adding J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ × K straight to kJ mol⁻¹; dropping the ½ in front of O₂; ignoring a phase change inside the temperature range.
  8. DSC: integrating down to zero instead of down to the baseline, or reading ΔH off the peak height instead of the area.

State functions and Joule–Thomson (2D)

  1. Writing Δq or q_B − q_A. q and w belong to paths, not states: ∫đq = q.
  2. Ignoring the subscript. (∂U/∂T)_V = C_V, but (∂U/∂T)_p = C_V + απ_TV.
  3. Dropping the minus sign in the Euler chain relation, or in κ_T = −(1/V)(∂V/∂p)_T and μ_T = −C_pμ.
  4. Saying Joule proved π_T = 0 for real gases. His bath was too insensitive; real gases have π_T = a/V_m² > 0.
  5. Putting b into the van der Waals π_T. Only a appears. b does appear in μ, as the −b heating term in (2a/RT − b)/C_p,m.
  6. Confusing Joule with Joule–Thomson. Free expansion keeps U constant. Throttling keeps H constant, because the flow work p_iV_i − p_fV_f is not zero.
  7. "All gases cool when they expand." He and H₂ warm on throttling at room temperature, because they are above their inversion temperatures.
  8. Sign of μ. μ > 0 means cooling, because the pressure falls (Δp < 0) and ΔT = μΔp.
  9. Units. 1 L bar = 100 J; 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa; with a in L² bar mol⁻², use R = 0.08314 L bar K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
