Learn

Gases (Atkins 1A to 1C)

14 min read

Scope: Thermo-1 (slides thermo.tex 62–595) plus tutorial P1–P4. Anything marked [beyond slides] is Atkins only. Constants: R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 0.08314 L bar K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; k_B = 1.381×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹.

1A The perfect gas

Key ideas

  • A system is what you study, the surroundings are the rest. Open: matter and energy cross. Closed: energy only. Isolated: neither.
  • At equilibrium the state is fixed by a few variables (P, V, T, n) linked by an equation of state. Intensive: P, T, density. Extensive: V, n, U. State function: U, T. Path function: Q, W.
  • Zeroth law: if A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, they are in equilibrium with each other. This is why a thermometer (C) can compare A and B. Temperature is the shared property. A metal bench feels colder than a wooden one only because it conducts heat away faster; both are at the same T.
  • A constant-volume gas thermometer has P ∝ T. At low pressure every gas extrapolates to P = 0 at −273.15 °C, which defines absolute zero and the Kelvin scale.
  • Boyle (PV const), Charles (V ∝ T) and Avogadro (V ∝ n) combine into one law, exact only as P → 0.

Key equations

PV=nRT=NkB​T,T/K=θ/∘C+273.15,T=T3​P3​P​(T3​=273.16 K)

Worked example (tutorial P1). A constant-volume thermometer reads 6.69 kPa at the triple point. Find the reading at 100 °C, and the temperature when it reads 8.00 kPa.

P=P3​T3​T​=6.69×273.16373.15​=9.14 kPa,ΔTΔP​=T3​P3​​=24.5 PaK−1
T=273.16×6.698.00​=326.6 K=53.5 ∘C

The zeroth law justifies reading the oil's temperature: after a while the thermometer and oil are in equilibrium, so they share T.

Beyond slides (Atkins 1A.1(a), 1A.2(b))

  • Pressure is force per area, P = F/A. Units: 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻² = 1 kg m⁻¹ s⁻². 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa (the standard pressure p°). 1 atm = 101 325 Pa exactly. 1 Torr = 101 325/760 Pa ≈ 133.3 Pa.
  • Dalton's law: a mixture of perfect gases exerts the sum of the pressures each would exert alone. The partial pressure is p_J = x_J p, with mole fraction x_J = n_J/n.
p=pA​+pB​+…,pJ​=xJ​p,xJ​=nnJ​​,J∑​xJ​=1

It is exact only in the perfect-gas limit.

1B The kinetic model

Key ideas

  • Model: N point-like molecules of mass m in random motion, no forces except brief elastic collisions, elastic wall hits.
  • Pressure is the average momentum delivered to the walls per unit time and area. Heavier or faster molecules hit harder, so P depends on ⟨v²⟩.
  • Comparing with PV = Nk_BT shows temperature is a measure of mean translational kinetic energy. It depends on T only, not on mass.
  • Speeds are spread out (Maxwell–Boltzmann). The v² factor is the growing number of ways to have a high speed; the exponential is the rarity of high energy. Raising T shifts the peak right and flattens the curve. Lighter molecules give a broader curve.

Key equations

PV=31​Nm⟨v2⟩=32​N⟨Ek​⟩,⟨Ek​⟩=23​kB​T,Ek,molar​=23​RT
f(v)=4π(2πRTM​)3/2v2e−Mv2/2RT
vmp​=M2RT​​<vˉ=πM8RT​​<vrms​=M3RT​​

For N₂ at 300 K: 422, 476, 517 m/s. All scale as √(T/M).

Derivation outline 1: pressure and ⟨E_k⟩

  1. One molecule, cube of side L, hits the wall with v_x and rebounds: Δp_x = −2mv_x per hit.
  2. It returns to the same wall every Δt = 2L/v_x, so the mean force is F = 2mv_x/(2L/v_x) = mv_x²/L.
  3. Sum over N molecules and divide by area L²: P = Nm⟨v_x²⟩/V.
  4. Isotropy gives ⟨v_x²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3, so PV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩ = ⅔N⟨E_k⟩.
  5. Equate to Nk_BT: ⟨E_k⟩ = ½m⟨v²⟩ = (3/2)k_BT. Hence v_rms = √(3k_BT/m) = √(3RT/M).

Derivation outline 2: v_mp from df/dv = 0 (tutorial P3a, homework P1B.10)

Drop constants and differentiate g(v) = v² e^{−Mv²/2RT}:

dvdg​=(2v−RTMv3​)e−Mv2/2RT=0⇒vmp2​=M2RT​

The roots v = 0 and v → ∞ are minima, so the non-trivial root is the maximum.

Worked example (tutorial P3). N₂ (M = 0.0280 kg mol⁻¹) at 500 K.

(a) v_mp = √(2×8.314×500/0.0280) = 545 m s⁻¹.

(b) Fraction with 290–300 m s⁻¹ ≈ f(v)Δv at v = 295, Δv = 10:

2RTMv2​=2×8.314×5000.0280×2952​=0.293,f≈9.1×10−4 sm−1⇒fΔv≈0.9%

(c) At 1000 K the fraction is smaller (about 0.37%): the peak moves to about 770 m/s and the curve flattens, leaving fewer molecules at 295 m/s.

Also on the slides (short exam answers)

  • N₂ at 300 K: v_rms = √(3×8.314×300/0.0280) = 517 m s⁻¹, close to the speed of sound in air (343 m/s). H₂: about 1.9 km/s. He: 1.37 km/s.
  • Equal T gives equal ⟨E_k⟩ (H₂ and O₂ in one box). H₂ is 4× faster since M differs by 16.
  • Doubling T multiplies v_rms by √2, not 2. Pressure at fixed V doubles.
  • He escape (tutorial P2): v_rms = 1.37 km/s at 300 K is far below 11.2 km/s. v_rms would equal 11.2 km/s only at T = Mv²/3R ≈ 2×10⁴ K. Escape still happens because the high-speed tail of the distribution is never empty, and collisions refill it. Total translational energy of 1 L at 1 bar is (3/2)PV = 150 J for any gas.

Beyond slides: collisions (Atkins 1B.2)

Treat molecules as hard spheres with collision cross-section σ = πd². A molecule sweeps volume σ v_rel per unit time, with relative speed v_rel = √2 ̄v. Then:

z=Vσvrel​N​=kB​Tσvrel​p​,λ=zvrel​​=σpkB​T​

z is collisions per second per molecule; λ is the mean free path. At fixed T, λ ∝ 1/p. At fixed V, λ is independent of T, since p ∝ T. Atkins' N₂ example at 1 atm, 25 °C with σ = 0.45 nm²: z ≈ 7×10⁹ s⁻¹ and λ ≈ 90 nm.

1C Real gases

Key ideas

  • The perfect-gas law assumes point molecules with no forces. It fails at high P (molecules have finite size, so less free volume) and low T (attractions reduce the force and frequency of wall hits).
  • The compression factor Z measures the deviation. Z < 1: attraction dominates. Z > 1: repulsion (size) dominates, typical at high P.
  • van der Waals corrects the two assumptions with two constants: b (excluded volume per mole) and a (strength of attraction).
  • Why a/V_m² and not a/V_m: attraction needs pairs of molecules, so it scales as the density squared.

Key equations

Z=RTPVm​​,(P+Vm2​a​)(Vm​−b)=RT⟺P=Vm​−bRT​−Vm2​a​
Gas a (L² bar mol⁻²) b (L mol⁻¹)
He 0.0346 0.0238
N₂ 1.370 0.0387
CO₂ 3.640 0.0427

Worked example (tutorial P4). 5.00 mol in a 1.00 L cylinder at 298 K. Use R = 0.08314 L bar K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so RT = 24.78 L bar mol⁻¹ and V_m = 0.200 L mol⁻¹.

Pideal​=0.20024.78​=124 bar (same for any gas)
PN2​​=0.200−0.038724.78​−0.20021.370​=153.6−34.3=119 bar
PHe​=0.200−0.023824.78​−0.20020.0346​=140.6−0.9=140 bar

Since V_m and T are the same, Z = P/P_ideal. For N₂, Z = 119/124 = 0.96 < 1: the a term (−34.3 bar) slightly outweighs the b term (+29.7 bar). For He, Z = 140/124 = 1.13 > 1: a is negligible, so b wins.

Slide example: 1.00 mol CO₂ in 0.500 L at 300 K gives P_ideal = 49.9 bar but P_vdW = 54.5 − 14.6 ≈ 40.0 bar. The ideal law is about 25% too high because attraction dominates; CO₂ liquefies easily.

Beyond slides (Atkins 1C)

  • Virial equation. Expand Z in powers of the density. B and C depend on temperature and are fitted from Z data. B is usually the dominant correction.
Z=1+Vm​B​+Vm2​C​+…(or Z=1+B′p+C′p2+…)
  • Boyle temperature. At T_B the second virial coefficient is zero, so Z → 1 with zero slope as p → 0 and the gas is nearly perfect over a wide range. For vdW, T_B = a/(Rb), which gives about 426 K for N₂.
  • Critical constants. Below T_c, compressing a gas along an isotherm condenses it to a liquid. At T_c the two phases merge at the critical point and above T_c there is only one (supercritical) phase. For vdW the critical point is where the isotherm has an inflection with zero slope:
Tc​=27Rb8a​,Vc​=3b,pc​=27b2a​,Zc​=RTc​pc​Vc​​=83​

For CO₂ this gives T_c ≈ 304 K and p_c ≈ 74 bar, close to the measured 304 K and 74 bar.

  • Corresponding states. Reduced variables X_r = X/X_c (for p, V_m, T). Real gases at the same p_r and T_r have similar Z and similar deviations from ideality. In reduced form the vdW equation contains no gas-specific constants.

Homework (from the plan). None is set for 1C. Tutorial P4 (above) covers it.

Check your understanding (1C). N₂ has Z < 1 at moderate pressure but Z > 1 at very high pressure at the same T. Explain using the a and b terms. Then state whether Z for He at 298 K can ever be below 1.

Answers to the check questions

  • 1A. No. The ratio must use kelvin: P₂/P₁ = T₂/T₁ = 313.15/293.15 = 1.07, a 6.8% rise. Doubling 20 °C to 40 °C is not doubling T.
  • 1B. H₂ has the larger v_rms. Both have the same mean kinetic energy per molecule, (3/2)k_BT, which depends on T only. v_rms(H₂)/v_rms(He) = √(M_He/M_H₂) = √(4.00/2.016) = 1.41.
  • 1C. At moderate pressure the molecules are far enough apart that attraction (the a/V_m² term) is the larger correction, so Z < 1. At very high pressure V_m approaches b, so RT/(V_m − b) grows faster than a/V_m² and repulsion wins, giving Z > 1. For He at 298 K, Z is always above 1: its Boyle temperature a/(Rb) ≈ 17 K is far below 298 K, so the b term dominates at every pressure.

Exam traps

  1. M in kg/mol. N₂ is 0.0280, not 28.0. Using g/mol makes v_rms wrong by √1000.
  2. T in kelvin in every formula and ratio, including P ∝ T.
  3. Units of R. 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with SI (Pa, m³); 0.08314 L bar K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ with L and bar. Never mix them.
  4. v_rms ∝ √T. Doubling T gives ×√2 in speed, ×2 in kinetic energy.
  5. Equal T means equal ⟨E_k⟩, not equal speed.
  6. v_mp < mean < v_rms, with the factors √2 : √(8/π) : √3.
  7. In vdW use V_m = V/n, and check a and b units (L² bar mol⁻², L mol⁻¹).
  8. Z < 1 means attraction dominates, Z > 1 means size dominates.
