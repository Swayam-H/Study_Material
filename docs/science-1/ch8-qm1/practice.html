Particle properties of waves

20 min read · 31 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

31 questions with worked answers (tap a solution to open it). These follow the style of your past papers (2025 and 2026 midsems, Quiz-1, the sci_midsem.pdf thermo answers) and the homework list. Expect short "state / explain / show" items for 2–3 points, derivations for 4–6 points, and estimates with round numbers, because no calculator is allowed. Points are shown in brackets. Answers are in the next section; try each question first.

A. State and explain

Problem 1 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

State the Stefan–Boltzmann law and Wien's displacement law. What does each tell you about a hotter blackbody?

Solution

Solution. Stefan–Boltzmann law. Total power radiated per unit area by a blackbody:

AP​=σT4,σ=5.67×10−8 W m−2K−4

Doubling T gives 16 times the power.

Wien's displacement law. The wavelength of peak emission:

λmax​T=2.898×10−3 m K

A hotter blackbody radiates much more power (as T4) and its spectrum peaks at a shorter wavelength (so it glows from red to white to blue).

Problem 2 [QM 1 · Recall · 3 pts]

What is the ultraviolet catastrophe? How did Planck's hypothesis remove it?

Solution

Solution. The catastrophe. Classically every cavity mode gets energy kT (equipartition), and the number of modes per unit frequency grows as ν2:

u(ν)=c38πν2​kT → ∞as ν→∞

The predicted energy density (and total power) diverges in the ultraviolet, in contradiction with experiment.

Planck's fix. Allow a mode of frequency ν to have only energies E=nhν.

  • At high ν, one quantum hν≫kT is rarely supplied by thermal motion, so these modes are almost never excited.
  • u(ν) therefore turns over and falls to zero:
u(ν)=c38πhν3​ehν/kT−11​

Problem 3 [QM 1 · Recall · 3 pts]

List three observations of the photoelectric effect that the wave theory of light cannot explain.

Solution

Solution. Three observations wave theory cannot explain (any three):

  1. No time delay. Electrons are emitted almost instantly (within 10−9 s), even for very dim light. A wave would need time to build up the energy.
  2. Kmax​ is independent of intensity. Brighter light gives more electrons, not faster ones. A wave's energy grows with intensity.
  3. Threshold frequency. Below ν0​ there is no emission at any intensity.
  4. Kmax​ rises linearly with ν, independent of intensity.

Explanation. Light arrives in quanta of energy hν, and one photon ejects one electron.

Problem 4 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

State Einstein's photoelectric equation and define every symbol.

Solution

Solution. Einstein's photoelectric equation.

hν=Kmax​+ϕ
  • hν: energy of one photon (frequency ν).
  • Kmax​: largest kinetic energy of an emitted electron (=eV0​, with V0​ the stopping potential).
  • ϕ: work function, the minimum energy needed to free an electron from the surface.

Consequence. Emission needs hν≥ϕ, so the threshold is ν0​=ϕ/h.

Problem 5 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why does doubling the intensity of light double the photocurrent but leave the stopping potential unchanged?

Solution

Solution. Intensity is the number of photons arriving per second (each of energy hν).

  • Doubling the intensity doubles the number of photons, so twice as many electrons are freed: the photocurrent doubles.
  • Each photon still carries the same hν, so each electron gets the same Kmax​=hν−ϕ. The stopping potential V0​=Kmax​/e is unchanged.

To change V0​ you must change the frequency, not the brightness.

Problem 6 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is meant by wave–particle duality of light? Name one experiment for each side.

Solution

Solution. Wave–particle duality. Light shows wave behaviour when it propagates and particle behaviour when it exchanges energy with matter.

aspect evidence experiment
wave interference, diffraction Young's double slit; X-ray diffraction
particle quanta of energy hν and momentum h/λ photoelectric effect; Compton scattering

Complementarity. An experiment shows one aspect or the other, never both at once. The wave describes where photons are likely to be; the particle describes how they are absorbed.

Problem 7 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why is X-ray production called the inverse photoelectric effect?

Solution

Solution. Photoelectric effect. A photon is absorbed and its energy becomes the kinetic energy of an electron:

hν→ϕ+K

X-ray tube. An electron accelerated through V arrives with energy eV and, on striking the target, produces a photon:

eV→hνmax​

So the process is the photoelectric effect in reverse: electron kinetic energy becomes photon energy.

Consequence. The short-wavelength cutoff of the continuous spectrum is

λmin​=eVhc​

independent of the target material.

Problem 8 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

State Bragg's law. Why can a crystal diffract X-rays but not visible light?

Solution

Solution. Bragg's law.

2dsinθ=nλ

Why a crystal diffracts X-rays but not visible light. Diffraction by a lattice is strong only when λ is comparable to or smaller than the plane spacing d (about 0.1 nm). Condition: sinθ=nλ/2d≤1 requires λ≤2d.

  • X-rays have λ≈0.1 nm: works.
  • Visible light has λ=400 to 700 nm, thousands of times larger than d: sinθ>1, so there is no Bragg reflection.

Problem 9 [QM 1 · Recall · 3 pts]

In Compton scattering, why does the scattered radiation have a longer wavelength? Why is the effect not seen with visible light?

Solution

Solution. Why the wavelength increases. The photon gives part of its energy and momentum to the electron it hits, which recoils. The scattered photon has less energy, therefore a longer wavelength:

λ′−λ=me​ch​(1−cosφ)≥0

Why visible light shows nothing. The shift is at most 2h/me​c=4.85 pm.

  • For visible light (λ≈500 nm) this is a fractional change of about 10−5: unmeasurable.
  • For X-rays (λ≈10 pm) it is about 50%: obvious.

Also the photon energy of visible light (∼2 eV) is comparable to atomic binding energies, so the electrons are not free, unlike for X-rays.

Problem 10 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why can pair production not happen in empty space?

Solution

Solution. Why not in empty space. A photon of energy E has momentum p=E/c (massless).

Suppose it turned into an electron–positron pair with the same total energy E. The pair has rest mass, so its total momentum is less than E/c:

ppair​c=E2−(2me​c2)2​<E

Energy and momentum cannot both be conserved.

With a nucleus. A heavy nearby nucleus absorbs the extra momentum while taking almost no kinetic energy (p2/2M is tiny), so the process is allowed above the threshold hν=2me​c2=1.02 MeV.

Problem 11 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is gravitational red shift? Why does it suggest a photon has gravitational mass?

Solution

Solution. Gravitational red shift. Light climbing out of a gravitational field has its frequency lowered (it is shifted toward the red), even though its speed stays c.

Why this suggests the photon has gravitational mass. To climb a height H, a mass m loses energy mgH. If the photon behaves the same way, with an effective mass m=hν/c2 (from E=mc2):

Δ(hν)=−c2hν​gH ⇒ νΔν​=−c2gH​

Experiment. Pound and Rebka (1960) measured this shift over a 22.5 m tower, about 2.5×10−15, in agreement.

Problem 12 [QM 1 · Recall · 2 pts]

Show that a photon of wavelength λ has momentum h/λ.

Solution

Solution. Starting point. For a massless particle the relativistic relation reduces to

E2=p2c2+m2c4  m=0 ​ E=pc

Photon. E=hν=hc/λ. Then

pc=λhc​ ⇒ p=λh​​

Check. Same as de Broglie's λ=h/p, which therefore applies to both light and matter.

B. Derive or show

Problem 13 [QM 1 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Starting from u(ν) = (8πhν³/c³)/(e^(hν/kT) − 1), show that it reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law for hν ≪ kT. What happens for hν ≫ kT?

Solution

Solution. Planck's law:

u(ν)=c38πhν3​ehν/kT−11​

Low frequency (x=hν/kT≪1). Expand ex−1≈x:

u(ν)≈c38πhν3​⋅hνkT​=c38πν2kT​​

the Rayleigh–Jeans law (classical equipartition).

High frequency (x≫1). ex−1≈ex:

u(ν)≈c38πhν3​e−hν/kT

Wien's form: the exponential kills the growth, so u→0 as ν→∞. This avoids the "ultraviolet catastrophe" of Rayleigh–Jeans, which grows as ν2 without limit.

Problem 14 [QM 1 · Derivation · 5 pts]

Derive the Compton shift formula λ′ − λ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cosφ). State every conservation law you use.

Solution

Solution. Setup. A photon (wavelength λ) hits an electron at rest. The photon scatters at angle φ with λ′; the electron recoils with momentum p and energy Ee​.

Conservation laws used: energy, and momentum (both components).

Energy: λhc​+mc2=λ′hc​+Ee​

Momentum (vector): pγ​=pγ′​+pe​, so squaring,

pe2​c2=(hν)2+(hν′)2−2h2νν′cosφ

Eliminate the electron. Ee​=hν−hν′+mc2, and Ee2​=pe2​c2+m2c4:

(hν−hν′)2+2mc2h(ν−ν′)+m2c4=(hν)2+(hν′)2−2h2νν′cosφ+m2c4

The ν2 and ν′2 terms cancel:

2mc2h(ν−ν′)=2h2νν′(1−cosφ) ⇒ νν′ν−ν′​=mc2h​(1−cosφ)

Multiply by c and use λ=c/ν:

λ′−λ=mch​(1−cosφ)​

Check. φ=0 gives no shift; the maximum shift 2h/mc=4.85 pm is at φ=180∘.

Problem 15 [QM 1 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Using conservation of energy and momentum, show that a free electron cannot absorb a photon completely.

Solution

Solution. Assume a free electron at rest absorbs a photon completely, becoming a single electron of energy Ee​ and momentum pe​.

Conservation.

  • Energy: hν+mc2=Ee​
  • Momentum: pe​=hν/c

Insert into Ee2​=pe2​c2+m2c4:

(hν+mc2)2=(hν)2+m2c4 ⇒ 2hνmc2=0

so ν=0: no photon. A free electron can only scatter a photon (Compton), never absorb it.

Photoelectric effect. The electron is bound: the lattice (a heavy partner) absorbs the extra momentum, so both conservation laws can be met.

Problem 16 [QM 1 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Show that the minimum photon energy for pair production is 2mₑc² when a heavy nucleus is present, and explain why the nucleus takes almost no energy.

Solution

Solution. Energy balance.

hν=2mc2+Ke+​+Ke−​+Knucleus​

Momentum. The nucleus (mass M) takes up the momentum p≤hν/c, with kinetic energy

Knucleus​=2Mp2​≤2Mc2(hν)2​

For hν≈1 MeV and a nucleus with Mc2∼105 MeV this is about 5 eV, utterly negligible. Hence the threshold is

hνmin​=2me​c2=1.02 MeV​

Why a nucleus is needed. In free space the photon has E=pc, but a pair at rest has E=2mc2 and p=0; no pair state has E=pc, so momentum and energy cannot both be conserved. The nucleus provides the missing momentum.

Problem 17 [QM 1 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Derive Bragg's condition 2d sinθ = nλ from the path difference between rays reflected from two adjacent planes.

Solution

Solution. Setup. Two adjacent planes are a distance d apart. A ray strikes at glancing angle θ (to the plane).

Path difference. The lower ray travels extra distance dsinθ on the way in and dsinθ on the way out:

Δ=2dsinθ

Constructive interference needs Δ to be a whole number of wavelengths:

2dsinθ=nλ,n=1,2,3,…​

Common mistake. θ is measured from the plane, not from the normal.

Problem 18 [QM 1 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that the stopping potential is a linear function of frequency. What are its slope and intercept?

Solution

Solution. Einstein's photoelectric equation:

hν=ϕ+Kmax​,Kmax​=eV0​
V0​=eh​ν−eϕ​

A straight line in V0​ against ν:

  • Slope =h/e: the same for every metal (that is how Millikan measured h).
  • ν-intercept =ν0​=ϕ/h (threshold frequency).
  • V-intercept =−ϕ/e.

Common mistake. The slope does not depend on the metal; only the intercepts do.

C. Numericals without a calculator

Problem 19 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

Light of wavelength 310 nm falls on a metal with work function 2.0 eV. Find KE_max of the photoelectrons and the stopping potential.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Photoelectric equation: hν=KEmax​+ϕ, and eV0​=KEmax​. Use hc=1240 eV nm.

Ephoton​=λhc​=3101240​=4.0 eV
KEmax​=4.0−2.0=2.0 eV​,V0​=eKEmax​​=2.0 V​

Problem 20 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

The threshold wavelength for a metal is 620 nm. Find its work function in eV.

Solution

Solution. Idea. At threshold the photon energy just equals the work function: ϕ=hν0​=hc/λ0​.

ϕ=620 nm1240 eV nm​=2.0 eV​

Problem 21 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

An X-ray tube runs at 40 kV. Find the cutoff wavelength.

Solution

Solution. Idea. The most energetic photon carries all the electron's energy eV, so λmin​=hc/eV.

λmin​=40000 eV1240 eV nm​=0.031 nm=31 pm​

Problem 22 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

X-rays of wavelength 0.1 nm reflect from a crystal at a first-order Bragg angle of 30°. Find the plane spacing d.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Bragg: 2dsinθ=nλ with n=1.

d=2sinθnλ​=2(0.5)0.1 nm​=0.1 nm​

Problem 23 [QM 1 · Numerical · 4 pts]

X-rays of wavelength 10.0 pm are Compton-scattered at 90°. Find the scattered wavelength and the kinetic energy given to the electron (in keV).

Solution

Solution. Idea. Compton shift Δλ=λC​(1−cosϕ) with λC​=2.43 pm. The electron's kinetic energy is the energy the photon loses.

Δλ=2.43(1−cos90∘)=2.43 pm ⇒ λ′=10.0+2.43=12.43 pm​
E=0.01001240​=124 keV,E′=0.012431240​≈100 keV
KEe​=E−E′≈24 keV​

Problem 24 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

What is the maximum Compton shift for scattering off an electron? Off a proton (m_p ≈ 1836 mₑ)?

Solution

Solution. Idea. The maximum shift is at ϕ=180∘: Δλmax​=2λC​=2h/mc, which is inversely proportional to the target mass.

Electron: 2λC​=4.85 pm​.

Proton: λC​=2.43 pm/1836=1.3 fm, so Δλmax​≈2.6 fm​, far too small to measure. That is why Compton scattering is seen from electrons, not nuclei.

Problem 25 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

The Sun's surface is at about 5800 K. Estimate the wavelength of peak emission. What colour is that?

Solution

Solution. Idea. Wien's law: λmax​T=2.898×10−3 m K.

λmax​=58002.9×10−3​≈5×10−7 m=500 nm​

That is blue-green (the peak of the visible band). The Sun looks white because it emits strongly across all visible wavelengths.

Problem 26 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

A 1.5 MeV gamma ray makes an electron–positron pair. What is their total kinetic energy?

Solution

Solution. Idea. Pair production needs the rest energy of two electron masses, 2me​c2=1.02 MeV. The rest is kinetic energy.

KEtotal​=1.5−1.02=0.48 MeV​

shared between the electron and the positron.

Problem 27 [QM 1 · Numerical · 3 pts]

Estimate how many photons per second a 1 mW red laser pointer (650 nm) emits.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Power = (energy per photon) × (photons per second).

Eγ​=6501240​=1.9 eV=1.9×1.6×10−19≈3×10−19 J
N=Eγ​P​=3×10−1910−3​≈3×1015 photons per second​

D. Estimate and discuss

Problem 28 [QM 1 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Estimate the number of visible photons per second reaching your eye from a 100 W bulb 10 m away. State your assumptions.

Solution

Solution. Assumptions. 5% of the 100 W is emitted as visible light, so Pvis​=5 W; average photon energy about 2.5 eV =4×10−19 J; pupil diameter 4 mm.

Photons emitted.

N˙=4×10−195​≈1019 s−1

Fraction reaching the eye. Spread over a sphere of radius 10 m, area 4πr2=1.3×103 m2. The pupil area is π(2×10−3)2=1.3×10−5 m2:

fraction≈1.3×1031.3×10−5​=10−8
N˙eye​≈1019×10−8=1011 photons/s​

Comment. Light is "continuous" to the eye because so many photons arrive.

Problem 29 [QM 1 · Discussion · 3 pts]

A detector shows electrons leaving a metal within 10⁻⁹ s of the light being switched on. Estimate how long the wave theory says it would take at an intensity of 10⁻⁶ W/m², assuming an electron absorbs over an area of one atom (about 10⁻²⁰ m²) and needs 2 eV.

Solution

Solution. Wave-theory estimate. On a classical wave, the energy absorbed by one atom is the intensity times the area it presents:

P=IA=(10−6 W/m2)(10−20 m2)=10−26 W

Time to collect 2 eV =3.2×10−19 J:

t=10−263.2×10−19​≈3×107 s≈1 year

Observation. Electrons emerge within 10−9 s.

Conclusion. Energy cannot arrive smoothly spread out; it must arrive in lumps (photons) that deliver hν to a single electron at once.

Problem 30 [QM 1 · Discussion · 4 pts]

Rank the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering and pair production by the photon energy at which each dominates in lead. Explain the ranking in one line each.

Solution

Solution. Ranking by photon energy in lead.

process dominates reason
Photoelectric below about 0.5 MeV the photon is absorbed whole by a bound electron; the cross-section falls steeply with energy
Compton about 0.5 to 5 MeV hν far exceeds binding energies, so electrons act as free, and the photon is scattered
Pair production above about 5 MeV needs hν>2me​c2=1.02 MeV, and grows with energy and with Z2

Order of increasing energy: photoelectric < Compton < pair production.

Problem 31 [QM 1 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Explain, in your own words, why Planck himself thought quantisation was a mathematical trick, and what convinced physicists that it was real.

Solution

Solution. Planck's view (1900). He introduced E=nhν only as a mathematical device to fit the blackbody curve, and hoped that a better classical theory would later remove it.

What made it real.

  1. Einstein (1905) used light quanta of energy hν to explain the photoelectric effect, including the threshold and the linear Kmax​(ν).
  2. Compton (1923) showed that X-rays scatter from electrons as particles with energy hν and momentum h/λ, which no wave theory can reproduce.

After that, quantisation was accepted as a real property of light, not a trick of the calculation.
