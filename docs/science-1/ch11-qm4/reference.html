Quantum mechanics

39 min read

Beiser ch 5 (with its tunnel-effect appendix) section by section, with each derivation written out, and the slides' own derivations (QM.tex:258 to :443: the momentum operator, the 3-D box, the oscillator series solution, the barrier boundary conditions) in full. 5.1 Quantum mechanics

Classical against quantum. In classical mechanics a particle's future is fixed by its initial position and momentum and the forces on it. In quantum mechanics the uncertainty principle forbids knowing the initial state exactly, so the theory predicts probabilities. Example: Bohr says the hydrogen electron is always 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m from the nucleus; quantum mechanics says that is the most probable distance, and most measurements give something else. Classical mechanics is not a separate theory but an approximation: everyday objects contain so many atoms that departures from average behaviour are unnoticeable.

The wave function. Ψ itself has no physical interpretation, but |Ψ|² at a place and time is proportional to the probability of finding the body there then. Momentum, angular momentum and energy can also be extracted from Ψ. The problem of quantum mechanics is to find Ψ when forces restrict the motion.

Complex Ψ. Wave functions are usually complex. Write Ψ = A + iB with A, B real; the complex conjugate (replace i by −i) is Ψ* = A − iB, and

∣Ψ∣2=Ψ∗Ψ=A2−i2B2=A2+B2≥0

always a positive real number, as a probability density must be.

Normalisation. The particle exists somewhere, so ∫|Ψ|² dV must be finite: if it were 0 there would be no particle, and ∞ means nothing. Choosing the constant in Ψ so that |Ψ|² equals the probability density P:

∫−∞∞​∣Ψ∣2dV=1(5.1)

Every acceptable Ψ can be normalised by multiplying by a constant.

Well-behaved wave functions. Only these give physically meaningful results:

  1. Ψ must be continuous and single-valued everywhere (P has one value at each place and time).
  2. ∂Ψ/∂x, ∂Ψ/∂y, ∂Ψ/∂z must be continuous and single-valued everywhere (needed for momentum, 5.6).
  3. Ψ must be normalisable, so Ψ → 0 as x, y, z → ±∞.

Model problems can break rule 2: a box with infinitely hard walls has a kink in ψ at the walls. Real walls are never infinitely hard, and there the derivative is continuous (compare the finite well, 5.9).

Probability in a region. For motion along x:

Px1​x2​​=∫x1​x2​​∣Ψ∣2dx(5.2)

The slides' postulates (QM.tex:258). (1) The state of a system is a wave function Ψ(x, t), possibly complex. (2) It evolves by Schrödinger's equation iℏ∂Ψ/∂t=H^Ψ. (3) Born: |Ψ(x, t)|² dx is the probability of finding the system at x at time t. (4) Every observable has an operator, and its expectation value is ⟨A⟩=∫Ψ∗A^Ψdx. (5) A measurement gives one result and "collapses" the wave function onto the state with that result.

5.2 The wave equation

Schrödinger's equation plays the role in quantum mechanics that F = ma plays in Newtonian mechanics, and it is a wave equation in Ψ. First recall the classical wave equation for a quantity y moving in x at speed v:

∂x2∂2y​=v21​∂t2∂2y​(5.3)

It follows from Newton's second law for mechanical waves and from Maxwell's equations for em waves.

Partial derivatives (box). ∂f/∂x means differentiate with respect to x holding every other variable fixed. For f = yx²: ∂f/∂x = 2yx, ∂f/∂y = x², ∂²f/∂x² = 2y, ∂²f/∂y² = 0.

Solutions. Every solution has the form F(t±x/v) for any differentiable F: t−x/v moves in +x, t+x/v in −x. Pulses, wave trains, superpositions and standing waves are all special cases. For a free, undamped (constant A), monochromatic (constant ω) wave in +x:

y=Ae−iω(t−x/v)=Acosω(t−vx​)−iAsinω(t−vx​)(5.5, 5.6)

using e−iθ=cosθ−isinθ. For a string only the real part, the familiar cosine, means anything.

Example 5.1 (Beiser): check that (5.5) solves (5.3). ∂y/∂x=(iω/v)y, so ∂2y/∂x2=(iω/v)2y=−(ω2/v2)y. ∂y/∂t=−iωy, so ∂2y/∂t2=−ω2y. Then ∂2y/∂x2=(1/v2)∂2y/∂t2. ✓

5.3 Schrödinger's equation: time-dependent form

The free-particle wave. Ψ is not measurable, so it may be complex. Take the free wave (5.5), and use ω = 2πν and v = νλ:

Ψ=Ae−iω(t−x/v)=Ae−2πi(νt−x/λ)

With E=hν=2πℏν and λ=h/p=2πℏ/p, 2πν=E/ℏ and 2π/λ=p/ℏ:

Ψ=Ae−(i/ℏ)(Et−px)(5.9)

the wave of a free particle with energy E and momentum p moving in +x.

Differentiate. Twice in x, once in t:

∂x2∂2Ψ​=−ℏ2p2​Ψ⇒p2Ψ=−ℏ2∂x2∂2Ψ​(5.10)
∂t∂Ψ​=−ℏiE​Ψ⇒EΨ=−iℏ​∂t∂Ψ​=iℏ∂t∂Ψ​(5.11)

Use the energy. At speeds well below c, the total energy is kinetic plus potential, with U(x, t) standing for the influence of the rest of the universe:

E=2mp2​+U(x,t)⇒EΨ=2mp2Ψ​+UΨ(5.12, 5.13)

Substitute (5.10) and (5.11):

iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2Ψ​+UΨ(5.14)

and in three dimensions

iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=−2mℏ2​(∂x2∂2Ψ​+∂y2∂2Ψ​+∂z2∂2Ψ​)+UΨ(5.15)

Is it valid? The route used a free particle (constant U) and then assumed the result for any U(x, y, z, t). That leap has no formal justification, nor does any other route, including Schrödinger's own. So:

Schrödinger's equation cannot be derived from other basic principles of physics; it is a basic principle in itself.

It is postulated, solved, and checked against experiment, and it agrees remarkably well (for non-relativistic problems; near c a more elaborate theory is needed). It does not add a principle: F = ma follows from it if the quantities are read as averages. Newton's laws weren't derived from anything either.

The slides' first motivating route (QM.tex:269): straight to the steady-state equation.

  1. A wave of wave number k obeys ∇2ψ+k2ψ=0 (the spatial part of the wave equation).
  2. De Broglie: k=2π/λ=p/ℏ, so ∇2ψ+(p/ℏ)2ψ=0, solved by ψ=eipx/ℏ.
  3. Free particle: E=p2/2m, and from step 2, p2ψ=−ℏ2∇2ψ, so −2mℏ2​∇2ψ=Eψ.
  4. In a potential, E=p2/2m+V(x), giving the time-independent Schrödinger equation
(−2mℏ2​∇2+V(x))ψ(x)=Eψ(x)

The slides' second route (QM.tex:283) is the one above: Ψ=ei(kx−ωt) with k=p/ℏ and ω=E/ℏ (the slide's "ω = ν/2π" should read ω = 2πν), so Ψ=e(i/ℏ)(px−Et); then ∂t​Ψ=−(i/ℏ)EΨ and ∂x2​Ψ=−(p2/ℏ2)Ψ; with E=p2/2m+V this gives (5.14).

Features. First order in time, so Ψ at one instant fixes Ψ at all later times. Linear in Ψ (5.4). The i on the left forces Ψ to be complex.

5.4 Linearity and superposition

Linear means every term contains Ψ or a derivative of Ψ to the first power, with no term free of Ψ and no Ψ², (∂Ψ/∂x)², and so on. So if Ψ₁ and Ψ₂ are solutions, so is

Ψ=a1​Ψ1​+a2​Ψ2​

(apply the equation term by term). Wave functions obey the superposition principle like other waves, so they interfere. Sections 3.4 and 3.7 already assumed this.

Electrons through two slits (Fig. 5.2). Slit 1 alone gives P1​=∣Ψ1​∣2; slit 2 alone gives P2​=∣Ψ2​∣2. With both open you might expect P1​+P2​, but wave functions add, not probabilities:

P=∣Ψ1​+Ψ2​∣2=(Ψ1∗​+Ψ2∗​)(Ψ1​+Ψ2​)=P1​+P2​+Ψ1∗​Ψ2​+Ψ2∗​Ψ1​

The last two terms, together 2Re(Ψ1∗​Ψ2​), are the interference terms; they make the alternating bright and dark bands, as for light through a double slit. The same algebra explains radiation from atoms (6.8).

Normalising a superposition (slides QM.tex:297). If Ψ₁ and Ψ₂ are normalised and orthogonal (∫Ψ₁*Ψ₂ dx = 0, true for eigenstates of different energy), then a₁Ψ₁ + a₂Ψ₂ is normalised when ∣a1​∣2+∣a2​∣2=1. The cross terms integrate to zero.

5.5 Expectation values

Definition. ⟨x⟩ is the average position you would get by measuring x on a great many particles with the same Ψ at the same instant.

Derivation. For N₁ particles at x₁, N₂ at x₂, and so on, the average position is the centre of mass:

xˉ=N1​+N2​+⋯N1​x1​+N2​x2​+⋯​=∑Ni​∑Ni​xi​​(5.16)

For one particle replace N_i by the probability of finding it in dx at x_i, Pi​=∣Ψi​∣2dx (5.17), and turn sums into integrals:

⟨x⟩=∫−∞∞​∣Ψ∣2dx∫−∞∞​x∣Ψ∣2dx​normalised​⟨x⟩=∫−∞∞​x∣Ψ∣2dx(5.18, 5.19)

Any function of position, such as the potential energy U(x), works the same way:

⟨G(x)⟩=∫−∞∞​G(x)∣Ψ∣2dx(5.20)

Example 5.2 (Beiser). ψ = ax for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, zero elsewhere. (a) P(0.45≤x≤0.55)=a2∫0.450.55​x2dx=a2[x3/3]0.450.55​=0.0251a2. (b) ⟨x⟩=a2∫01​x3dx=a2/4. (Normalising, a2∫01​x2dx=a2/3=1, so a² = 3, giving P = 0.0753 and ⟨x⟩ = 3/4.)

Why momentum and energy need something else. ⟨p⟩ can't be ∫p(x)|Ψ|²dx, because no function p(x) can exist: if x is specified exactly (Δx = 0), Δp ≥ ħ/2Δx forbids a definite p. Likewise ΔEΔt ≥ ħ/2 forbids E(t). Classically, solving the equations gives x(t), p(x, t) and E(x, t), the whole future; in quantum mechanics you get only Ψ, and the future is probabilities. Operators (5.6) give ⟨p⟩ and ⟨E⟩.

5.6 Operators

The hint. Differentiate the free-particle wave (5.9):

∂x∂Ψ​=ℏi​pΨ,∂t∂Ψ​=−ℏi​EΨ⇒pΨ=iℏ​∂x∂Ψ​,EΨ=iℏ∂t∂Ψ​(5.21, 5.22)

So momentum corresponds to the operator (ħ/i)∂/∂x and energy to iħ∂/∂t. An operator tells you what to do to whatever follows it.

p^​=iℏ​∂x∂​=−iℏ∂x∂​,E^=iℏ∂t∂​(5.23, 5.24)

(Eq. 5.22 was on the postmark of the Austrian stamp for Schrödinger's 100th birthday.)

They are general, not just for free particles. Write E = KE + U as an operator equation, E^=KE+U^, with U^=U. Since KE = p²/2m,

KE=2mp^​2​=2m1​(iℏ​∂x∂​)2=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2​(5.26)

so E^=KE+U^ reads iℏ∂/∂t=−(ℏ2/2m)∂2/∂x2+U. Acting on Ψ gives Schrödinger's equation (5.14). Postulating the operators is equivalent to postulating Schrödinger's equation. In 3-D (slides) KE=−2mℏ2​(∂x2​+∂y2​+∂z2​).

Expectation values with operators.

⟨p⟩=∫−∞∞​Ψ∗p^​Ψdx=iℏ​∫−∞∞​Ψ∗∂x∂Ψ​dx,⟨E⟩=∫−∞∞​Ψ∗E^Ψdx=iℏ∫−∞∞​Ψ∗∂t∂Ψ​dx(5.28, 5.29)

Why the order Ψ (operator) Ψ.* The alternatives fail. ∫p^​(Ψ∗Ψ)dx=(ℏ/i)[Ψ∗Ψ]−∞∞​=0 always, since Ψ → 0 at ±∞. And ∫Ψ∗Ψp^​dx leaves the operator with nothing to act on. For x or U(x) the order doesn't matter; for differential operators it does.

General rule. Express the observable G in terms of x and p, replace p by (ħ/i)∂/∂x, and

⟨G(x,p)⟩=∫−∞∞​Ψ∗G^Ψdx(5.30)

All the information the uncertainty principle permits is in Ψ. Slides: all operators can be built from position and momentum operators, K^(r^,p^​).

The slides' derivation of the momentum operator (QM.tex:309)

Classically p = m dx/dt. Ask what ⟨p⟩ = m d⟨x⟩/dt is in terms of Ψ. Write C = ħ²/2m and subscripts for partial derivatives.

Step 1. Schrödinger's equation and its conjugate.

iℏΨt​=−CΨxx​+VΨ,−iℏΨt∗​=−CΨxx∗​+VΨ∗

Step 2. Differentiate ⟨x⟩. x is just an integration variable, so

dtd⟨x⟩​=∫x(Ψ∗Ψt​+Ψt∗​Ψ)dx

From step 1, Ψt​=(1/iℏ)(−CΨxx​+VΨ) and Ψt∗​=−(1/iℏ)(−CΨxx∗​+VΨ∗). The V terms cancel:

Ψ∗Ψt​+Ψt∗​Ψ=−iℏC​(Ψ∗Ψxx​−ΨΨxx∗​)=−iℏC​∂x∂​(Ψ∗Ψx​−ΨΨx∗​)

(check: the derivative of the bracket is Ψx∗​Ψx​+Ψ∗Ψxx​−Ψx​Ψx∗​−ΨΨxx∗​).

Step 3. Integrate by parts ("uv rule"), with the boundary term zero because Ψ → 0 at ±∞:

dtd⟨x⟩​=−iℏC​∫x∂x​(Ψ∗Ψx​−ΨΨx∗​)dx=+iℏC​∫(Ψ∗Ψx​−ΨΨx∗​)dx

Step 4. One more integration by parts gives ∫ΨΨx∗​dx=−∫Ψ∗Ψx​dx, so the two terms are equal:

dtd⟨x⟩​=iℏ2C​∫Ψ∗∂x∂Ψ​dx=imℏ​∫Ψ∗∂x∂Ψ​dx

Step 5. Multiply by m:

⟨p⟩=mdtd⟨x⟩​=∫Ψ∗(iℏ​∂x∂​)Ψdx⇒p^​=iℏ​∂x∂​

(The slide's last line prints h/i; it should be ħ/i.) This also shows the classical relation p = m dx/dt holds for averages, the start of the "F = ma for averages" statement in 5.3.

5.7 Schrödinger's equation: steady-state form

Separating out time. When U depends only on position, the free-particle wave function already shows the pattern:

Ψ=Ae−(i/ℏ)(Et−px)=Ae−(iE/ℏ)te(ip/ℏ)x=ψe−(iE/ℏ)t(5.31)

The time dependence of every wave function with a time-independent U has this same form. Why (slides QM.tex:297): try Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)χ(t) in (5.14) and divide by ψχ:

iℏχ1​dtdχ​=ψ1​(−2mℏ2​dx2d2ψ​+Uψ)

The left side depends only on t, the right only on x, so both equal a constant, which is the energy E. Then χ(t)=χ(0)e−iEt/ℏ, and

−2mℏ2​dx2d2ψ​+Uψ=Eψ⟺dx2d2ψ​+ℏ22m​(E−U)ψ=0(5.32)

In 3-D:

∂x2∂2ψ​+∂y2∂2ψ​+∂z2∂2ψ​+ℏ22m​(E−U)ψ=0(5.33)

Stationary states. ∣Ψ∣2=ψ∗e+iEt/ℏψe−iEt/ℏ=∣ψ∣2: the probability density does not change in time.

Quantisation appears naturally. If the steady-state equation has well-behaved solutions, each belongs to a specific E. Energy quantisation is part of the theory and characteristic of all stable systems. Analogy: a string of length L fixed at both ends must have y = 0 at the ends (a boundary condition), and only wavelengths λn​=2L/(n+1), n = 0, 1, 2, … survive (Fig. 5.3).

Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The energies E_n for which the steady-state equation can be solved are the eigenvalues; the matching ψ_n are the eigenfunctions (German Eigenwert, "proper value"). Hydrogen's En​=−me4/(32π2ε02​ℏ2n2) are eigenvalues (ch 6). Angular momentum is also quantised in stable systems, with eigenvalues L=l(l+1)​ℏ, l = 0, 1, …, n − 1.

A variable that is not quantised (for example the electron's position in hydrogen) gives a spread of results whose average is ⟨G⟩ = ∫G|ψ|²dx. The probability of finding the electron is smeared out; the electron is not. Every measurement finds a whole electron.

Operators and eigenvalues. G is quantised to the values G_n when the wave functions satisfy

G^ψn​=Gn​ψn​(Gn​ real)(5.34)

It is a postulate that a measurement of G can then yield only one of the G_n, and if every system is in the state ψ_k, every measurement gives G_k.

Example 5.3 (Beiser). ψ = e^(2x) is an eigenfunction of d²/dx²: dx2d2​e2x=dxd​2e2x=4e2x=4ψ, eigenvalue 4.

The Hamiltonian. From (5.25) and (5.26),

H^=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2​+U,H^ψn​=En​ψn​(5.35, 5.36)

named after the classical Hamiltonian (total energy in terms of coordinates and momenta; chapter 4, Hamiltonian dynamics). The energy levels are the eigenvalues of Ĥ.

5.8 Particle in a box

Setup. A particle moves along x between infinitely hard walls at x = 0 and x = L. It loses no energy at the walls. Formally U = ∞ outside and U = 0 inside (Fig. 5.4). It can't have infinite energy, so ψ = 0 outside. (Slides QM.tex:323: in regions x<0 and x>L the term Vψ is infinite while Eψ is finite, so the only solution is ψ = 0.)

Inside. With U = 0, (5.32) becomes

dx2d2ψ​+ℏ22mE​ψ=0i.e.dx2d2ψ​=−k2ψ,k=ℏ2mE​​(5.37)

whose solution is

ψ=Asinkx+Bcoskx(5.38)

Boundary conditions. ψ(0) = 0: sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1, so B = 0. ψ(L) = 0: A sin kL = 0 with A ≠ 0, so kL = nπ:

ℏ2mEn​​​L=nπ,n=1,2,3,…(5.39)

(n = 0 gives ψ = 0 everywhere, no particle; negative n gives the same states as positive.)

Energies. Solve (5.39):

En​=2mL2n2π2ℏ2​=8mL2n2h2​,n=1,2,3,…(5.40)

the same as the standing-wave result (3.18), with the same meaning: quantised, no zero energy, noticeable only for small m and L.

Wave functions and normalisation. ψn​=Asin(nπx/L). These are finite, single-valued and continuous, with continuous derivatives except at the walls. Normalise using sin2θ=21​(1−cos2θ):

∫0L​A2sin2Lnπx​dx=2A2​[∫0L​dx−∫0L​cosL2nπx​dx]=2A2​[x−2nπL​sinL2nπx​]0L​=2A2L​(5.43)

Setting this equal to 1:

A=L2​​,ψn​=L2​​sinLnπx​(5.46)

(Slides QM.tex:336: B = √(2/L) in their labelling, and E_n = n²E₁ with E₁ = h²/8mL².)

Probability densities (Fig. 5.5).

  • |ψ_n|² = 0 at both walls in every state.
  • |ψ₁|² is largest, 2/L, at the centre: the ground-state particle is most likely in the middle.
  • |ψ₂|² = 0 at the centre: in the n = 2 state the particle is never there.
  • ψ_n has n − 1 nodes inside the box (slides: "notice the number of nodes").
  • Classically every position is equally likely. At large n the rapid wiggles of |ψ_n|² average to the uniform 1/L (correspondence principle).
  • ψ_n looks like the vibrations of a string fixed at both ends because the two obey equations of the same form with the same restrictions.

Example 5.4 (Beiser). Probability between 0.45L and 0.55L:

P=L2​∫x1​x2​​sin2Lnπx​dx=[Lx​−2nπ1​sinL2nπx​]x1​x2​​

n = 1: P = 0.198 (19.8%), twice the classical 10%. n = 2: P = 0.0065 (0.65%), consistent with the node at the centre.

Example 5.5 (Beiser): ⟨x⟩. Using ∫xsin2axdx=4x2​−4axsin2ax​−8a2cos2ax​ with a = nπ/L:

⟨x⟩=L2​∫0L​xsin2Lnπx​dx=L2​[4x2​−4nπ/Lxsin(2nπx/L)​−8(nπ/L)2cos(2nπx/L)​]0L​=L2​⋅4L2​=2L​

(sin 2nπ = 0, and cos 2nπ = cos 0 = 1, so the cosine terms cancel.) The average position is the middle in every state, even when |ψ|² = 0 there: ⟨x⟩ is an average, set by the symmetry of |ψ|².

Momentum. With dψn​/dx=2/L​(nπ/L)cos(nπx/L) and ∫sinaxcosaxdx=sin2ax/2a:

⟨p⟩=∫0L​ψn​iℏ​dxdψn​​dx=iL22nπℏ​∫0L​sinLnπx​cosLnπx​dx=iLℏ​[sin2Lnπx​]0L​=0

But En​=p2/2m suggests

The ± is the answer: the particle moves back and forth, so the average is 21​(nπℏ/L)+21​(−nπℏ/L)=0.

Momentum eigenfunctions. ψ_n is not one: p^​ψn​=2/L​(ℏ/i)(nπ/L)cos(nπx/L), which is not a number times ψ_n. Use sinθ=(eiθ−e−iθ)/2i to split ψ_n into

Then p^​ψn+​=iℏ​⋅Linπ​ψn+​=+Lnπℏ​ψn+​ and likewise p^​ψn−​=−Lnπℏ​ψn−​. Two momentum eigenfunctions per energy level, one for each direction, with eigenvalues ±nπħ/L, as (5.47) said.

The 3-D box (slides QM.tex:346)

Setup. 0≤x≤Lx​, 0≤y≤Ly​, 0≤z≤Lz​, with V = 0 inside and ∞ outside, so ψ = 0 outside. With C = ħ²/2m, inside:

−C(∂x2∂2​+∂y2∂2​+∂z2∂2​)ψ=Eψ

Separate. Try ψ=ψ1​(x)ψ2​(y)ψ3​(z) and divide by ψ:

ψ1​ψ1′′​​+ψ2​ψ2′′​​+ψ3​ψ3′′​​=−CE​

Each term depends on a different variable, so each is a constant: ψ1′′​=−kx2​ψ1​, and so on, with C(kx2​+ky2​+kz2​)=E. Each is a 1-D box problem, so kx​=n1​π/Lx​, ky​=n2​π/Ly​, kz​=n3​π/Lz​:

ψ=Lx​Ly​Lz​8​​sinLx​n1​πx​sinLy​n2​πy​sinLz​n3​πz​,E=8mh2​(Lx2​n12​​+Ly2​n22​​+Lz2​n32​​)

with each n = 1, 2, 3, …. (The slide writes Ly​ in the last sine; it should be Lz​.) A 3-D problem needs three quantum numbers, one per boundary condition, which is why hydrogen has three (6.1).

Degeneracy in a cube (L_x = L_y = L_z = L). In units of h²/8mL², E = n₁² + n₂² + n₃²:

E (n₁, n₂, n₃) Degeneracy

Different states with the same energy are degenerate; the symmetry of the cube causes it, and stretching one side lifts it.

5.9 Finite potential well

Setup (Fig. 5.7). A well of width L (0 < x < L) with walls of height U, and a particle with E < U. Classically it bounces between the walls and never enters regions I (x < 0) or III (x > L). Quantum mechanically it can.

Outside the well. In I and III, (5.32) with E − U < 0:

dx2d2ψ​−a2ψ=0,a=ℏ2m(U−E)​​(5.53, 5.54)

Solutions are real exponentials, ψI​=Ceax+De−ax and ψIII​=Feax+Ge−ax. Ψ must stay finite: e−ax→∞ as x→−∞ (so D = 0) and eax→∞ as x→+∞ (so F = 0):

ψI​=Ceax,ψIII​=Ge−ax(5.57, 5.58)

The wave function decays exponentially into the walls, with penetration depth about 1/a.

Inside. As in the box, ψII​=Asin(2mE​x/ℏ)+Bcos(2mE​x/ℏ). Now ψ is not zero at the walls (it equals C at x = 0 and Ge^(−aL) at x = L), so both sine and cosine are allowed.

Matching. ψ and dψ/dx must be continuous at x = 0 and x = L: the inside and outside pieces must meet with the same value and the same slope. Only certain energies E_n allow a smooth fit, so the energy is still quantised (Fig. 5.8).

Why the levels are lower. The wave spills into the walls, so it is effectively longer than in an infinite well of the same width. Longer λ means smaller p = h/λ and so a lower energy for each n.

More (beyond Beiser's text). Taking the well centred on the origin, the even states satisfy ktan(kL/2)=a and the odd ones −kcot(kL/2)=a, with k=2mE​/ℏ; these are solved graphically. There is always at least one bound state, and only a finite number; a deeper or wider well holds more. The particle has a real chance of being found inside the walls, where classically KE < 0.

5.10 Tunnel effect

Idea. A barrier of height U and finite width L (Fig. 5.9). A particle with E < U would always bounce back classically. Quantum mechanically ψ decays inside the barrier but is not zero at the far side, so some of the wave gets through: the particle tunnels. The higher and wider the barrier, the smaller the chance.

Slides (QM.tex:390). The oscillator already showed probability beyond the classical turning points, where KE < 0. At each edge of the barrier, ψ and dψ/dx must be continuous.

Approximate transmission probability (derived in the appendix below):

T≈e−2k2​L,k2​=ℏ2m(U−E)​​(5.60, 5.61)

T falls exponentially with the width L and with √(m(U − E)), so a small change in either changes T by a large factor. Reflection R = 1 − T.

Where it matters.

  • Alpha decay: an alpha particle with a few MeV escapes a nucleus whose wall is perhaps 25 MeV high. It might hit the wall 10³⁸ times before getting out, but it does (Gamow, 1928).
  • Tunnel diode: electrons cross barriers higher than their kinetic energy.
  • Scanning tunnelling microscope (box): Binnig and Rohrer (1981; Nobel 1986, shared with Ruska, inventor of the electron microscope). A probe whose tip is a single atom is brought within a nanometre or two of a conducting surface with about 10 mV across the gap. Electrons tunnel even though they need several eV (the work function) to escape. Since T∝e−2k2​L, a gap change of 0.01 nm changes the current measurably. Scanning with a piezoelectric drive while holding the current constant maps the surface; the map is really one of constant electron density, so different elements look different. The atomic force microscope (AFM) feels repulsive forces instead and works on any surface, including biological ones. (The slides' "Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)" on QM.tex:433 should read scanning tunnelling microscope.)

Example 5.6 (Beiser). Electrons of 1.0 eV and 2.0 eV hit a barrier 10.0 eV high and 0.50 nm wide. For 1.0 eV, k2​=2(9.1×10−31)(9.0)(1.6×10−19)​/1.054×10−34=1.6×1010 m⁻¹, so 2k₂L = 16 and T₁ = e⁻¹⁶ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ (one in 8.9 million). For 2.0 eV, T₂ = 2.4 × 10⁻⁷. Doubling the width to 1.0 nm: T₁ = 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ and T₂ = 5.1 × 10⁻¹⁴. T is more sensitive to width than to energy here.

Outside the barrier (regions I and III, U = 0):

What each term means.

5.11 Harmonic oscillator

Classical oscillator. Simple harmonic motion comes from a linear restoring force, Hooke's law F = −kx. With F = ma:

dt2d2x​+mk​x=0,x=Acos(2πνt+ϕ),ν=2π1​mk​​(5.62 to 5.64)

φ is fixed by x and the direction of motion at t = 0.

Why it matters everywhere. Expand any restoring force about the equilibrium x = 0 in a Maclaurin series:

F(x)=Fx=0​+(dxdF​)0​x+21​(dx2d2F​)0​x2+⋯

F = 0 at equilibrium, and for small x the x², x³ terms are negligible, so F≈(dF/dx)0​x, Hooke's law with k=−(dF/dx)0​>0. All oscillations are simple harmonic when small enough: molecules, atoms in crystals, objects on springs.

Potential energy. The work to move from 0 to x against F:

U(x)=−∫0x​Fdx=k∫0x​xdx=21​kx2,E=21​kA2(5.65)

a parabola (Fig. 5.10). Classically E can be anything and the particle swings between −A and +A.

What to expect quantum mechanically. (1) Discrete energies, not a continuum. (2) The lowest energy is not 0 but some E₀. (3) Some probability of being found beyond ±A.

Energy levels

With U = ½kx²:

dx2d2ψ​+ℏ22m​(E−21​kx2)ψ=0(5.66)

Change to dimensionless position and energy:

y=(ℏ1​km​)1/2x=ℏ2πmν​​x,α=ℏ2E​km​​=hν2E​(5.67, 5.68)

so that

dy2d2ψ​+(α−y2)ψ=0(5.69)

ψ must → 0 as y → ±∞ to be normalisable, and the equation allows that only when α = 2n + 1, n = 0, 1, 2, …. With α = 2E/hν:

En​=(n+21​)hν,n=0,1,2,…(5.70)
E0​=21​hν(zero-point energy)(5.71)
  • Quantised in steps of hν, evenly spaced (Planck's assumption in 2.2, but shifted by ½hν).
  • The zero-point energy is the lowest possible; an oscillator in equilibrium with its surroundings approaches E₀, not 0, as T → 0 K. It is what the uncertainty principle demands: at rest at the bottom, x and p would both be exactly 0.
  • Level patterns compared (Fig. 5.11). Hydrogen: E_n ∝ −1/n², crowding together. Box: E_n ∝ n², spreading apart. Oscillator: E_n ∝ n + ½, the only evenly spaced one.

The slides' series solution (QM.tex:353 to :378)

This is how α = 2n + 1 comes out. Write C = ħ²/2m.

Step 1. Rescale. Put x = by in −Cψxx​+21​kx2ψ=Eψ:

−b2C​ψyy​+21​kb2y2ψ=Eψ⇒ψyy​−2Ckb4​y2ψ+CEb2​ψ=0

Choose b so that kb4/2C=1, i.e. b4=ℏ2/mk (Beiser's y), and call α=Eb2/C, which works out to 2E/hν. (The slide drops the factor ½ in the middle term; with it the scaling agrees with Beiser.) Result: ψyy​+(α−y2)ψ=0.

Step 2. Large-y behaviour. For y → ±∞, α is negligible: ψyy​≈y2ψ. Try ψ=eβy2: ψyy​=(4β2y2+2β)ψ≈4β2y2ψ, so 4β2=1 and β = ±½. Only β = −½ is normalisable: ψ∼e−y2/2.

Step 3. Factor it out. Try ψ=f(y)e−y2/2:

ψy​=e−y2/2(f′−yf),ψyy​=e−y2/2(f′′−2yf′−f+y2f)

Then ψyy​+(α−y2)ψ=e−y2/2[f′′−2yf′+(α−1)f]=0, so

f′′−2yf′+(α−1)f=0(Hermite’s equation)

Step 4. Power series. Put f=∑n​an​yn. Then f′′=∑n(n−1)an​yn−2 and 2yf′=∑2nan​yn. Shift the index in f'' (n → n + 2) and collect powers of yⁿ:

(n+2)(n+1)an+2​−2nan​+(α−1)an​=0⇒an+2​=(n+1)(n+2)2n+1−α​an​

Step 5. The series must stop. If it doesn't, for large n an+2​/an​→2/n, the same as the series of ey2, so f∼ey2 and ψ∼e+y2/2 blows up. The only way out is for the series to terminate: 2n+1−α=0 for some whole number n (with the other-parity series set to zero). So α = 2n + 1, which is En​=(n+21​)hν, and fn​ is a polynomial of degree n.

Step 6. The first few.

  • n = 0, α = 1: a₂ = 0, so f₀ = 1 and ψ₀ ∝ e^(−y²/2), a Gaussian.
  • n = 1, α = 3: odd series stops at a₁y, so f₁ ∝ y, conventionally H₁ = 2y.
  • n = 2, α = 5: a2​=21−5​a0​=−2a0​, so f₂ ∝ 1 − 2y², conventionally H₂ = 4y² − 2. (The slide writes α = 4 for n = 2; it should be 5.)

  • Beyond the classical limits (Fig. 5.12). Each ψ_n leaks past ±A with exponentially falling probability, as in the finite well. For n = 0 the classical limit is y = ±1 (α = 1 = y²), and the probability outside is erfc(1) ≈ 16% (the slides, QM.tex:379, quote about 10%). It shrinks as n grows.
