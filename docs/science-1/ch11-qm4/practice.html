Quantum mechanics

18 min read · 29 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

29 questions with worked answers (tap a solution to open it). The style follows your midsem papers and Quiz-1. Points are in brackets. Use hc = 1240 eV nm, ħc = 197 eV nm, mₑc² = 0.511 MeV, h = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV s.

A. State and explain

Problem 1 [QM 4 · Recall · 3 pts]

What conditions must a wave function satisfy to be physically acceptable? Why?

Solution

Solution. Conditions on Ψ.

  1. Continuous and single-valued, so the probability density is unambiguous.
  2. ∂Ψ/∂x continuous wherever U is finite. Otherwise ∂2Ψ/∂x2 would be infinite and the equation could not hold.
  3. Normalisable: ∫∣Ψ∣2dx=1, so Ψ→0 at infinity and the particle is certainly somewhere.

Consequence. These conditions are what restrict the solutions to a discrete set of energies for bound states.

Problem 2 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

Write the time-dependent Schrödinger equation in one dimension and name each term.

Solution

Solution. Time-dependent Schrödinger equation (1-D).

iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2Ψ​+U(x)Ψ

Terms.

  • iℏ∂Ψ/∂t: the energy operator acting on Ψ.
  • −2mℏ2​∂x2∂2Ψ​: kinetic energy, since p^​2/2m with p^​=−iℏ∂/∂x.
  • UΨ: potential energy.

So the equation says: total energy = kinetic + potential.

Problem 3 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why must the wave function be complex?

Solution

Solution. Reason. The Schrödinger equation has a factor i on one side and real terms on the other:

iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=H^Ψ

A real Ψ cannot satisfy it for a travelling particle. A free-particle solution is Ψ=Aei(kx−ωt), which is complex.

What is measurable. Only ∣Ψ∣2=Ψ∗Ψ, which is real and non-negative. The phase of Ψ itself is not observable, but relative phases matter for interference.

Problem 4 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

What does the linearity of the Schrödinger equation imply? Why does it lead to interference?

Solution

Solution. Linearity. If Ψ1​ and Ψ2​ are solutions, so is aΨ1​+bΨ2​: the superposition principle.

Interference. The probability density of a sum is

∣Ψ1​+Ψ2​∣2=∣Ψ1​∣2+∣Ψ2​∣2+2Re(Ψ1∗​Ψ2​)

The last term is the cross term. It can be positive or negative, so probabilities do not simply add: they can reinforce (constructive) or cancel (destructive). This is the origin of double-slit interference for electrons.

Problem 5 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is an expectation value? Write ⟨x⟩ and ⟨p⟩.

Solution

Solution. Expectation value. The average result of measuring a quantity on many identical systems prepared in the same state Ψ.

⟨x⟩=∫−∞∞​x∣Ψ∣2dx
⟨p⟩=∫−∞∞​Ψ∗(−iℏ∂x∂​)Ψdx

In general ⟨Q⟩=∫Ψ∗Q^​Ψdx for any observable with operator Q^​.

Note. It is an average over many runs, not the result of one measurement.

Problem 6 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is a stationary state? Why is its probability density constant in time?

Solution

Solution. Stationary state. A solution of the form

Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)e−iEt/ℏ

with a definite energy E.

Constant probability density.

∣Ψ∣2=∣ψ∣2​e−iEt/ℏ​2=∣ψ(x)∣2

because ∣eiθ∣=1. Nothing observable changes in time, so the state does not radiate, and all expectation values are constant.

Problem 7 [QM 4 · Recall · 3 pts]

Explain how energy quantisation follows from the steady-state Schrödinger equation without any extra postulate.

Solution

Solution. How quantisation arises. The steady-state equation

ψ′′+ℏ22m​(E−U)ψ=0

has solutions for every E, but only certain values of E give a ψ that is continuous, finite, normalisable and satisfies the boundary conditions. Those special values are the allowed energies.

Example (box). ψ=Asinkx with ψ(L)=0 forces kL=nπ, so

En​=2mℏ2k2​=2mL2n2π2ℏ2​

No separate quantum postulate is needed: it follows from the equation and the boundary conditions.

Problem 8 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why are the energy levels of a finite well lower than those of an infinite well of the same width?

Solution

Solution. Reason. In a finite well the wave function leaks into the walls, so the particle is effectively spread over a region wider than L.

wider region ⇒ longer λ ⇒ smaller p=h/λ ⇒ lower E

Limits.

  • Well depth →∞: the leakage vanishes and En​→n2h2/8mL2.
  • A finite well has a finite number of bound states, and fewer when it is shallower or narrower.

Problem 9 [QM 4 · Recall · 3 pts]

What is quantum tunnelling? Give two real examples.

Solution

Solution. Tunnelling. A particle can pass through a potential barrier higher than its energy.

Mechanism. Inside the barrier ψ decays exponentially, ψ∝e−k2​x, but it is not zero at the far side, so there is a non-zero probability of finding the particle there:

T≈e−2k2​L,k2​=ℏ2m(U−E)​​

Real examples.

  • Alpha decay: α particles escape the Coulomb barrier of the nucleus.
  • Scanning tunnelling microscope: current tunnels between tip and surface.
  • Tunnel diode and flash memory.
  • Fusion in the Sun: protons tunnel through their mutual repulsion.

Problem 10 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is zero-point energy? Why is it required by the uncertainty principle?

Solution

Solution. Zero-point energy. The lowest energy of a bound system lies above the bottom of its potential well.

  • Box: E1​=h2/8mL2>0.
  • Oscillator: E0​=21​hν.

Why the uncertainty principle requires it. A particle at rest at the bottom would have both Δx=0 and Δp=0, so ΔxΔp=0<ℏ/2. Confining the particle to a region Δx forces Δp≳ℏ/2Δx, and hence a kinetic energy of order (Δp)2/2m.

Consequence. Helium stays liquid at absolute zero at ordinary pressure.

Problem 11 [QM 4 · Recall · 3 pts]

Compare the probability distribution of a quantum harmonic oscillator in its ground state with that of a classical oscillator of the same energy.

Solution

Solution. Quantum ground state (n=0). ∣ψ0​∣2∝e−mωx2/ℏ: a Gaussian peaked at the centre, with a small probability of being found beyond the classical turning points (tunnelling into the forbidden region).

Classical oscillator of the same energy. It moves fastest at the centre, so it spends the least time there: the probability density is smallest at the centre, largest at the turning points ±A, and zero beyond them.

Large n. The quantum density oscillates rapidly, but its average follows the classical shape (peaks near the turning points). This is the correspondence principle.

Problem 12 [QM 4 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is degeneracy? Give an example from the 3-D box.

Solution

Solution. Degeneracy. Different states with the same energy. The number of such states is the degree of degeneracy.

Cubical box. E∝n12​+n22​+n32​. The states

(1,1,2), (1,2,1), (2,1,1)

all have n12​+n22​+n32​=6, so the energy level 6E0​ is 3-fold degenerate.

Cause. Degeneracy reflects symmetry (here, the cube's); changing one side length breaks the symmetry and splits the level.

B. Derive or show

Problem 13 [QM 4 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Starting from a free-particle wave Ψ = Ae^(−(i/ħ)(Et − px)) and E = p²/2m + U, obtain the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

Solution

Solution. Free-particle wave. Ψ=Ae−(i/ℏ)(Et−px).

Differentiate.

∂t∂Ψ​=−ℏiE​Ψ ⇒ EΨ=iℏ∂t∂Ψ​
∂x2∂2Ψ​=−ℏ2p2​Ψ ⇒ p2Ψ=−ℏ2∂x2∂2Ψ​

Energy relation. Multiply E=2mp2​+U by Ψ and substitute:

iℏ∂t∂Ψ​=−2mℏ2​∂x2∂2Ψ​+UΨ​

The equation is then postulated to hold for any U(x,t). It is linear in Ψ, which allows superposition.

Problem 14 [QM 4 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Separate variables to obtain the steady-state Schrödinger equation from the time-dependent one when U = U(x).

Solution

Solution. Separate variables (valid when U=U(x)): Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)f(t).

iℏψf′=−2mℏ2​ψ′′f+Uψf

Divide by ψf:

iℏff′​=ψ1​[−2mℏ2​ψ′′+Uψ]

The left side depends only on t and the right only on x, so both equal a constant E.

Time part: f=e−iEt/ℏ.

Space part:

−2mℏ2​ψ′′+Uψ=Eψ⟺ψ′′+ℏ22m​(E−U)ψ=0​

Problem 15 [QM 4 · Derivation · 6 pts] (hard)

Solve the Schrödinger equation for a particle in an infinite box of width L: find Eₙ, the normalised ψₙ, and sketch |ψₙ|² for n = 1, 2, 3.

Solution

Solution. Inside the box (U=0): ψ′′=−k2ψ with k=2mE​/ℏ, so

ψ=Asinkx+Bcoskx

Boundary conditions. ψ(0)=0⇒B=0. ψ(L)=0⇒sinkL=0⇒kL=nπ:

En​=2mL2n2π2ℏ2​=8mL2n2h2​​

Normalise.

∫0L​A2sin2Lnπx​dx=A22L​=1 ⇒ ψn​=L2​​sinLnπx​​

∣ψn​∣2: n=1 has one hump (maximum at L/2); n=2 two humps with a zero at L/2; n=3 three humps with zeros at L/3 and 2L/3.

Common mistake. n=0 is not allowed (ψ≡0), so E1​>0 is the zero-point energy.

Problem 16 [QM 4 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that ⟨x⟩ = L/2 for every state of the infinite box.

Solution

Solution. Symmetry. ∣ψn​(x)∣2=L2​sin2Lnπx​. Replace x→L−x:

sin2Lnπ(L−x)​=sin2(nπ−Lnπx​)=sin2Lnπx​

so ∣ψn​∣2 is symmetric about x=L/2.

Mean.

⟨x−L/2⟩=∫(x−2L​)∣ψn​∣2dx=0

(odd integrand about L/2), so ⟨x⟩=L/2​ for every n.

Problem 17 [QM 4 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that e^(ikx) is an eigenfunction of the momentum operator, and that sin kx is not. What does this say about the momentum of a particle in a box?

Solution

Solution. Momentum operator p^​=−iℏd/dx.

p^​eikx=−iℏ(ik)eikx=ℏkeikx

an eigenfunction with eigenvalue p=ℏk.

p^​sinkx=−iℏkcoskx=(number)sinkx

so sinkx is not an eigenfunction.

Meaning for the box. sinkx=2ieikx−e−ikx​ is a superposition of p=+ℏk and p=−ℏk, with equal weights. A measurement gives ±ℏk with probability 21​ each, so ⟨p⟩=0 but p is not definite. (This is the standing-wave picture: a particle bouncing back and forth.)

Problem 18 [QM 4 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Normalise ψ(x) = Ax(L − x) for 0 ≤ x ≤ L (zero outside).

Solution

Solution. Condition. ∫0L​∣ψ∣2dx=1:

A2∫0L​x2(L−x)2dx=1

Integral. ∫0L​(L2x2−2Lx3+x4)dx=3L5​−2L5​+5L5​=30L5​.

A230L5​=1 ⇒ A=L530​​​

Problem 19 [QM 4 · Derivation · 4 pts]

For the finite well, write the form of ψ inside and outside, and state the conditions used to join them.

Solution

Solution. Finite well of depth U and width L, with E<U.

Inside (U=0): ψ′′=−k2ψ, k=2mE​/ℏ:

ψ=Asinkx+Bcoskx

Outside (U>E): ψ′′=a2ψ, a=2m(U−E)​/ℏ:

ψleft​=Ceax,ψright​=De−ax

The growing exponentials are dropped so that ψ stays finite as ∣x∣→∞.

Matching conditions. At each wall, ψ and dψ/dx are continuous (the potential is finite, so ψ′′ is finite). Together with normalisation these fix the allowed energies, which come out as a finite number of discrete levels. The wave function leaks into the walls over a distance 1/a.

Problem 20 [QM 4 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Write the energy levels of a particle in a cubical 3-D box. Find the first three energy levels and their degeneracies.

Solution

Solution. Separable problem. ψ=ψn1​​(x)ψn2​​(y)ψn3​​(z) and the energies add:

E=2mL2π2ℏ2​(n12​+n22​+n32​)≡E0​(n12​+n22​+n32​)
level (n1​,n2​,n3​) energy degeneracy
1st (1,1,1) 3E0​ 1
2nd (1,1,2) and permutations 6E0​ 3
3rd (1,2,2) and permutations 9E0​ 3

Check. (2,2,2) gives 12E0​ and (1,1,3) gives 11E0​, so nothing else fits below 9E0​. The degeneracy arises from the cube's symmetry.

C. Numericals without a calculator

Problem 21 [QM 4 · Numerical]

Find the ground-state energy of an electron in a box 1 nm wide.

Solution

Solution. Idea. E1​=h2/8mL2. Write it as (hc)2/(8me​c2L2) so everything is in eV and nm.

E1​=8(0.511×106 eV)(1 nm)2(1240 eV nm)2​=4.09×1061.54×106​≈0.38 eV​

Problem 22 [QM 4 · Numerical]

For a particle in a box, find the probability of finding it in the middle third of the box for n = 1 and n = 2. Compare with the classical answer.

Solution

Solution. Idea. With ψn​=2/L​sin(nπx/L),

P=∫L/32L/3​L2​sin2Lnπx​dx=31​−2nπ1​[sin34nπ​−sin32nπ​]
n=1:31​+2π3​​≈0.33+0.28=0.61​
n=2:31​−4π3​​≈0.33−0.14=0.20​

Compare. A classical particle is equally likely anywhere, so P=1/3 for every state. The quantum answer oscillates about 1/3 and approaches it as n→∞ (the correspondence principle).

Problem 23 [QM 4 · Numerical]

An electron with E = 1 eV meets a barrier 5 eV high and 0.5 nm wide. Estimate T. What happens to T if the width doubles?

Solution

Solution. Idea. T≈e−2k2​L with k2​=2m(U−E)​/ℏ.

k2​=ℏc2(me​c2)(U−E)​​=197 eV nm2(0.511×106)(4)​​≈1972020​≈10 nm−1
2k2​L=2(10)(0.5)≈10 ⇒ T≈e−10≈5×10−5​

Doubling the width: the exponent doubles, so T≈e−20≈2×10−9. T is squared, not halved.

Problem 24 [QM 4 · Numerical]

Repeat question 23 for a proton. Comment.

Solution

Solution. Idea. k2​∝m​.

The proton is 1836 times heavier, so k2​ is 1836​≈43 times larger and

2k2​L≈10×43≈430 ⇒ T≈e−430≈0 (effectively zero)

Comment. Tunnelling matters for light particles and thin barriers. The mass dependence enters through the exponent, so the effect disappears very quickly as the mass grows.

Problem 25 [QM 4 · Numerical]

A molecule vibrates at 10¹⁴ Hz. Find its zero-point energy and the spacing between levels, in eV.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Oscillator levels En​=(n+21​)hν: spacing hν, zero-point energy 21​hν.

hν=(4.14×10−15 eV s)(1014 s−1)≈0.41 eV
spacing=0.41 eV​,E0​=21​hν≈0.21 eV​

Problem 26 [QM 4 · Numerical]

Find the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron in a 1 nm box drops from n = 3 to n = 1.

Solution

Solution. Idea. En​=n2E1​, so E3​−E1​=(9−1)E1​=8E1​.

ΔE=8(0.38 eV)≈3.0 eV ⇒ λ=3.01240​≈410 nm​(violet)

D. Estimate and discuss

Problem 27 [QM 4 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Explain why a scanning tunnelling microscope can sense height changes of about 0.01 nm.

Solution

Solution. Tunnelling current. Electrons tunnel across the vacuum gap L between the tip and the surface, with

T≈e−2k2​L,k2​=ℏ2mϕ​​≈10 nm−1  (ϕ∼a few eV)

Sensitivity. A change δL=0.01 nm changes the exponent by

2k2​δL=2(10)(0.01)=0.2 ⇒ IδI​≈e0.2−1≈20%

A 20% current change is easy to measure, so heights of 0.01 nm (a hundredth of an atom's size) are detected.

Problem 28 [QM 4 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Alpha particles of about 5 MeV escape from nuclei where the Coulomb barrier is about 30 MeV. Explain how, and why half-lives vary from microseconds to billions of years.

Solution

Solution. Mechanism. An α particle with E≈5 MeV sits inside a Coulomb barrier about 30 MeV high. Classically it cannot escape; quantum mechanically it tunnels through.

Why half-lives vary so much. The decay rate is proportional to the transmission probability T≈e−2k2​L, which depends exponentially on the barrier width and on U−E​. A small change in the α energy changes both strongly.

An energy change from about 4 MeV to 9 MeV changes the half-life by more than 20 orders of magnitude, from billions of years to microseconds. This is the Geiger–Nuttall law.

Problem 29 [QM 4 · Discussion · 2 pts]

Explain why the harmonic oscillator is useful for any system near a stable equilibrium.

Solution

Solution. Taylor expansion about a stable minimum x0​. Since U′(x0​)=0:

U(x)≈U(x0​)+21​U′′(x0​)(x−x0​)2

This is a spring with k=U′′(x0​)>0, so small oscillations have

ω=mU′′(x0​)​​,En​=(n+21​)ℏω

Examples. Molecular vibrations (the ∼1014 Hz absorption in infrared), atoms in crystals (specific heat of solids), and the electromagnetic field modes of a cavity.
