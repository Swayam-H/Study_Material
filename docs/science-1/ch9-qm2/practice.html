Wave properties of particles

17 min read · 27 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

27 questions with worked answers (tap a solution to open it). The style follows your midsem papers and Quiz-1: short state/explain items, derivations, and no-calculator numericals and estimates. Points are in brackets. Use hc = 1240 eV nm, ħc = 197 eV nm, mₑc² = 0.511 MeV, m_pc² ≈ m_nc² ≈ 938 MeV.

A. State and explain

Problem 1 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

State de Broglie's hypothesis. Why don't we see wave effects for a cricket ball?

Solution

Solution. De Broglie's hypothesis. Every particle of momentum p has a wavelength

λ=ph​=mvh​

Cricket ball. m=0.1 kg, v=10 m/s:

λ=(0.1)(10)6.6×10−34​≈7×10−34 m

This is 1019 times smaller than a proton. Wave effects (diffraction, interference) appear only when the wavelength is comparable to the size of the obstacle or slit, and no such slit exists, so the ball never shows them.

Problem 2 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

What is the physical meaning of |Ψ|²? What does normalisation mean?

Solution

Solution. Meaning of ∣Ψ∣2. It is the probability density: ∣Ψ(x,t)∣2dx is the probability of finding the particle between x and x+dx at time t. (Ψ itself is complex and not directly measurable.)

Normalisation.

∫−∞∞​∣Ψ∣2dx=1

The particle is certainly somewhere. A wave function that cannot be normalised (for example eikx over all space) cannot describe a single localised particle.

Problem 3 [QM 2 · Recall · 3 pts]

Why can't a single sinusoidal wave represent a moving particle? What is used instead?

Solution

Solution. Why one wave fails. A single sinusoid ei(kx−ωt) has:

  • one exact wavelength, so its amplitude is the same everywhere: it is spread over all space and cannot locate the particle;
  • a phase velocity vp​=c2/v>c, which cannot be the speed of a particle.

What is used instead. A wave packet: a superposition of waves with a range of wavelengths. Their interference makes the amplitude large only in a small region, and that region (the envelope) moves at the group velocity, which equals the particle's speed v.

Price. Localising the packet needs a spread of wavelengths, which is the uncertainty principle.

Problem 4 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

Distinguish phase velocity and group velocity. Which one equals the particle's speed?

Solution

Solution. Phase velocity vp​=ω/k: the speed of the crests of a single wave.

Group velocity vg​=dω/dk: the speed of the envelope of a wave packet.

For a de Broglie wave:

vp​=vc2​>c,vg​=v

The particle's speed is the group velocity. The phase velocity can exceed c, but it carries no energy or information. They are related by vp​vg​=c2.

Problem 5 [QM 2 · Recall · 3 pts]

Describe the Davisson–Germer experiment and explain how it confirmed matter waves.

Solution

Solution. Experiment (1927). A beam of 54 eV electrons hit a nickel crystal. The scattered intensity at the detector showed a sharp peak at φ=50∘.

Analysis. Treat the surface atoms as a diffraction grating of spacing d=0.215 nm:

dsinφ=nλ ⇒ λ=(0.215 nm)sin50∘=0.165 nm

de Broglie prediction. λ=1.226/54​ nm=0.167 nm.

The two agree to within 1%.

Why it confirms matter waves. A peak at a definite angle that moves as the voltage changes is interference, which only waves produce. It was the first direct proof that electrons behave as waves.

Problem 6 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why must the lowest energy of a particle in a box be greater than zero?

Solution

Solution. Wave argument. The wave function must vanish at both walls, so at least half a wavelength fits in the box: λmax​=2L. That means a non-zero momentum

p=2Lh​ ⇒ E1​=2mp2​=8mL2h2​>0

Uncertainty argument. The particle is confined, so Δx≤L and Δp≥ℏ/2L; an exactly zero momentum would have Δp=0, which is impossible.

Name. This is the zero-point energy: a confined quantum particle can never be at rest.

Problem 7 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

State the Heisenberg uncertainty principle for position–momentum and for energy–time.

Solution

Solution. Position–momentum.

ΔxΔp≥2ℏ​

Energy–time.

ΔEΔt≥2ℏ​

where ℏ=h/2π=1.055×10−34 J s (ℏc=197 eV nm).

Meaning. Δx and Δp are the spreads (standard deviations) in a state. It is a property of the state, not of the measuring apparatus.

Problem 8 [QM 2 · Recall · 3 pts]

"The uncertainty principle is due to the clumsiness of our measuring instruments." Is this correct? Explain.

Solution

Solution. No. The principle is not about clumsy instruments.

Reason. A particle has wave properties. A localised wave packet is built from a range of wavelengths, and the narrower the packet (small Δx) the wider the range needed (large Δp). This is a property of waves, true even for perfect measurement.

Consequence. A particle simply does not have exact x and p at the same time. Better instruments cannot beat ΔxΔp≥ℏ/2.

Common mistake. Heisenberg's microscope is an illustration of the idea, not the cause.

Problem 9 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

Explain Heisenberg's microscope argument in three lines.

Solution

Solution. Heisenberg's microscope.

  1. To "see" an electron you must scatter at least one photon of wavelength λ off it. Resolution is limited to Δx≈λ.
  2. The photon carries momentum h/λ, and gives the electron an unknown kick Δp≈h/λ.
  3. Then
ΔxΔp≈λ⋅λh​=h

whatever wavelength you choose: a shorter λ improves Δx but worsens Δp.

Problem 10 [QM 2 · Recall · 2 pts]

Why are spectral lines not perfectly sharp?

Solution

Solution. Reason. An excited state lives only a finite time Δt (its lifetime) before decaying. By the energy–time uncertainty principle its energy is uncertain by

ΔE≥2Δtℏ​

So the emitted photons have a spread of energies, giving the line a natural width.

Example. For Δt=10−8 s, ΔE≈3×10−8 eV, tiny but non-zero. (Doppler and collision broadening add to it in practice.)

Corollary. The ground state has Δt=∞, so it is perfectly sharp.

B. Derive or show

Problem 11 [QM 2 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Show that the phase velocity of a de Broglie wave is c²/v and that the group velocity equals v.

Solution

Solution. de Broglie relations. E=hν and p=h/λ, so ω=2πE/h and k=2πp/h.

Phase velocity.

vp​=kω​=pE​=γmvγmc2​=vc2​​

This exceeds c, but no energy or signal travels at vp​.

Group velocity. vg​=dω/dk=dE/dp. From E2=p2c2+m2c4:

2EdE=2pc2dp ⇒ dpdE​=Epc2​=γmc2γmvc2​=v​

The wave packet moves with the particle.

Check. vp​vg​=c2 ✓.

Problem 12 [QM 2 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Show that the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated from rest through V volts is λ = h/√(2meV), assuming v ≪ c.

Solution

Solution. Energy. With v≪c, the work done by the field becomes kinetic energy:

eV=21​mv2=2mp2​ ⇒ p=2meV​
λ=ph​=2meV​h​​

Numerically λ≈V​1.226​ nm, with V in volts: for V=100 V, λ=0.123 nm, comparable to atomic spacings.

Problem 13 [QM 2 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Using the standing-wave condition, derive the energy levels of a particle in a 1-D box of width L. Sketch the first three wave functions.

Solution

Solution. Standing wave. The wave function must vanish at the walls, ψ(0)=ψ(L)=0, so a whole number of half-wavelengths fit in the box:

L=n2λn​​ ⇒ λn​=n2L​

Momentum and energy.

pn​=λn​h​=2Lnh​,En​=2mpn2​​=8mL2n2h2​​

Wave functions ψn​∝sin(nπx/L):

n shape nodes inside
1 half a sine wave 0
2 full sine wave 1 (at L/2)
3 one and a half waves 2

Check. n=0 is not allowed (ψ≡0), so the lowest energy is E1​>0: zero-point energy.

Problem 14 [QM 2 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Using ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2, show that an electron can't exist inside a nucleus of size 10⁻¹⁴ m.

Solution

Solution. Uncertainty principle. Confined to Δx≈10−14 m =10−5 nm:

Δp≥2Δxℏ​ ⇒ Δpc≥2(10−5 nm)197 eV nm​≈10 MeV

Relativistic regime. Δpc≈10 MeV≫me​c2=0.51 MeV, so K≈pc≈10 MeV.

Conclusion. An electron confined to the nucleus would carry about 10 MeV of kinetic energy, but nuclear beta-decay electrons have only a few MeV, and no nuclear potential could bind it that strongly. So electrons cannot be nuclear constituents. (A proton, being much heavier, gets only about 0.1 MeV from confinement, consistent with nuclear binding.)

Problem 15 [QM 2 · Derivation · 4 pts]

Using the uncertainty principle, estimate the minimum energy of a particle of mass m in a box of width L. Compare with the exact E₁ = h²/8mL².

Solution

Solution. Estimate. Confining the particle to the box gives Δx≈L, so

p≈Δp≈2Lℏ​ ⇒ E≈2mp2​=8mL2ℏ2​

Compare with the exact E1​=8mL2h2​=8mL24π2ℏ2​:

Eest​E1​​=4π2≈40

The estimate is off by a numerical factor (the uncertainty relation only gives a lower bound on Δp, and the true ground state has more momentum spread than the minimum), but it gets the scaling E∝1/mL2 right, which is its purpose.

Problem 16 [QM 2 · Derivation · 3 pts]

Using the relativistic relations E = γmc² and p = γmv, write the de Broglie wavelength of an electron with kinetic energy K, valid at all speeds.

Solution

Solution. Relativistic relations. Total energy E=K+mc2 and E2=p2c2+m2c4:

p2c2=(K+mc2)2−m2c4=K2+2Kmc2
λ=ph​=K2+2Kmc2​hc​​

Non-relativistic limit K≪mc2: drop K2, giving

λ≈2Kmc2​hc​=2mK​h​

✓ (agrees with Problem 12).

Check. For a 1 MeV electron, pc=1+1.02​=1.42 MeV, λ=1240/1.42×106 nm =8.7×10−4 nm; the non-relativistic formula would give 1.2×10−3 nm (about 40% too long).

C. Numericals without a calculator

Problem 17 [QM 2 · Numerical]

Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V.

Solution

Solution. Idea. For an electron accelerated through V volts, λ=h/2meV​≈1.226/V​ nm.

λ=100​1.226​ nm=0.123 nm​

Problem 18 [QM 2 · Numerical]

Find the de Broglie wavelength of a 0.1 kg ball moving at 10 m/s, and compare it with the size of a proton (about 10⁻¹⁵ m).

Solution

Solution. Idea. λ=h/p=h/mv.

λ=(0.1 kg)(10 m/s)6.6×10−34 J s​=6.6×10−34 m​

Compared with a proton (∼10−15 m) it is about 1019 times smaller, so no wave effect could ever be seen for such a ball.

Problem 19 [QM 2 · Numerical]

In the Davisson–Germer experiment the electrons were accelerated through 54 V. Find their de Broglie wavelength.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Same formula as above, with V=54 V.

λ=54​1.226​=7.351.226​ nm=0.167 nm​

This matches the diffraction result (0.165 nm), which confirmed matter waves.

Problem 20 [QM 2 · Numerical]

An electron is confined to a box 0.1 nm wide. Find E₁ and the energy of the photon emitted in the n = 2 → 1 transition.

Solution

Solution. Idea. En​=n2h2/8mL2. Write it as E1​=(hc)2/(8me​c2L2) so that all numbers are in eV and nm.

E1​=8(0.511×106 eV)(0.1 nm)2(1240 eV nm)2​=4.09×1041.54×106​≈38 eV​
E2​−E1​=4E1​−E1​=3E1​≈113 eV​

which is a far-ultraviolet photon (λ=1240/113≈11 nm).

Problem 21 [QM 2 · Numerical]

An electron is located within 0.1 nm. Estimate the minimum spread in its momentum and its minimum kinetic energy.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Uncertainty principle ΔxΔp≥ℏ/2, then KE≈(Δp)2/2m.

Δpc≥2Δxℏc​=2(0.1 nm)197 eV nm​≈985 eV ⇒ Δp≈5×10−25 kg m/s
KE≈2me​c2(Δpc)2​=1.02×106(985)2​≈1 eV​

Check. Atomic energies are of order eV, as they should be for an electron confined to atomic size.

Problem 22 [QM 2 · Numerical]

A proton is confined to a nucleus of size 10⁻¹⁴ m. Estimate its minimum kinetic energy. Is this a reasonable nuclear energy scale?

Solution

Solution. Idea. Same method, for a proton (mp​c2=938 MeV) with Δx=10−14 m =10−5 nm.

Δpc≥2×10−5 nm197 eV nm​≈10 MeV
KE≈2mp​c2(Δpc)2​=2(938)102​≈0.05 MeV​

Using Δp≈ℏ/Δx (a factor 2 larger) gives about 0.2 MeV. Either way it is ∼0.1 MeV, within an order of magnitude or two of nuclear binding energies per nucleon (MeV), so confinement alone gives the right nuclear scale. (An electron in the same space would need ∼10 MeV, far more than beta-decay energies, which rules out electrons living in the nucleus.)

Problem 23 [QM 2 · Numerical]

An excited atomic state lives 10⁻⁸ s. Find the minimum uncertainty in its energy, in eV.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Energy–time uncertainty: ΔEΔt≥ℏ/2.

ΔE≥2Δtℏ​=2(10−8 s)6.6×10−16 eV s​≈3×10−8 eV​

This is the natural line width of the spectral line.

Problem 24 [QM 2 · Numerical]

Find the de Broglie wavelength of a thermal neutron with KE = 0.025 eV. Why are such neutrons used to study crystals?

Solution

Solution. Idea. Non-relativistic neutron: p=2mK​, so pc=2(mn​c2)K​ and λ=hc/pc.

pc=2(939×106 eV)(0.025 eV)​≈6.9 keV
λ=6900 eV1240 eV nm​≈0.18 nm​

Why used on crystals. 0.18 nm is the size of atomic spacings, so the neutrons diffract strongly. Being neutral they also penetrate deep into the material.

D. Estimate and discuss

Problem 25 [QM 2 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Estimate the size of a hydrogen atom using the uncertainty principle: minimise E(r) = ħ²/(2mr²) − ke²/r.

Solution

Solution. Idea. Confining the electron to a radius r gives Δp≈ℏ/r, so its kinetic energy is at least ℏ2/2mr2.

E(r)=2mr2ℏ2​−rke2​

Minimise.

drdE​=−mr3ℏ2​+r2ke2​=0 ⇒ r=mke2ℏ2​=a0​=0.053 nm​
Emin​=−2a0​ke2​=−13.6 eV​

Comment. The uncertainty principle alone gives the right size and energy of hydrogen: kinetic energy (∝1/r2) resists confinement while Coulomb attraction (∝−1/r) pulls in, and the balance fixes the atom's size.

Problem 26 [QM 2 · Discussion · 3 pts]

Electron microscopes use electrons of about 100 keV. Estimate their wavelength and explain why they resolve much finer detail than light microscopes.

Solution

Solution. Wavelength of a 100 keV electron. Relativistic momentum:

pc=K2+2Kmc2​=0.12+2(0.1)(0.511)​ MeV=0.335 MeV
λ=pchc​=3.35×105 eV1240 eV nm​=3.7 pm​

Comparison. Visible light has λ≈500 nm, about 105 times longer.

Why finer detail. A microscope cannot resolve features much smaller than the wavelength used, so electrons can in principle resolve about 105 times finer detail. In practice lens defects limit electron microscopes to about 0.1 nm.

Problem 27 [QM 2 · Discussion · 2 pts]

A dust grain of 1 μg sits in a 1 μm box. Using E₁ = h²/8mL², explain without exact arithmetic why we never notice its quantised energy.

Solution

Solution. Numbers. m=10−9 kg, L=10−6 m:

E1​=8mL2h2​=8(10−9)(10−12)(6.6×10−34)2​≈5×10−47 J (∼10−46 J)

Compare with thermal energy at room temperature, kB​T≈4×10−21 J:

E1​kB​T​∼1025

Why it is invisible. The level spacing, En​−En−1​∝n, is far smaller than anything measurable, and the grain has about 1025 quanta thermally. The energy looks continuous: this is the correspondence principle (large quantum numbers give classical behaviour).
