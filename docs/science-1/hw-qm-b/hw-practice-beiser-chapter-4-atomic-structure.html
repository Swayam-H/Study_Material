HW practice: Beiser Chapter 4, Atomic structure

8 min read · 11 practice problems

Solutions stay collapsed until you click them.

In-chapter examples 4.1 to 4.7 (Example 4.8, Rutherford scattering, is outside the course scope) and end-of-chapter problems 5, 6, 13, 16 and 30. Constants: a0​=0.0529 nm, R=1.097×107 m⁻¹, En​=−13.6/n2 eV, 1/λ=R(1/nf2​−1/ni2​).

Problem 1 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.1]

13.6 eV is needed to separate a hydrogen atom into a proton and an electron, i.e. E=−13.6 eV. Find the orbital radius and velocity of the electron.

Solution

Solution. In the Bohr model, E=−8πϵ0​re2​ (kinetic +8πϵ0​re2​, potential −4πϵ0​re2​):

r=8πϵ0​∣E∣e2​=2(13.6 eV)1.44 eV nm​=0.0529 nm​

This is the Bohr radius a0​. The kinetic energy equals 13.6 eV, so

v=m2KE​​=9.11×10−312(13.6)(1.602×10−19)​​=2.19×106 m/s​ (≈c/137)

Problem 2 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.2]

An electron collides with a hydrogen atom in its ground state and excites it to n=3. How much energy was given to the atom?

Solution

Solution. E1​=−13.6 eV and E3​=−13.6/9=−1.51 eV, so

ΔE=E3​−E1​=13.6(1−91​)=12.1 eV​

In an inelastic collision the atom takes exactly the level difference (the incident electron keeps the rest of its energy).

Problem 3 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.3]

(a) Find the quantum number of the Bohr orbit in a hydrogen atom whose radius is 0.0100 mm. (b) What is the energy of the atom in this state?

Solution

Solution. (a) rn​=n2a0​:

n=a0​rn​​​=5.29×10−11 m1.00×10−5 m​​=435​

(b) En​=−435213.6​=−7.2×10−5 eV​.

Such a Rydberg atom is nearly ionized and about 104 times larger than normal.

Problem 4 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.4]

Find the longest wavelength in the Balmer series (the Hα line).

Solution

Solution. The Balmer series has nf​=2. The longest wavelength is the smallest energy step, ni​=3:

λ1​=R(41​−91​)=365R​ ⇒ λ=5R36​=5(1.097×107)36​=656 nm​

(red, visible).

Problem 5 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.5]

(a) Find the orbital frequencies of electrons in the n=1 and n=2 Bohr orbits. (b) What is the frequency of the photon emitted for n=2→n=1? (c) An electron spends about 10−8 s in an excited state. How many revolutions does an n=2 electron make in 1.00×10−8 s?

Solution

Solution. (a) fn​=vn​/2πrn​ with vn​=v1​/n and rn​=n2a0​, so fn​=f1​/n3. For n=1: f1​=2.19×106/(2π⋅5.29×10−11)=6.58×1015 Hz​. For n=2: f2​=f1​/8=8.2×1014 Hz​.

(b) hν=13.6(1−1/4)=10.2 eV, so ν=10.2/(4.136×10−15)=2.47×1015 Hz​. This is not equal to either orbital frequency (6.6×1015 Hz and 8.2×1014 Hz): the classical picture would predict radiation at the orbital frequency, and the quantum result differs. For large n the photon frequency for n→n−1 approaches the orbital frequency (correspondence principle).

(c) Revolutions =f2​t=(8.2×1014)(1.00×10−8)=8.2×106​, so the electron makes millions of turns before radiating.

Problem 6 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.6]

Positronium is a positron and an electron orbiting each other. Compare the wavelengths of its spectral lines with those of ordinary hydrogen.

Solution

Solution. The Bohr energies depend on the reduced mass:

En​=−32π2ϵ02​ℏ2n2μe4​

For hydrogen μ≈me​. For positronium both bodies have mass me​, so μ=me​/2 and

En​=−n26.8 eV​

All energy differences are half those of hydrogen, so every photon has half the energy and every wavelength is twice as long. For example the n=3→2 line is at 2×656=1312 nm (infrared) instead of 656 nm. The orbit is also twice as big (rn​=2n2a0​).

Problem 7 [HW practice · Beiser Ex 4.7]

A muon has mass 207me​ and charge ±e. A negative muon can be captured by a nucleus to form a muonic atom. (a) A proton captures a μ−; find the radius of the first Bohr orbit. (b) Find the ionization energy.

Solution

Solution. Use the reduced mass μ=mμ​+mp​mμ​mp​​=207+1836(207)(1836)​me​=186me​. Bohr results scale as r∝1/μ and E∝μ:

(a) r1​=186a0​​=1860.0529 nm​=2.8×10−4 nm=284 fm​, which is 186 times smaller than hydrogen's.

(b) Eion​=13.6 eV×186=2.53 keV​.

Problem 8 [HW practice · Beiser Ch 4 Problems 5 and 6]

  1. What is the shortest wavelength in the Brackett series? 6. What is the shortest wavelength in the Paschen series?
Solution

Solution. The shortest wavelength of a series (the series limit) comes from ni​→∞:

λmin​1​=nf2​R​ ⇒ λmin​=Rnf2​​

5. Brackett (nf​=4): λmin​=1.097×10716​=1458 nm​ (infrared).

6. Paschen (nf​=3): λmin​=1.097×1079​=820 nm​ (infrared).

Problem 9 [HW practice · Beiser Ch 4 Problem 13]

Compare the uncertainty in the momentum of an electron confined to a region of linear dimension a0​ with the momentum of an electron in the ground-state Bohr orbit.

Solution

Solution. Take Δx∼a0​. The uncertainty principle gives

Δp≥2a0​ℏ​=2(5.29×10−11)1.055×10−34​=1.0×10−24 kg m/s

In the Bohr ground state mva0​=ℏ, so

p=a0​ℏ​=2.0×10−24 kg m/s

So Δp≥p/2: the minimum uncertainty is of the same order as the momentum itself. An electron localized to atomic size must have a momentum of about this size, which is what the Bohr model says it has.

Problem 10 [HW practice · Beiser Ch 4 Problem 16]

A beam of 13.0 eV electrons bombards gaseous hydrogen. What series of wavelengths will be emitted?

Solution

Solution. The most the atom can absorb is 13.0 eV, so it reaches level n only if 13.6(1−1/n2)≤13.0:

level energy above ground / eV
n=2 10.20
n=3 12.09
n=4 12.75
n=5 13.06 (not reached)

So n=2,3,4 can be excited. The emitted lines, from 1/λ=R(1/nf2​−1/ni2​):

series transition λ
Lyman 2→1, 3→1, 4→1 121.5 nm, 102.5 nm, 97.2 nm
Balmer 3→2, 4→2 656.1 nm, 486.0 nm
Paschen 4→3 1874.6 nm

Six lines in total: three in the Lyman series (ultraviolet), two in the Balmer series (visible), and one in the Paschen series (infrared).

Problem 11 [HW practice · Beiser Ch 4 Problem 30]

An antiproton has the mass of a proton but a charge of −e. If a proton and an antiproton orbited each other, how far apart would they be in the ground state? Why might you think such a system could not occur?

Solution

Solution. The two bodies have equal mass, so the reduced mass is μ=mp​/2=918me​. The Bohr radius of the relative motion scales as 1/μ, and the proton–antiproton separation is this radius:

r=μe24πϵ0​ℏ2​=918a0​​=9180.0529 nm​=5.8×10−5 nm=5.8×10−14 m​

(each particle orbits the centre at half this distance).

Such a system could not last because a particle and its antiparticle annihilate. At a separation of 58 fm they are only a few proton radii apart (the strong interaction acts at about 1 fm), so annihilation into mesons happens almost at once. Protonium-like atoms exist only as very short-lived states.
