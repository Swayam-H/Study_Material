1A and 1B: gases and the kinetic model

Problem 1 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 1A.1]

Nitrogen gas is put in a vessel of constant volume at 100 atm and 300 K, then heated to 500 K. What pressure would it exert, assuming perfect-gas behaviour?

Solution

Solution. Volume and amount are fixed, so the combined gas law pV/nT=R reduces to p1​/T1​=p2​/T2​:

p2​=p1​T1​T2​​=100 atm×300500​=167 atm​

Problem 2 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 1A.2]

The mass percentage composition of dry air at sea level is approximately N₂ 75.5, O₂ 23.2, Ar 1.3. What is the partial pressure of each component when the total pressure is 1.20 atm?

Solution

Solution. Mole fractions do not depend on sample size, so take 100 g of air and convert with n=m/M:

gas mass / g M / g mol⁻¹ n / mol xJ​=nJ​/n pJ​=xJ​p / atm
N₂ 75.5 28.01 2.695 0.781 0.937
O₂ 23.2 32.00 0.725 0.210 0.252
Ar 1.3 39.95 0.0325 0.0094 0.0113

Total n=3.453 mol. Using pJ​=xJ​p with p=1.20 atm:

p(N2​)=0.937 atm,p(O2​)=0.252 atm,p(Ar)=0.0113 atm​

The three add to 1.20 atm, as Dalton's law requires.

Problem 3 [HW practice · Thermo · E1A.2]

(a) Could 131 g of xenon in a vessel of volume 1.0 dm³ exert a pressure of 20 atm at 25 °C if it behaved as a perfect gas? If not, what pressure would it exert? (b) The same question for 25 g of argon in 1.5 dm³ at 30 °C, pressure 2.0 bar.

Solution

Solution. Compute the pressure a perfect gas would exert, p=nRT/V, and compare.

(a) n=131/131.3=0.998 mol, T=298.15 K:

p=1.0(0.998)(0.082058)(298.15)​ atm=24 atm​

It would exert about 24 atm, not 20 atm. So no: 20 atm is not what a perfect gas of this amount would give.

(b) n=25/39.95=0.626 mol, T=303.15 K, V=1.5×10−3 m³:

p=1.5×10−3(0.626)(8.3145)(303.15)​=1.05×106 Pa=10.5 bar​

So no: it would exert 10.5 bar, not 2.0 bar.

Problem 4 [HW practice · Thermo · E1A.9]

(a) The mass density of a gaseous compound was found to be 1.23 kg m⁻³ at 330 K and 20 kPa. What is its molar mass? (b) In another experiment 250 cm³ of a gas at 152 Torr and 298 K had mass 33.5 mg (after buoyancy correction). What is the molar mass?

Solution

Solution. For a perfect gas pV=(m/M)RT, so

M=pρRT​

(a) M=2.0×104(1.23)(8.3145)(330)​=0.1687 kg mol−1=169 g mol−1​

(b) p=152/760 atm=2.027×104 Pa. Then n=pV/RT=(2.027×104)(2.50×10−4)/(8.3145×298.15)=2.044×10−3 mol and

M=2.044×10−3 mol33.5 mg​=16.4 g mol−1​

(methane-like).

Problem 5 [HW practice · Thermo · E1A.11]

(a) A vessel of volume 22.4 dm³ contains 2.0 mol H₂ and 1.0 mol N₂ at 273.15 K. Find (i) the mole fractions, (ii) the partial pressures, (iii) the total pressure. (b) The same for 1.5 mol H₂ and 2.5 mol N₂.

Solution

Solution. xJ​=nJ​/n, pJ​=nJ​RT/V and p=∑pJ​.

(a) n=3.0 mol. (i) x(H2​)=2/3=0.667, x(N2​)=1/3=0.333. (iii) p=nRT/V=(3.0)(0.082058)(273.15)/22.4=3.00 atm. (ii) p(H2​)=32​(3.00)=2.00 atm and p(N2​)=1.00 atm.

x=0.667, 0.333;pJ​=2.00, 1.00 atm;p=3.00 atm (3.04 bar)​

(b) n=4.0 mol. x(H2​)=0.375, x(N2​)=0.625, p=4.0×0.082058×273.15/22.4=4.00 atm, so p(H2​)=1.50 atm and p(N2​)=2.50 atm.

(Because 22.4 dm³ is the molar volume at 0 °C and 1 atm, each mole contributes 1 atm.)

Problem 6 [HW practice · Thermo · P1A.2]

Neptunians use a Celsius-type scale based on the melting point (0 °N) and boiling point (100 °N) of hydrogen. In the limit of zero pressure, pV is 28 dm³ atm at 0 °N and 40 dm³ atm at 100 °N. Where is absolute zero on their scale?

Solution

Solution. For a perfect gas pV=nRT is linear in absolute temperature, so it is linear in any linear temperature scale θ:

pV=a+bθ,a=28,b=10040−28​=0.12 dm3atm(∘N)−1

Absolute zero is where pV=0:

0=28+0.12θ0​ ⇒ θ0​=−0.1228​=−233 ∘N​

Check: the interval between the two fixed points is 100 °N and pV rises by a factor 40/28=1.43, so absolute zero lies 28/12=2.33 intervals below 0 °N.

Problem 7 [HW practice · Thermo · P1A.11]

Derive the barometric formula p=p0​e−h/H with H=RT/Mg by showing that dp=−ρgdh. Evaluate (a) the pressure difference between the top and bottom of a 15 cm laboratory vessel, and (b) the external pressure at 11 km if the ground pressure is 1.0 atm. Take H≈8 km.

Solution

Solution. Derivation. A thin slab of air between h and h+dh is supported by the pressure difference across it. Its weight per unit area is ρgdh, so

dp=−ρgdh

For a perfect gas the mass density is ρ=Mp/RT. Substituting (this is the point where ρ depends on p):

pdp​=−RTMg​dh=−Hdh​ ⇒ lnp0​p​=−Hh​ ⇒ p=p0​e−h/H​

with H=RT/Mg (taking T constant).

(a) For h=0.15 m, h/H=0.15/8000=1.9×10−5, so

Δp≈p0​Hh​=(1 atm)(1.9×10−5)=1.9×10−5 atm (≈1.9 Pa)

Over a laboratory vessel the pressure is uniform to about two parts in 105.

(b) p=(1.0 atm)e−11/8=0.25 atm​.

Problem 8 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 1B.1]

Calculate vrms​ and the mean speed vmean​ of N₂ molecules at 25 °C.

Solution

Solution. M=0.02802 kg mol⁻¹, T=298 K.

vrms​=M3RT​​=0.028023(8.3145)(298)​​=515 m s−1​
vmean​=πM8RT​​=π(0.02802)8(8.3145)(298)​​=475 m s−1​

The ordering vmp​<vmean​<vrms​ holds, with vmp​=2RT/M​=421 m s⁻¹.

Problem 9 [HW practice · Thermo · E1B.2]

(a) Calculate the root-mean-square speeds of H₂ and O₂ molecules at 20 °C. (b) The same for CO₂ and He.

Solution

Solution. vrms​=3RT/M​ with T=293.15 K and 3RT=7312 J mol⁻¹.

gas M / kg mol⁻¹ vrms​ / m s⁻¹
H₂ 0.002016 1.90 × 10³
O₂ 0.03200 478
CO₂ 0.04401 408
He 0.004003 1.35 × 10³

At the same temperature the speed scales as M−1/2: H₂ is 4 times faster than O₂, since 32/2​=4.

Problem 10 [HW practice · Thermo · E1B.8]

(a) At what pressure does the mean free path of argon at 20 °C become comparable to the diameter of a 100 cm³ vessel containing it? Take σ=0.36 nm2. (b) At what pressure does it become comparable to 10 times the diameter of the atoms themselves?

Solution

Solution. Mean free path of a perfect gas:

λ=2​σpkB​T​ ⇒ p=2​σλkB​T​

(a) Treat the vessel as a sphere: V=πd3/6=1.00×10−4 m³ gives d=5.76×10−2 m. With T=293.15 K and σ=3.6×10−19 m²:

p=2​(3.6×10−19)(5.76×10−2)(1.3806×10−23)(293.15)​=0.14 Pa​

(A cube of side 4.64 cm gives 0.17 Pa; the answer depends on the shape assumed but is about 0.1 Pa either way.)

(b) From σ=πdAr2​ (collision cross-section with collision diameter d), dAr​=σ/π​=0.339 nm, so λ=3.39 nm:

p=2​(3.6×10−19)(3.39×10−9)(1.3806×10−23)(293.15)​=2.3×106 Pa≈23 atm​

At this pressure the mean free path is only ten atomic diameters, so the dilute-gas formula is already doubtful.

Problem 11 [HW practice · Thermo · P1B.7]

Calculate the escape velocity from the surface of a planet of radius R. Evaluate it for (i) Earth (R=6.37×106 m, g=9.81 m s⁻²) and (ii) Mars (R=3.38×106 m, mMars​/mEarth​=0.108). At what temperatures do H₂, He and O₂ have mean speeds equal to their escape speeds? What proportion of molecules can escape at (i) 240 K and (ii) 1500 K?

Solution

Solution. Escape speed. Energy conservation: 21​mve2​=GMm/R. Since g=GM/R2, GM=gR2 and

ve​=2gR​​
  • Earth: ve​=2(9.81)(6.37×106)​=1.12×104 m s⁻¹ (11.2 km s⁻¹).
  • Mars: gM​=g(mM​/mE​)(RE​/RM​)2=9.81(0.108)(6.37/3.38)2=3.76 m s⁻², so ve​=2(3.76)(3.38×106)​=5.04×103 m s⁻¹.

Temperature at which vmean​=ve​. Set 8RT/πM​=ve​:

T=8RπMve2​​
gas T on Earth / K T on Mars / K
H₂ 1.2×104 2.4×103
He 2.4×104 4.8×103
O₂ 1.9×105 3.8×104

Fraction able to escape (speed >ve​). With a=M/2RT​, x=ave​, integrating the Maxwell distribution:

P(v>ve​)=erfc(x)+π​2​xe−x2
gas Earth, 240 K Earth, 1500 K Mars, 240 K Mars, 1500 K
H₂ 3×10−27 1.5×10−4 1.1×10−5 0.25
He ∼10−53 1×10−8 5×10−11 0.043
O₂ 0 ∼10−69 ∼10−88 4×10−14

Even though the mean speed of H₂ is far below ve​ on Earth, the high-speed tail lets a small fraction escape at 1500 K (the upper atmosphere), which, accumulated over geological time, explains why Earth has lost its light gases but kept O₂ and N₂. Mars, with its much lower ve​, loses H₂ rapidly and He and O₂ much more slowly.

Problem 12 [HW practice · Thermo · P1B.8]

Plot the Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distributions for molar mass 100 g mol⁻¹ at temperatures between 200 K and 2000 K.

Solution

Solution. The distribution is

f(v)=4π(2πRTM​)3/2v2e−Mv2/2RT

Plot f(v) against v (0 to about 1500 m s⁻¹) for T=200, 500, 1000, 2000 K. The shape is fixed by the most probable speed vmp​=2RT/M​ and the peak height f(vmp​)=eπ​vmp​4​:

T / K vmp​ / m s⁻¹ peak f(vmp​) / 10⁻³ s m⁻¹
200 182 8.1
500 288 5.1
1000 408 3.6
2000 577 2.6

What the plot shows:

  • Each curve rises as v2 (few slow molecules, because few ways to have small speed), peaks at vmp​ and falls off exponentially.
  • Raising T moves the peak to the right as T​ and lowers and broadens it, because the area under every curve is 1.
  • The high-speed tail grows rapidly with T: this is what drives reaction rates and evaporation.

Problem 13 [HW practice · Thermo · P1B.10]

The maximum of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution occurs where df/dv=0. Find an expression for the most probable speed of molecules of molar mass M at temperature T.

Solution

Solution. Write f(v)=Av2e−Bv2 with A=4π(M/2πRT)3/2 and B=M/2RT. Differentiate:

dvdf​=A(2v−2Bv3)e−Bv2=2Av(1−Bv2)e−Bv2

This vanishes at v=0 (a minimum, since f=0 there), at v→∞, and where 1−Bv2=0:

v2=B1​=M2RT​ ⇒ vmp​=M2RT​​​

The second derivative is negative there, so it is a maximum.

3C, 3D, 3E: third law, Gibbs energy, thermodynamic relations

Problem 14 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 3C.1]

The molar constant-pressure heat capacity of a nonmetallic solid at 4.2 K is 0.43 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. What is its molar entropy at that temperature (relative to Sm​(0))?

Solution

Solution. At very low temperature the Debye law applies: Cp,m​=aT3. Then

Sm​(T)−Sm​(0)=∫0T​TCp,m​​dT=a∫0T​T2dT=3aT3​=3Cp,m​(T)​
Sm​(4.2 K)=Sm​(0)+30.43​=Sm​(0)+0.14 J K−1mol−1​

The integral converges at T→0, which is why the third law lets us start entropy sums at T=0.

Problem 15 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 3D.2]

How much energy is available for sustaining muscular and nervous activity from the oxidation of 1.00 mol of glucose molecules under standard conditions at 37 °C? The standard entropy of reaction is +182.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ and Δr​H∘=−2808 kJ mol⁻¹.

Solution

Solution. The maximum non-expansion work at constant T and p is −Δr​G∘. Taking Δr​H∘ independent of temperature between 25 and 37 °C:

Δr​G∘=Δr​H∘−TΔr​S∘=−2808 kJ mol−1−(310 K)(0.1824 kJ K−1mol−1)=−2865 kJ mol−1
wnon-exp,max​=2865 kJ per mole of glucose​

The positive Δr​S∘ makes the Gibbs energy more negative than the enthalpy: the products (CO₂, H₂O) have more freedom than the reactants.

Problem 16 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 3E.2]

Show thermodynamically that πT​=(∂U/∂V)T​=0 for a perfect gas, and compute its value for a van der Waals gas.

Solution

Solution. From dU=TdS−pdV, at constant T:

πT​=(∂V∂U​)T​=T(∂V∂S​)T​−p

The Maxwell relation from dA=−SdT−pdV is (∂S/∂V)T​=(∂p/∂T)V​, so

πT​=T(∂T∂p​)V​−p​

Perfect gas. p=nRT/V, so (∂p/∂T)V​=nR/V and πT​=nRT/V−p=0.

van der Waals gas. p=V−nbnRT​−V2an2​, so (∂p/∂T)V​=V−nbnR​ and

πT​=V−nbnRT​−(V−nbnRT​−V2an2​)=V2an2​​

The internal energy increases on expansion because molecules must be pulled apart against attractions, measured by a. The excluded volume b does not appear.

Problem 17 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 3E.3]

For a phase transition of a solid, Δtrs​V=+1.0 cm3mol−1 independent of pressure. By how much does the Gibbs energy of transition change when the pressure rises from 1.0 bar to 3.0 Mbar?

Solution

Solution. (∂G/∂p)T​=V, so for the transition Δtrs​G(pf​)−Δtrs​G(pi​)=Δtrs​VΔp:

Δ(Δtrs​G)=(1.0×10−6 m3mol−1)(3.0×1011−1.0×105) Pa=+3.0×102 kJ mol−1​

The positive Δtrs​V means high pressure disfavours the transition; 3 Mbar is the pressure scale of the Earth's core.

Problem 18 [HW practice · Thermo · P3D.1]

A perfect gas is in a cylinder of fixed volume divided by a frictionless, thermally insulating piston into sections A and B. B is held at 300 K (isothermal). Each section holds 2.00 mol, CV,m​=20 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, initially TA​=TB​=300 K and VA​=VB​=2.00 dm3. Heat is supplied to A so it expands reversibly, compressing B to 1.00 dm³. (a) Find the final pressure and TA​. (b) ΔS of A. (c) ΔS of B. (d) ΔU of each section. (e) ΔA for B, and explain why it cannot be found for A. (f) What does reversibility imply for the total ΔA?

Solution

Solution. (a) B is isothermal at 300 K: pf​=nRT/VB​=(2.00)(8.3145)(300)/(1.00×10−3)=4.99×106 Pa=49.9 bar​. Section A takes up the rest of the fixed total volume: VA​=4.00−1.00=3.00 dm³, and the pressures are equal, so

TA​=nRpVA​​=(2.00)(8.3145)(4.99×106)(3.00×10−3)​=900 K​

(b) Use the hint: heat at constant volume from 300 K to 900 K, then expand isothermally from 2.00 to 3.00 dm³:

ΔSA​=nCV,m​lnTi​Tf​​+nRlnVi​Vf​​=(2)(20)ln3+(2)(8.3145)ln1.5=43.9+6.7=+50.7 J K−1​

(c) ΔSB​=nRln(Vf​/Vi​)=(2)(8.3145)ln(1/2)=−11.5 J K−1​

(d) U depends only on T: ΔUA​=nCV,m​ΔT=(2)(20)(600)=+24.0 kJ​ and ΔUB​=0​ (isothermal).

(e) For B, T is constant, so ΔAB​=ΔUB​−TΔSB​=0−(300)(−11.5)=+3.46 kJ​ (this equals the work done on B, nRTln2). For A, ΔA=ΔU−Δ(TS) and T changes from 300 K to 900 K, so Δ(TS)=Tf​Sf​−Ti​Si​ needs the absolute entropies, not just ΔS; they are not given. The shortcut ΔA=ΔU−TΔS is valid only at constant T.

(f) For the whole system, dA=−SA​dTA​−SB​dTB​−pd(VA​+VB​) in a reversible change. The total volume is fixed and TB​ is constant, so the pdV terms cancel exactly between the two sections (the work A does on the piston is the work done on B). Reversibility therefore means no net work crosses the cylinder wall, and ΔAtotal​=−∫SA​dTA​, which is not zero but depends only on heating A.

Problem 19 [HW practice · Thermo · I3.3(d)]

Show how the first derivative of G, (∂G/∂p)T​, varies with pressure and plot the resulting expression over a pressure range. What is the physical significance of (∂G/∂p)T​?

Solution

Solution. From dG=Vdp−SdT,

(∂p∂G​)T​=V

For one mole of a perfect gas Vm​=RT/p, so the slope is a rectangular hyperbola that diverges as p→0 and decays as 1/p:

p / bar 0.1 0.5 1 2 5 10
Vm​ at 298 K / dm³ 247.8 49.6 24.8 12.4 4.96 2.48

Integrating gives the familiar logarithm, Gm​(p)=Gm∘​+RTln(p/p∘), which rises steeply at low p and flattens at high p. For a van der Waals gas the slope is Vm​(p) from the cubic and stays above b; for a liquid or solid Vm​ is nearly constant, so Gm​ is almost linear in p with a small slope.

Significance. The slope of the Gibbs energy against pressure is the volume. It is always positive, so G always rises on compression, and it is far larger for gases than for condensed phases. That is why raising the pressure favours the phase with smaller molar volume (e.g. ice melting under a skate, since ice has the larger volume).

4B: physical transformations

Problem 20 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 4B.1]

Calculate the effect on the chemical potentials of ice and water of increasing the pressure from 1.00 bar to 2.00 bar at 0 °C. The mass density of ice is 0.917 g cm⁻³ and of liquid water 0.999 g cm⁻³.

Solution

Solution. dμ=Vm​dp, and for an incompressible phase Δμ=Vm​Δp=MΔp/ρ. With M=1.802×10−2 kg mol⁻¹ and Δp=1.00×105 Pa:

Δμ(ice)=917(1.802×10−2)(1.00×105)​=+1.97 J mol−1​
Δμ(water)=999(1.802×10−2)(1.00×105)​=+1.80 J mol−1​

The chemical potential of ice rises more than that of water, so if they were in equilibrium at 1 bar, at 2 bar μ(ice)>μ(water) and ice tends to melt. That is the sign of a negative dT/dp for the melting line of water.

Problem 21 [HW practice · Thermo · Ex 4B.2]

Estimate the typical size of the effect of increasing pressure on the boiling point of a liquid.

Solution

Solution. The Clapeyron equation for vaporization is dTdp​=TΔvap​VΔvap​H​. At the boiling point Δvap​H/T is Trouton's constant, 85 JK−1mol−1. Since Vm​(g)≫Vm​(l), Δvap​V≈Vm​(g)≈25 dm3mol−1=2.5×10−2 m3mol−1. Then

dTdp​≈2.5×10−285​=3.4×103 Pa K−1=0.034 atm K−1
dpdT​≈29 K atm−1​

A pressure change of +0.1 atm raises the boiling temperature by about 3 K. Any liquid behaves about like this, which is why a pressure cooker works and why water boils lower at altitude.

Problem 22 [HW practice · Thermo · P4B.12]

The enthalpy changes by dH=Cp​dT+Vdp. The Clapeyron equation relates dp and dT along a phase boundary. (a) Show that along such a boundary dΔtrs​H=Δtrs​Cp​dT+(Δtrs​H/T)dT. (b) Show it can be written d(Δtrs​H/T)=Δtrs​Cp​dlnT.

Solution

Solution. (a) Apply dH=Cp​dT+Vdp to each phase, α and β, and subtract (the transition is α→β):

dΔtrs​H=Δtrs​Cp​dT+Δtrs​Vdp

Along the boundary the Clapeyron equation holds, dp/dT=Δtrs​H/(TΔtrs​V), so Δtrs​Vdp=(Δtrs​H/T)dT:

dΔtrs​H=Δtrs​Cp​dT+TΔtrs​H​dT​

(b) Start from the second form and expand the left side:

d(TΔtrs​H​)=TdΔtrs​H​−T2Δtrs​H​dT

Substitute (a) for dΔtrs​H:

=TΔtrs​Cp​dT​+T2Δtrs​H​dT−T2Δtrs​H​dT=Δtrs​Cp​TdT​=Δtrs​Cp​dlnT​

So Δtrs​H/T=Δtrs​S changes along the boundary only because the two phases have different heat capacities, which is the familiar dΔS=ΔCp​dlnT.
