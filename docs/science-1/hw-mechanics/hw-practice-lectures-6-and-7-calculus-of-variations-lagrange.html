Marion and Thornton, Examples 6.1, 6.3, 6.5, 7.3, 7.7, 7.8, 7.10 and Problem 7-17. (Example 6.2, the brachistochrone, and Example 7.11, the particle on a cylinder, already have full solutions in Chapters 3 and 4.) The homework list says "E-7.17"; the book has no Example 7.17 (examples stop at 7.12), so it is taken to mean Problem 7-17.

Problem 1 [HW practice · MT E6.1]

Consider f=(dy/dx)2 with y(x)=x. Add the function η(x)=sinx and find J(α) between x=0 and x=2π. Show that the stationary value of J(α) occurs for α=0.

Solution

Solution. The varied path is y(α,x)=x+αsinx, which has the same end points y(0)=0, y(2π)=2π for every α. Then dy/dx=1+αcosx and

J(α)=∫02π​(1+αcosx)2dx=∫02π​(1+2αcosx+α2cos2x)dx=2π+0+πα2
dαdJ​=2πα=0 ⇒ α=0​

and d2J/dα2=2π>0, so it is a minimum. The true extremal (the straight line y=x) is the member with α=0, and neighbouring paths all have larger J.

Problem 2 [HW practice · MT E6.3]

Find the equation of the line connecting two fixed points (x1​,y1​) and (x2​,y2​) such that the surface of revolution generated by rotating it about the y-axis has minimum area.

Solution

Solution. A strip of the surface has area dA=2πxds=2πx1+y′2​dx, so we extremize

A=2π∫x1​x2​​x1+y′2​dx,f=x1+y′2​

f does not contain y, so $\partial f/\partial y' $ is constant:

∂y′∂f​=1+y′2​xy′​=a ⇒ x2y′2=a2(1+y′2) ⇒ y′=x2−a2​a​

Integrate:

y=acosh−1ax​+b ⟺ x=acoshay−b​​

This is a catenary, and the surface of revolution is a catenoid (the shape a soap film takes between two rings). The constants a,b are fixed by requiring the curve to pass through both end points. For some end-point separations no such catenary exists, and the minimal surface is then two discs.

Problem 3 [HW practice · MT E6.5]

A disk rolls without slipping on an inclined plane. Determine the equation of constraint in terms of the coordinates y and θ, then use it to find the motion and the friction force.

Solution

Solution. Let y be the distance the centre has moved down the plane and θ the rotation angle of the disk (radius R). Rolling without slipping means the arc length rolled equals the distance moved:

y=Rθ ⇒ g(y,θ)=y−Rθ=0​

so ∂g/∂y=1 and ∂g/∂θ=−R (these are the coefficients attached to the multiplier λ).

Using the multiplier. The Lagrangian (with I=21​MR2 and the plane at angle α) is L=21​My˙​2+21​Iθ˙2+Mg(ℓ−y)sinα. The equations with a multiplier are ∂qk​∂L​−dtd​∂q˙​k​∂L​+λ∂qk​∂g​=0 gives

y: Mgsinα−My¨​+λ=0,θ: −Iθ¨−λR=0

From the second, λ=−Iθ¨/R=−21​MRθ¨=−21​My¨​ (using y¨​=Rθ¨). Substituting into the first:

Mgsinα−My¨​−21​My¨​=0 ⇒ y¨​=32​gsinα​

and λ=−31​Mgsinα. The magnitude of the friction force that enforces rolling is ∣λ∣=31​Mgsinα, and the generalized constraint force and torque are Qy​=λ and Qθ​=−λR. Compared with frictionless sliding (gsinα), rolling gives only 2/3 of the acceleration because part of the energy goes into rotation.

Problem 4 [HW practice · MT E7.3]

Consider projectile motion under gravity in two dimensions. Find the equations of motion in both Cartesian and polar coordinates.

Solution

Solution. Cartesian (x horizontal, y vertical): T=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2), U=mgy, so L=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2)−mgy.

x: dtd​(mx˙)=0⇒mx¨=0​,y: dtd​(my˙​)=−mg⇒my¨​=−mg​

Polar (r the distance from the launch point, θ the elevation angle from horizontal): x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, so T=21​m(r˙2+r2θ˙2), U=mgrsinθ and

L=21​m(r˙2+r2θ˙2)−mgrsinθ
r: mr¨=mrθ˙2−mgsinθ ⇒ r¨−rθ˙2+gsinθ=0​
θ: dtd​(mr2θ˙)=−mgrcosθ ⇒ rθ¨+2r˙θ˙+gcosθ=0​

The Cartesian equations are decoupled and trivial; the polar ones are coupled and nonlinear. The coordinates are a matter of convenience, but the physics is identical, which is the point of Lagrange's formulation (the same recipe in any coordinates).

Problem 5 [HW practice · MT E7.7]

A bead slides along a smooth wire bent in the shape of a parabola z=cr2. The bead rotates in a circle of radius R when the wire is rotating about its vertical symmetry axis with angular velocity ω. Find the value of c.

Solution

Solution. Use r as the only generalized coordinate, with θ˙=ω fixed and z=cr2, z˙=2crr˙:

T=21​m(r˙2+r2ω2+z˙2)=21​m[r˙2(1+4c2r2)+r2ω2],U=mgcr2

Lagrange's equation for r:

dtd​[mr˙(1+4c2r2)]=m[4c2rr˙2+rω2−2gcr]

Only after deriving the equation set r=R (constant), so that r˙=r¨=0:

0=m(Rω2−2gcR) ⇒ c=2gω2​​

This is the shape of a rotating liquid surface (a parabola); a bead at any radius stays at rest relative to the wire.

Problem 6 [HW practice · MT E7.8]

Consider the double pulley system of Figure 7-6 (a fixed pulley with mass m1​ on one side and a movable pulley on the other; the movable pulley carries masses m2​ and m3​). Use the coordinates x and y shown and determine the equations of motion.

Solution

Solution. Take the pulleys massless and the strings inextensible. Let x be the depth of m1​ below the fixed pulley and y the depth of m2​ below the movable pulley, whose own depth is ℓ1​−x. The velocities are

v1​=x˙,v2​=−x˙+y˙​,v3​=−x˙−y˙​

The Lagrangian is

L=21​m1​x˙2+21​m2​(y˙​−x˙)2+21​m3​(x˙+y˙​)2+m1​gx+m2​g(ℓ1​−x+y)+m3​g(ℓ1​−x+ℓ2​−y)

Lagrange's equations:

x: (m1​+m2​+m3​)x¨+(m3​−m2​)y¨​=(m1​−m2​−m3​)g
y: (m2​+m3​)y¨​+(m3​−m2​)x¨=(m2​−m3​)g

Solving the pair:

x¨=gm1​(m2​+m3​)+4m2​m3​m1​(m2​+m3​)−4m2​m3​​,y¨​=m1​(m2​+m3​)+4m2​m3​2gm1​(m2​−m3​)​​

Checks: if m2​=m3​=m, then y¨​=0 and x¨=g(m1​−2m)/(m1​+2m), the ordinary Atwood machine with a combined mass 2m on the right. If m1​=0, x¨=−g (the movable pulley falls freely).

Problem 7 [HW practice · MT E7.10]

A particle of mass m starts at rest on top of a smooth fixed hemisphere of radius a. Find the force of constraint and determine the angle at which the particle leaves the hemisphere.

Solution

Solution. Use r and θ (measured from the vertical) with the constraint f=r−a=0 and multiplier λ (which equals the radial constraint force):

L=21​m(r˙2+r2θ˙2)−mgrcosθ
r: mrθ˙2−mgcosθ−mr¨+λ=0  (∂f/∂r=1),θ: mgrsinθ−dtd​(mr2θ˙)=0

Apply r=a, r˙=r¨=0:

θ: θ¨=ag​sinθ ⇒ θ˙2=a2g​(1−cosθ)(from rest at the top)
r: λ=mgcosθ−maθ˙2=mgcosθ−2mg(1−cosθ)
λ=mg(3cosθ−2)​

This is the normal force the surface exerts, directed outward. It decreases as the particle slides and reaches zero when

cosθ=32​ ⇒ θ=48.2∘​

after which the constraint cannot supply a negative (pulling) force, so the particle leaves the surface at that angle.

Problem 8 [HW practice · MT 7-17]

A particle of mass m can slide freely along a wire AB whose perpendicular distance from the origin O is h. The wire rotates in the vertical plane at constant angular velocity θ˙=ω. The position of the particle is described by the angle θ and the distance q from the foot C of the perpendicular. With gravity acting and θ(0)=0, q(0)=0, q˙​(0)=0, show that q(t)=2ω2g​(coshωt−cosωt). Sketch it. Compute the Hamiltonian and compare it with the total energy. Is the total energy conserved?

Solution

Solution. Take θ measured so that at θ=0 the perpendicular OC lies along x and the wire is vertical (as in the figure). With n^=(cosθ,sinθ) along OC and t^=(sinθ,−cosθ) along the wire, the position is r=hn^+qt^, and since dn^/dθ=−t^, dt^/dθ=n^:

r˙=(q˙​−hω)t^+qωn^,y=hsinθ−qcosθ
L=21​m[(q˙​−hω)2+q2ω2]−mg(hsinωt−qcosωt)

Lagrange's equation for q:

dtd​[m(q˙​−hω)]=mqω2+mgcosωt ⇒ q¨​−ω2q=gcosωt

Solve. Homogeneous solutions are coshωt and sinhωt. A particular solution is qp​=Acosωt: −ω2A−ω2A=g, so A=−g/2ω2. With q(0)=0 and q˙​(0)=0 the sinh term vanishes and

q(t)=2ω2g​(coshωt−cosωt)​

Sketch: q=0 at t=0 with zero slope. For small t, q≈2ω2g​⋅ω2t2=21​gt2, the free-fall value. For ωt≫1 the cosh term takes over and q≈4ω2g​eωt, with a small oscillating ripple −2ω2g​cosωt on top. The bead is flung outward along the wire.

Hamiltonian. p=∂L/∂q˙​=m(q˙​−hω) and

H=pq˙​−L=21​mq˙​2−21​mω2(h2+q2)+mg(hsinωt−qcosωt)

Total energy E=T+U=21​mq˙​2−mhωq˙​+21​mω2(h2+q2)+mg(hsinωt−qcosωt).

H=E: the kinetic energy is not a homogeneous quadratic function of q˙​ (the constraint depends on time, so T contains terms linear in q˙​ and independent of q˙​), and H=T2​−T0​+U while E=T2​+T1​+T0​+U.

Conservation. L depends explicitly on time through θ=ωt, so dH/dt=−∂L/∂t=mgω(hcosωt+qsinωt)=0: H is not conserved. E is not conserved either, because the rotating wire does work on the bead through the constraint force (it is the motor driving the wire that supplies the energy).
